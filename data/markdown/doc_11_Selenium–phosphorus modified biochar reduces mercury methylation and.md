Biochar is a frequently employed for solidifying and stabilizing mercury (Hg) contamination in soil. However, it often results in an elevated presence of soil methylmercury (MeHg), which introduces new environmental risks. Consequently, there is a necessity for developing a safer modified biochar for use in Hg-contaminated soil. This study employed sodium selenite (at a safe dosage for soil) and hydroxyapatite to modify straw biochar (BC) based on the interaction between selenium (Se) and phosphorus (P). This process led to the formation of Se-modified biochar (Se-BC), P-modified biochar (P-BC), and Se and P co-modified biochar (Se-P-BC). Additionally, solvent adsorption  experiments  and  pot  experiments  (BC/soil  mass  ratio:  0.5  %)  were  conducted  to  investigate  the impacts of these soil amendments on soil Hg methylation and bioavailability. Se and P co-modification substantially increased the surface area, pore volume, and Hg adsorption capacity of BC. BC treatment increased the simulated gastric acid-soluble Hg, organo-chelated Hg, and MeHg in the soil. Conversely, Se-P-BC significantly reduced these forms of Hg in the soil, indicating that Se-P-BC can transform soil Hg into less bioavailable states. Among the different biochar treatments, Se-P-BC exhibited the most pronounced reductions in soil MeHg, total Hg, and MeHg in water spinach, achieving reductions of 63 %, 71 %, and 70 %, respectively. The co-modification of Se and P displayed a synergistic reduction effect in managing soil Hg pollution, which is associated with the increase  of  available  Se  in  the  soil  due  to  phosphorus  addition.  The  significantly  reduced  dissolved  organic carbon  and  the  abnormally  high  SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentration  in  the  soil  of  Se-P-BC  treatment  also  inhibited  Hg methylation and bioavailability in the soil. In summary, Se-P-BC substantially increased reduction percentage in plant Hg content while mitigating the risk of secondary pollution arising from elevated soil MeHg.

## 1. Introduction

Mercury (Hg) is a global pollutant that exists in various forms in the environment (Chen et al., 2018). Among these forms, methylmercury (MeHg) is particularly toxic due to its bioaccumulative nature and significant biomagnification effects in the food chain, drawing widespread attention (Henriques et al., 2013; Ottesen et al., 2013). Agricultural soil Hg pollution is severe, especially in Hg mining areas, where mining and smelting  activities  release  substantial  amounts  of  Hg-containing  substances into surface water, agricultural soil, and the atmosphere. For example, the research has reported that the Hg concentrations in the surveyed area of the Wanshan Hg mining area in Guizhou, China, ranged from 6.5 to 790 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 in the soil, leading to potential ecological risks

Common agents employed in soil solidification/stabilization encompass organic remediation agents like biochar, organic fertilizers, peat, as well as inorganic remediation agents such as clay minerals, phosphorus-containing compounds, and selenium-containing compounds. Biochar, recognized for its expansive surface  area,  high  cation  exchange  capacity,  and  plentiful  surface functional  groups,  exhibits  remarkable  adsorption  capabilities.  It  is readily  available,  environmentally friendly,  and frequently  employed for heavy metal remediation in soil (Gholizadeh and Hu, 2021; Jha et al., 2023).  Biochar  can  be  produced  from  a  variety  of  raw  materials, including  agricultural  waste  like  chicken  manure,  pig  manure,  wood chips, straw, as well as industrial organic waste and urban sludge (Chen et al., 2023a; Chen et al., 2023b; Suliman et al., 2016). Straw biochar, due to its widespread availability and significant production volume, has garnered significant attention. Utilizing rice straw to produce biochar  offers  an  efficient  means  of  returning  straw  to  the  field  and reducing straw burning. Biochar derived from crop straw finds widespread application as a soil conditioner. For instance, Ma et al. (2022) reported that the addition of corn straw biochar effectively reduces the weak acid-extractable Cd and Pb content in soil while increasing the residual  content,  thus  mitigating  the  toxicity  of  heavy  metals.  There have also been numerous studies conducted on the use of straw biochar for remediating soil Hg pollution. Most of these studies indicate that biochar effectively reduces Hg levels in crops (Hu et al., 2021; Man et al., 2021; Shu et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2021). However, some studies have shown that the addition of biochar to soil did not achieve the expected results. For instance, one study discovered that adding a low dose of wheat straw biochar to the soil only resulted in a 5 % reduction in rice THg, while a high dose of biochar actually promoted the enrichment of THg in rice (Du, 2021). The research of Wang et al. (2021) found that adding different proportions of rice straw biochar (1 % or 4 %) could reduce MeHg concentrations in rice grains by 49 % -92 % but increased MeHg concentrations in wheat grains by 48 % -84 %. More importantly, numerous studies have shown that adding biochar to the soil can increase the risk of soil MeHg exposure (Hu et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2019). For example, it has been shown that the addition of 4 % straw biochar to soil significantly increased the MeHg content in paddy soil (Shu et al., 2016). The research of Wang et al. (2021) also found that, during the rice-wheat rotation, biochar addition led to higher MeHg concentrations in the soil, with increases of 60 % -116 % during the rice season and 52 % -292 % during the wheat season. Consequently, there is a need to explore more stable, efficient, and safer modified biochar materials for application in Hg-contaminated soil.  However,  there  are  currently  few  modified  biochars  applied  to Hg-contaminated soil (Huang et al., 2022; Lv et al., 2021; O ' Connor et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2020), particularly in terms of materials aimed at reducing soil MeHg.

Biochar modification can be categorized into physical and chemical modification (Chen et al., 2024; Sizmur et al., 2017), with the latter being  the  more  widely  adopted  method.  Chemical  modification  primarily  involves  acid  modification,  alkali  modification,  impregnation loading modification, and so on (Devi et al., 2020; Sricharoenchaikul et  al.,  2008).  Studies  have  demonstrated  that  modified  biochar  can enhance its physical and chemical properties, such as porosity, specific surface  area,  surface  oxygen  functional  groups,  and  adsorption  sites. This  enhancement  significantly  increases  its  capacity  to  adsorb  and immobilize heavy metals (Rajapaksha et al., 2016).

Phosphorus (P)-based materials are extensively employed in the field of heavy metal pollution control because the PO4 3 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; in P-based materials plays a crucial role in immobilizing heavy metals (Knox et al., 2008). Among these materials, hydroxyapatite (HAP), with the molecular formula Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2, is considered a cost-effective and environmentally friendly adsorbent. This is due to its large specific surface area and the ease with which divalent heavy metal ions can replace Ca 2 + in its structure, a well-recognized fact among researchers (Nzihou and Sharrock,  2010).  Presently,  HAP  is  primarily  used  for  removing  and adsorbing cadmium, lead, and other heavy metals, with limited applications in the realm of soil Hg pollution control.

Selenium (Se), as an essential trace element, can antagonize heavy metals like Hg, Cr, and Cd (Wang et al., 2016). For instance, field investigations in the Wanshan Hg mining area in Guizhou, China, have shown that the Hg content in rice decreases as the Se content in soil increases (Zhang et al., 2012). Research has also indicated that adding Se to soil can reduce the MeHg content in rice (Wang et al., 2014).

Both Se and P can be absorbed by plants as nutrients. Although Se and P are not elements of the same group, the radius and chemical form of  phosphate  and  selenite  ions  are  similar,  and  their  migration,  absorption, and transport processes in the soil-plant system will interact with  each  other.  For  example,  in  the  soils,  Se  and  P  can  exhibit competitive adsorption. Ryden et al. (1987) found that the affinity of soil colloids such as oxides to P is higher than that to Se. The research of Huang et al. (2024) also observed that, when Se and P co-exist, the binding ability of soil to P is stronger than Se, and the adsorption site of Se will be reduced.

Building  on  the  aforementioned  arguments,  this  study  used  rice straw as the raw material for biochar, used sodium selenite (Na2SeO3) and HAP as modification materials, and employed a simple and fast impregnation loading method to prepare selenium and phosphorus comodified  biochar  (Se-P-BC).  Then,  a  pot  experiment  was  conducted using water spinach to investigate the effects and mechanisms of Se-PBC on soil MeHg, as well as its effects on reducing THg and MeHg in water spinach. The objective was to develop a more efficient and environmentally friendly soil Hg pollution control technology that not only effectively reduces crop Hg but also mitigates the risk of increased soil MeHg due to biochar addition.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Preparation and modification of biochar

The preparation of straw biochar (BC) was referred to the research of Chen et al. (2023a). Dry rice straw was ground to a particle size of less than 20 meshes. It was then placed in a sealed stainless steel box and subjected to pyrolysis in a muffle furnace. The pyrolysis temperature was set at 500 ◦ C, with a heating rate of 10 ◦ C ⋅ min GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , and maintained at the target temperature for 4 h. After pyrolysis, the BC was allowed to cool, ground to pass through a 100-mesh sieve, and stored in sealed plastic bags for later use.

The preparations of selenium-modified biochar (Se-BC), phosphorusmodified  biochar  (P-BC)  and  selenium  and  phosphorus  co-modified biochar  (Se-P-BC)  were  referred  to  the  researches  of  Huang  et  al. (2022) and Ahmed et al. (2022).

Preparation of Se-BC: A specific amount  of sodium  selenite (Na2SeO3) was dissolved in deionized water, followed by the addition of a  predetermined  amount  of  biochar  (maintaining  a  mass  ratio  of Na2SeO3/BC at 1:1477). The ratio of Na2SeO3  to BC in Se-BC is calculated based on the pre-set amount of Se added to the soil (1.5 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , a safe dosage for soil) and the amount of Se-BC added to the soil (0.5 %) in the pot experiment. The mixture was stirred for 6 h, and after settling, excess moisture was drained. Subsequently, it was dried in an oven at 105 ◦ C until a constant weight was achieved, resulting in Se-BC.

Preparation  of  P-BC:  A  predetermined  amount  of  hydroxyapatite (HAP) was placed in deionized water and stirred to ensure complete dispersion. Then, a specific amount of biochar (maintaining a mass ratio of HAP/BC at 1:5) was added. The ratio of HAP to BC in P-BC was set in reference to the research of Zhou et al. (2022). After stirring, draining, and following the same drying procedure as above, P-BC was obtained.

Preparation of Se-P-BC: A certain amount of Na2SeO3 was dissolved in a beaker, followed by the addition of a predetermined amount of HAP,

After thorough stirring, draining, and drying (following the same procedure as above), Se-P-BC was obtained.

## 2.2. Adsorption isotherm experiment

Adsorption  isotherm  of  Hg(II)  onto  the  biochar  samples  were determined  by  adding  0.05  g  adsorbent  to  a  50  mL  centrifuge  tube containing 20 mL of Hg(II) solution where the concentration is 10, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 200 mg L GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , respectively. The tubes were shaken at 180 rpm in the mechanical shaker for 12 h at room temperature. After the sorption period, samples were filtered through 0.45 μ m PTFE filters (Jin Teng Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd, Tianjin, China), and then analyzed for aqueous Hg(II) concentrations. All treatments were conducted in triplicate.

## 2.3. Pot experiment

The pot experiment took place in the greenhouse atop the School of Resources and Environmental Engineering at Guizhou University. The soil used for the experiment was collected from the surface layer (0 -20 cm) of a Hg-contaminated paddy field in the Wanshan mining area of Guizhou Province, China (N27 ◦ 33 ′ 51 ″ , E109 ◦ 11 ′ 35 ″ ). The soil contained concentrations of Hg, Se, and P at 3.8 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , 1.25 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , and 746 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , respectively. After removing roots and gravel from the soil, it was air-dried, crushed, and sieved through a 6.0 mm mesh for subsequent use. The study included five treatment groups (Table 1), with three replicates for each group, and a control group (CK) with no biochar addition.  In  relevant  research  on  soil  mercury  pollution  control,  the effective dosage of different biochar added varies greatly, approximately between 0.3 % and 7 % (biochar/soil) (Wang et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2021). Considering the goals of soil remediation and economic feasibility, we chose a relatively low dose for the experiment. A total of 1.5 kg of soil was placed in 3 L plastic pots, and different soil amendments were added at a mass ratio of 0.5 % (biochar/soil). Following this, a small amount of tap water was added to the plastic basin to moisten the soil, and then the soil and amendment were fully mixed and incubated for 4 days. The final Se concentration in the Se-BC and Se-P-BC-treated soils was 2.58 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , which did not exceed the maximum allowable value (3 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ) for soil Se content specified in the ' Geochemical Evaluation Specification  for  Land  Quality ' (DZ/T  0295 -2016).  Water  spinach ( Ipomoea aquatica Forsk ) is one of the common crops in the Wanshan mining area of Guizhou Province, and it has fewer pests and diseases, making it easier to plant and manage. Therefore, in this study, water spinach was selected as a potted plant. Water spinach seedlings (about 8 cm tall),  purchased  from  a  mercury-free  farmland  in  Guizhou,  were planted in the soil (two per pot). During the whole growing period (60 days),  tap  water  is  irregularly  poured  into  the  soil  to  keep  the  soil moisture at about 60 %, and nitrogen fertilizer was applied once (0.4 g ⋅ pot GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ) during the mid-term. The pot experiment was conducted in an indoor greenhouse, where the temperature was controlled within the range 20 -30 ◦ C.

## 2.4. Chemical analysis

The available Hg concentrations in the soils were determined with the method of Bloom et al. (2003), which involved sequential selective extractions of Hg from geological solids. Briefly, the water-soluble Hg was extracted with deionized water; the simulated gastric acid-soluble Hg was extracted with 0.1 M CH3COOH + 0.01 M HCl; organo-chelated Hg was extracted  with  1  M  KOH.  All  Hg  in  extracting  solution  was oxidized to divalent Hg, then reduced to Hg 0 , trapped in gold tubes, and analyzed  by  cold  vapor  atomic  fluorescence  spectrometry  (CVAFS, Brooks Rand Co, USA).

Aqueous Hg(II) concentrations in the adsorption experiments were determined  by  cold  vapor  atomic  absorption  spectrometry  (F732 -V, Huaguang Machinery and Instrument Company, Shanghai, China). The THg concentrations in the plants were determined with a DMA-80 solid sample Hg meter (DMA-80, Milestone, Italy). The MeHg in the plants was analyzed via KOH digestion, CH2Cl2 solvent extraction, water backextraction  by  heating  and  the  ethylation-GC-CVAFS  technique  (Feng et  al.,  2008;  Liang  et  al.,  1996).  The  MeHg  in  soil  was  analyzed  by CuSO4 -HNO3 -CH2Cl2 solvent  extraction,  water  back-extraction  by heating and ethylation-GC-CVAFS techniques (He et al., 2004).

The available Se content of the soil was extracted by the NaH2PO4 (Kubachka  et  al.,  2017),  and  analyzed  using  an  atomic  fluorescence spectrophotometer  (AFS-9700,  HaiGuang  Instruments  Co,  Beijing, China).  The  DOC  in  the  soil  was  determined  by  an  automatic  TOC analyzer (Vario TOC, Elementar, Germany). The SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentrations in the soil were measured by Dionex ICS-1100 ion chromatography. The pH in the soil was measured using a pH meter (SX731, Sanxin, Shanghai, China).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed using a  Thermo  Scientific  Nicolet  iS10  FTIR  spectrometer  (Waltham,  MA, USA) with a wavenumber range of 400 -4000 cm GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 and a resolution of 0.4 cm GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 . The pore structures of biochar were studied by the N2 sorption method using a surface area analyzer (ASAP 2460, Micromeritics, USA) at 77 K.

## 2.5. Data processing

SPSS 23.0 was used for statistical analyses and Origin 2021 software was used for plotting. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test significant differences between the treatment groups. Significant differences  between  the  treatment  groups  are  denoted  by  different lowercase characters in the figures and tables.

## 2.6. Quality assurance and quality control

In order to reduce blanks in sample collection and Hg analysis processes, the glass instruments used in the experiment were soaked in 10 % nitric acid for more than 24 h, washed with deionized water, and burned in a muffle furnace for 1 h at 470 ◦ C. Quality assurance and quality control for the THg and MeHg analyses were conducted using duplicates,  matrix spikes and/or standard reference materials (SRMs). For

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Infrared spectra and pore structure of BC and Se-P-BC

Fig. 1 displays the fourier transform infrared spectra of BC and Se-PBC. From the figure, it is apparent that the spectra of BC and Se-P-BC share similarities, with several functional groups in common. However, Se-P-BC exhibits additional stretching vibration peaks. BC demonstrates a bending vibration peak associated with the C --O functional group at 1420 cm GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 . Following Se and HAP co-modification, Se-P-BC reveals an enhanced bending vibration peak at 1420 cm GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , signifying increased aromaticity in Se-P-BC. This suggests an increased number of Π -electron donors during the heavy metal adsorption process (Harvey et al., 2011). In comparison to the BC spectrum, Se-P-BC displays a new bending  vibration  peak  corresponding  to  O -P -O  at  561  cm GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 and characteristic  peaks  of  -Se  groups  around  605  cm GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 .  These  findings indicate the successful loading of Se and HAP onto the biochar (Dong et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2018).

The pore size distribution of Se-P-BC and BC are provided in Fig. S1. The surface area and pore volume of Se-P-BC were 76.02 m 2 g GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 and 17.466 cm 3  g GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , respectively, nearly twice those of BC (38.03 m 2  g GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , 8.74 cm 3  g GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ).

## 3.2. Adsorption isotherm analysis of biochar for aqueous Hg

The isothermal adsorption experiment plays an important role in studying the migration or retention of Hg(II) at the solid-phase interface of biochar. Fig. 2 depicts the adsorption isotherms of BC and Se-P-BC for Hg(II),  with  the  experimental  data  fitted  using  the  Langmuir  and Freundlich  equations,  respectively.  The  equations  and  corresponding fitting  parameters  used  for  the  fitting  are  provided  in  Table  S1.  As observed in Fig. 2, the equilibrium adsorption capacities of both BC and Se-P-BC increased with higher initial concentrations of Hg(II), reaching equilibrium at a concentration of 60 mg L GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 .  At identical initial concentrations,  Se-P-BC  exhibited  an  equilibrium  adsorption  capacity almost  double  that  of  BC,  indicating  a  significantly  enhanced  Hg(II) removal capacity following Se and P co-modification.

## 3.3. Soil pH, DOC, SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; , and available Se concentrations

3.5. Concentrations of THg and MeHg in water spinach stems

Fig.  6  illustrates  the  concentrations  of  THg  and  MeHg  in  water spinach stems across various treatment groups. In the control, the concentrations of THg and MeHg in water spinach stems were 40.11 ± 4.64 ng g GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 and 15.20 ± 2.46 ng g GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , respectively. ## 4. Discussion

## 4.1. Why does Se-P-BC significantly reduce soil Hg methylation?

In comparison to the control, the application of BC led to a slight increase in soil MeHg concentration. This aligns with findings from some studies indicating that the introduction of biochar can heighten the risk of soil MeHg exposure (Hu et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2019). Many studies have suggested that biochar amendments can induce alterations in soil organic matter, humic acid, DOC content, and sulfate concentration, consequently influencing the transformation and movement of soil Hg (Tian et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2022). Our study also revealed that the addition of BC increased soil DOC concentration. DOC can stimulate the activity of microorganisms involved  in  Hg  methylation  by  providing  an  organic  carbon  source, thereby  promoting  Hg  methylation  (Hall  et  al.,  2008).  Additionally, some studies have indicated that the introduction of biochar into soil can elevate sulfate concentration and boost the abundance of sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) (Tian et al., 2023), which can promote Hg methylation (Beckers and Rinklebe, 2017; Hu et al., 2021). However, in our study, the addition of BC had a negligible effect on sulfate concentration, possibly due to variations in the source material of the biochar. In contrast to the BC treatment, the Se-BC treatment resulted in a slight decrease in soil MeHg concentration. Se and Hg have antagonistic effects (Tran et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2016). Numerous studies have demonstrated  that  the  incorporation  of  Se  into  soil  can  significantly reduce soil MeHg (Liu et al., 2022). This is due to the fact that the added Se can combine with Hg in the soil to form insoluble HeSe (Dang et al., 2019; Tran et al., 2021), thus reducing the bioavailability and methylation of mercury in the soil. However, in our study, the Se-BC treatment did not yield a significant reduction in soil MeHg, potentially attributable to the relatively low Se dosage applied. The amount of Na2SeO3 used for modification in our study was lower compared to other research (Lv et al., 2021), and the concentration of Se added to the soil was only 1.5 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 .

Compared to the control, BC and Se-BC treatments, both P-BC and SeP-BC treatments led to a significant reduction in soil MeHg, with the SeP-BC treatment achieving the most pronounced reduction effect at 63 %. This outcome can be elucidated through the following mechanisms:

- 1  Se-P-BC demonstrated enhanced adsorption and fixation capabilities for Hg, reducing the availability of Hg for methylation. Adsorption
- 2  Se-P-BC converts a higher proportion of soil Hg into less bioavailable forms than organo-chelated Hg, thereby enhancing the stability of Hg fixation in the soil. As mentioned in 3.4, BC treatment significantly increased organo-chelated Hg, whereas other modified biochar treatments significantly reduced organo-chelated Hg, particularly the Se-P-BC treatment. This implies that most of the Hg in Se-P-BC is not merely adsorbed onto the biochar but rather combines with Se and P on Se-P-BC and enters less accessible residual forms within the soil. Our study employed an extraction method for Hg forms based on the research conducted by Bloom et al. (2003), which is better suited for the separation of Hg forms. Organo-chelated Hg primarily refers to the form bound to organic matter, such as humic substances. While this form is less bioavailable compared to water-soluble and simulated gastric acid-soluble Hg, it has  been  found  to  exhibit  the  strongest  correlation  with  Hg methylation potential (Bloom et al., 2003), suggesting that its stability is relatively weak. BC significantly increased organo-chelated Hg, which elevated the potential for Hg methylation and rendered the effectiveness of BC in immobilizing Hg in specific soil environments unstable.
- 3  Se and P within Se-P-BC exhibited a synergistic effect in reducing soil MeHg.  The  Se-P-BC  treatment  demonstrated  a  more  pronounced reduction in soil MeHg compared to the combined effects of individual  modifications  (Se-BC  and  P-BC),  indicating  that  Se  and  P within Se-P-BC synergistically contributed to the reduction of soil MeHg. As mentioned in 3.3, compared the treatments without Se addition, Se-BC addition only slightly increased the available Se in the soil, while Se-P-BC significantly increased available Se in the soil ( p &lt; 0.05), indicating that the simultaneous addition of Se and P increased the activity of soil selenium. Due to the significant increase in soil available selenium in Se-P-BC treatment, the antagonistic effect of Se and Hg is enhanced, thereby reducing soil Hg methylation. Some studies have also observed that P addition can increase the activity of soil Se (Jiang et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2017). When Se and P co-exist, the binding ability of soil to P is stronger  than  Se,  and  the  adsorption  site  of  Se  will  be  reduced (Huang et al., 2024). However, Huang et al. (2024) also found this phenomenon is not always observed. In certain ranges of P to Se ratios,  P  addition  did  not  increase  soil  Se  activity  (Huang  et  al., 2024). In P-BC treatment, the ratio of P to Se in the soil may be within this range, and therefore no increase in available Se in the soil was observed. No observed increase in available selenium may also be related to the lower pH in the P-BC treatment. Some studies have found that lower pH is beneficial for the adsorption of selenium in soil (Li et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2015).
- 4  Se-P-BC  significantly  decreased  soil  DOC  while  simultaneously increasing SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentration. As mentioned in 3.3, the DOC content  increased  by  10  %  in  the  BC  treatment,  while  all  modified

## 5. Conclusion

In comparison to the BC treatment, Se and P co-modification substantially increased the surface area, pore volume, and Hg adsorption capacity  of  BC.  Se-P-BC  can  transform  soil  Hg  into  less  bioavailable states,  thereby  enhancing  the  stability  of  Hg  fixation  in  the  soil. Consequently, this improves the stability of soil Hg pollution control effectiveness.  Unmodified  biochar  slightly  increased  soil  MeHg  concentration and exhibited a lower reduction effect on plant Hg, while SeP-BC demonstrated the significant reduction effect on soil MeHg, water spinach THg, and MeHg. The co-modification of Se and P displayed a synergistic  reduction  effect  in  managing  soil  Hg  pollution,  which  is associated with the increase of available Se in the soil due to phosphorus addition. Furthermore, the substantial reduction in soil DOC and the abnormal elevation of SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentration in the Se-P-BC treatment may have further inhibited the methylation of soil Hg. To summarize, Se-PBC  remediation  material  offers  the  advantages  of  a  straightforward modification process, minimal addition levels, and effective remediation.  Furthermore, it mitigates the risks of increasing soil MeHg and excessive soil Se, making it a potentially suitable choice for widespread application.

## 4.2. The impact of Se-P-BC on Hg accumulation in plants

The results of the pot experiment reveal that the incorporation of various biochars led to a reduction in both THg and MeHg content in water spinach stems, with Se-P-BC demonstrating the most pronounced reduction effect. Despite a slight increase in soil MeHg in the BC treatment, it resulted in a slight decrease in THg (21 %) and a significant decrease in MeHg (45 %) in water spinach. This suggests that biochar possesses  a  robust  capacity  for  adsorbing  and  immobilizing  MeHg, consequently reducing its mobility or bioavailability (Bailon et al., 2022; Bundschuh et al., 2015; Bussan et al., 2016; Gomez-Eyles et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2017; Shu et al., 2016). Previous studies have reported that MeHg binds to organic sulfur groups in biochar or soil, thus decreasing the mobility of MeHg in paddy soil and lowering the accumulation coefficient of MeHg in rice (Man et al., 2021; Shu et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020; Xing et al., 2020).

In  comparison  to  the  BC  treatment,  the  Se-BC  treatment  did  not exhibit significant differences in THg and MeHg concentrations in water spinach, likely due to the low Se content used in this study. Among all the treatments, the Se-P-BC treatment displayed the most substantial reduction in THg and MeHg in water spinach, surpassing the combined effects of Se-BC and P-BC. This indicates a synergistic reduction effect. This observation aligns with the distribution pattern of soil MeHg discussed in Section 4.1. As discussed earlier, Se-P-BC exhibits a stronger adsorption and immobilization effect on Hg compared to BC, thereby reducing the bioavailability of Hg. Furthermore, in the case of Se-P-BC treatment,  its  notably  low  soil  MeHg  content  may  lead  to  relatively lower  MeHg  concentrations  in  plants.  Additionally,  as  previously mentioned, P addition facilitates the release of available Se, intensifying the antagonistic effect between Se and Hg during the crop element absorption process, further reducing THg and MeHg in plants.
