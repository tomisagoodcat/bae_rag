Keywords: Mercury contaminated soil Soil erosion Biochar amendment Mercury contamination Runoff and infiltration

Mercury  (Hg)  contaminated  soil  is  a  potential  hazardous  material  especially  under  soil  erosion  and  surface runoff. This work aims to use rice husk biochar to immobilize Hg and prevent erosion, and find the optimal production temperature and particle size of the biochar. The biochars were produced at 300, 500, and 700 ◦ C and sieved to three particle sizes ~20, &lt; 2, and &lt; 0.15 mm. They were applied to a Hg contaminated loamy sand (20.2 mg/kg) and undergone simulated rainfall erosion representing 7 years of heavy rain events in Beijing. All biochar amendments reduced the runoff volume by 5.1 -15.4%. Hg amount in runoff were significantly reduced by 36.7 -48.8% after the amendments of biochar. The Hg concentration of infiltration was reduced by biochar treatments except that produced at 300 ◦ C, while its amount was increased due to larger infiltration volume. All biochar amendments significantly reduced soil loss in runoff by 43.5 -77.2%. Hg was enriched in the sediments (39.7 -46.8  mg/kg)  compared  with  the  parent  soil  (20.2  mg/kg)  regardless  of  biochar  treatment,  but  its bioavailability was low. Higher pyrolysis temperature of the rice husk biochars resulted in less runoff, more infiltration, and better erosion prevention, while the effect of biochar particle size is less significant.

## 1. Introduction

Mercury (Hg) is an extreme toxic element that can destroy the nervous system of animals and humans (Zhao et al., 2020a). It is one of the WHO ' s  'ten  leading  chemicals  of  concern ' ,  and  anthropogenic  Hg pollution places over 8 million people at risk around the world (Beckers and Rinklebe, 2017). A large number of anthropogenic Hg (e.g., 1960 tonnes), resulting from artisanal and small-scale gold mining, fossil fuel combustion, power plants etc., is emitted to the atmosphere annually (Sundseth et al., 2017). The atmospheric deposition of these Hg, especially surrounding hotspots (e.g., coal-fire power plants), can cause their accumulation in soil and, consequently the pollution of soil when its concentration  exceeds  the  environmental  regulatory  limit  (Li  et  al., 2017). In addition, the contact with Hg mining wastes and agricultural irrigation  using  Hg  contaminated  water  can  also  result  in  the  Hg contamination of soil (Xing et al., 2020).

Soil  contamination  with  Hg  has  a  significant  impact  on  human health: Hg may accumulate in crops and end in food chain; Hg may also leach  to  groundwater through  rainwater infiltration  and  damage the source of drinking water as well as the ecosystem (Beckers et al., 2019a). More importantly, Hg can migrate via surface runoff both in soluble form and carried by sediments (Zhang and Wang, 2020). Due to this erosion  effect,  Hg  can  transport  (e.g.,  to  a  river)  and  affect  a  large number of areas that were not contaminated and bring risks to a variety of receptors (Zhang and Wang, 2020; Wang et al., 2017). It was observed that heavy metal concentrations (Cr, Cu, Pb, and Ni) in sediment carried by surface runoff were 3.98 times higher than in the parent soil (Quinton and  Catt,  2007).  Soil  erosion  extensively  exist  in  nature  and  is  a worldwide environmental problem (Borrelli et al., 2017). It was estimated that 73.5 Pg soil was eroded in agricultural land alone globally (Borrelli et al., 2017). Therefore, the erosion of Hg contaminated agricultural land pose a significant environmental threat.

The remediation of Hg contaminated soil requires cost-effective, lowenergy  consuming,  and  long-term  effective  technologies.  Although phytoremediation and thermal treatment (Marrugo-Negrete et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2018) have been used for Hg remediation, they did not gain public interests for large-scale application. The reason mainly relates to their  inherent  drawbacks  such  as  long  time  consuming  for  phytoremediation  and  high  energy  consuming  for  thermal  treatment  (Xing et al., 2020). Recently, soil amendment has attracted extensive attention for the remediation of Hg contaminated soil (Zhao et al., 2020a; Beckers et al., 2019a; Wang et al., 2020). Soil amendment mixes additives with soil to immobilize contaminants and consequently reduce their migration risks to the environment (Wang et al., 2021c; Hou et al., 2021). It generally takes advantages in cost-effectiveness, fast implementation, easy-to-application etc. Soil amendment is particularly applicable for Hg contaminated agricultural land or green land as it does not destroy soil function. Conversely, a range of green and sustainable additives (e.g., biochar) can improve soil fertility, structure, and resilience (Tian et al., 2020; Hou et al., 2020a; Hou, 2021).

But very limited studies were conducted for Hg, and therefore its influence is not clear.  There  are  also  very  limited  studies  investigating  the  effects  of production temperature and particle size of biochar on soil erosion. It is hypothesized  in  this  study  that  the  changing  properties  of  biochar resulting from different production temperature and particle size may affect its performance  in  Hg  immobilization  and  soil aggregate stabilization.

The cost and efficiency of soil amendment highly depend on additives. Biochar is regarded as among the most promising additives, due to its  high  adsorption  capacity,  low-cost,  and  potential  long-term  effectiveness (Shen et al., 2016, 2017; El-Naggar et al., 2020). Several studies have used biochar to immobilize Hg in soil. Zhao et al. (2020) applied rick husk biochar and sulfur modified rice hush biochar (5% w/w) to a Hg polluted agricultural soil (28.3 mg/kg) (Zhao et al., 2020a). They observed that the Hg concentration in 0.1 M HCl extraction solution was reduced by 57.4% and 70.2% by the two amendments, respectively. They also applied laboratory accelerated ageing tests simulating natural time interval to assess the long-term performance, and observed that the immobilization can remain effective for at least 50 and 75 simulated years  for  the  two  amendments,  respectively.  Xing  et  al.  (2020)  conducted a field investigation applying rice hull-derided biochar (24 t ha -1 and 72 t ha -1 ) to a Hg contaminated agricultural soil (39.8 mg/kg), and observed that both Hg accumulation in polished rice and Hg concentration in soil pore water were significantly reduced. On the contrary, Beckers  et  al.  (2019a)  added  pine  cone  biochar  (80  t  ha -1 )  to  a  Hg contaminated floodplain soil (31.2 mg/kg), but did not observe significant impact of biochar on Hg immobilization. These conflicted findings suggest that whether biochar can effectively immobilize Hg in soil remains unclear, and needs further investigation.

In addition to the chemical immobilization of Hg, there is a research gap  on  the  question  on  how  biochar  will  affect  the  erosion  of  Hg contaminated soil and how this will alter Hg migration. Several studies have investigated the influence of biochar on soil erosion. Peng et al. (2019)  found  that  wheat  straw  biochar  amendment  can  effectively reduce  the  interrill  erosion  potential  of  sloping  cropland  by  up  to 21.34% due to improved soil aggregate stability and organic carbon content when the slope is ≤ 15 ◦ . Likewise, Agbede and Adekiya (2020) observed that hardwood biochar applied at 10, 20 and 30 t ha GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 reduced soil  loss  by  32.3%,  56.9%,  and  69.7%  in  a  humid  tropic  sandy  soil. However, Zhang et al. (2018) conducted a rainfall simulation experiment and observed that oak and hickory hardwood biochar (2 -8% w/w) significantly increased total soil loss by 20.4 -50.8%. Therefore, whether biochar plays a positive or negative role on soil erosion remains unclear under  a  heavy  rain/leaching  condition  after  the  soil  has  been  compacted. To the best of the authors ' knowledge, no previous studies have investigated  the  effect  of  biochar  amendment  on  the  erosion  of  Hg contaminated soil and associated Hg migration.

Pyrolysis temperature and particle size are two of the most crucial factors  affecting  biochar  properties  (Shen  et  al.,  2015,  2019a;  Zhao et al., 2018; Pariyar et al., 2020). Previous studies found optimal pyrolysis temperature and particle size for biochars to adsorb Pb, Cu, Zn, or Ni (Shen et al., 2017, 2015, 2019a). For instance, it was observed that increasing pyrolysis temperature between 300 ◦ C and 700 ◦ C resulted in higher adsorption capacity of wheat rice biochar and rice husk biochar for Pb (Shen et al., 2019a; Shi et al., 2019). Based on these evidence, this work applied risk husk biochars, produced  at  three  different  temperatures  and  sieved  to  three  different particle sizes, to a Hg contaminated soil and conducted rainfall tests simulating 7 years of heavy rainfall wash. Rice husk is an abundant agricultural waste in China ( &gt; 900 Tera grams annually) and rice husk biochar was suggested to be an effective sorbent for Hg (Xing et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2019; O ' Connor et al., 2018). Both runoff and infiltration were collected and tested to reveal 1) whether biochar can chemically  immobilize  Hg  in  the  soil  and  reduce  its  leachability,  2)  how biochar will affect the erosion of Hg contaminated soil and if so, whether the sediments (the solid part carried by runoff) have risks to the environment, 3) what are the optimized production temperature between 300 ◦ C and 700 ◦ C and particle size (raw pellet size, &lt; 2 mm, and &lt; 0.15 mm) of biochar for Hg immobilization and erosion prevention.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Soil and biochar

Soils were collected from the campus of Tsinghua University, Beijing, China. Grass and trees randomly distributed on the sampled area. Top soils (0 -30 cm) were dug out and homogenized. After air drying for 7 days and oven drying at 40 ◦ C for 48 h, the soils were smashed, ground, and sieved to &lt; 2 mm, during which the grass roots, small gravels, and other non-soil components were removed. The basic properties of the soil were tested. Briefly, pH was determined at a solid to liquid ratio of 1:2.5 based on British Standard BS1377. Cation exchange capacity was tested using hexamminecobalt trichloride exchange method based on China ' s standard HJ 889-2017. Dissolved organic carbon (DOC) content was  determined  based  on  borate  buffer  (pH = 7)  extraction  method (Gonet  and  Debska,  2006).  Total  organic  carbon  (TOC)  content  was determined based on Chinese Standard GB 9834-88. Particle size distribution was measured by a laser particle size analyzer (Mastor2000, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) in wet mode. The soil texture is loamy sand based on USDA classification (Table 1).

The soils were then spiked with Hg(NO3)2  solutions to obtain artificial  contaminated  soil  with  Hg  concentrations  of  20  mg/kg,  representing  typical  agricultural  soil  contaminated  by  Hg  in  mining  areas (Wang  et  al.,  2016).  The  Hg  contaminated  soil  was  incubated  at  a moisture content of 20% w/w for 7 days for homogenization before biochar amendment.

The feedstock of biochars is rice husks from Taizhou, Jiangsu, China. In  order  to  investigate  the  influence  of  pyrolysis  temperature  on  Hg immobilization and erosion prevention, the pellets of the rice husks were heated in a slow pyrolyzer (Bian et al., 2016) to a designated temperature (300, 500, or 700 ◦ C) with a heating rate of 5 ◦ C/min and a residence time of 1 h at the peak temperature based on (Shen et al., 2019a;

## 2.2. Biochar amendment and experimental design

The design of biochar amendment is shown in Table 3. In order to assess the effects of pyrolysis temperature and particle size of biochar on soil  erosion and Hg immobilization, two groups of experiments were designed. For the first group, biochars produced at the three different temperatures were added to the Hg contaminated soil, respectively, all with medium particle size, namely BC300M, BC500M, and BC700M. For the second group, biochars with the three different particle sizes were added to the Hg contaminated soil, respectively, with a pyrolysis temperature of 500 ◦ C, namely BC500C, BC500M, and BC500F. It should be noted that the results of both Control and BC500M treatments in group 1 and group 2 were collected from the same sample.

After  design,  the  biochars  were  thoroughly  mixed  with  the  Hg contaminated soil at a dosage of 5% (biochar:soil dry weight) based on (O ' Connor et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2019c) using an electronic mixer (SM966, Cheftronic, China) and incubated for 7 days at the moisture content of 20% w/w to avoid either too dry or wet for the soil. Afterwards, the mixtures were compacted to soil trays, respectively, to obtain a bulk density of 1.2 g/cm 3 (Keesstra et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2019).

The design of the soil erosion experiment is shown in Fig. 1. The soils were designed with a slope of 15 ◦ to aid the collection of runoffs, the degree of which can be found in previous soil erosion experiments to simulate  sloping  agricultural  land  (Peng  et  al.,  2019;  Ouyang  et  al., 2018; Ricci et al., 2020). The water trays were put on top of the soil trays to simulate rainfall. Orderly small pores were created on the bottom of the water tray to make sprinkle. Filter papers were put on the bottom of the water trays to control the rain speed. The runoffs and infiltrations were collected separately for each treatment via outlets connected to buckets.

The  rainfall  simulation  was  designed  based  on  local  climate.  In Beijing,  the  4  largest  rainfall  events  (219,  61,  57,  50 mm = 97 mm average) account for ~42% of annual precipitation (919 mm) (O ' Connor et al., 2019). Each day, an amount of 97 mm of simulated rainwater was  added  to  the  water  tray  which  takes  about  3 h  to  drop  out,

It should be noted that the soil layer of control group and BC500M were broken down after cycle 10. Water can directly go down through the  open  channel  and  no  runoff  happened  for  these  two  samples. Therefore, the experimental data for these two groups after cycle 10 were not analyzed, and the effects of simulated rainfall after cycle 10 was not further discussed.

## 2.3. Physical and chemical analysis

The volume was determined for each collected runoff and infiltration. Afterwards, they were settled down for 12 h. The supernatant was collected, infiltrated using a 0.45 µ m filter, and analyzed for Hg concentration. The Hg amount in runoff and infiltration was calculated by multiplying its concentration and water volume. The sediments from runoff was obtained through infiltration and oven drying at 40 ◦ C until reaching constant weight before mass weighting. The particle size distribution and total and bioavailable Hg concentrations of the sediments were determined. Particle size distribution was measured as per Section 2.1. The total Hg concentration of the sediments were obtained by aqua regia digestion of the sample followed by Hg concentration analysis after dilution and filtration. The bioavailable Hg was tested using 0.1 M HCl solution extraction at a solid to liquid ratio of 1:5 based on (Zhao et al., 2020a).  All  solution  Hg  concentrations  were  determined  using  a cold-vapor atomic fluorescence spectroscopy (CV AFS) (Tekran 2600). The detection limit of CV AFS is 0.01 μ g/L, and the analytical quality was controlled by reference material check.

## 2.4. Statistical analysis

The pH, CEC, DOC, and Hg concentration in runoff, infiltration, and sediments were analyzed in triplicates. Where necessary, the mean and standard deviations were reported for each experiment. The difference between  groups  was  evaluated  by  a  one-way  analysis  of  variance (ANOVA) at the significance level of 0.05 where available. Other tests were conducted once.

During infiltration, the DOC of BC300 dissolved downwards and simultaneously complexed with Hg in soil, thus transporting more Hg to infiltration water.  The  lower  pH  with  BC300M  may  also  result  in  lower  Hg adsorption due to increased electrostatic repulsion between biochar and Hg cations (Al-Ghouti et al., 2019).

## 3.3. Soil loss in runoff

All biochar amendments significantly reduced soil loss from cycle 1 -4 (Fig. 6a and b and Table 8), suggesting biochar ' s  importance in inhibiting soil erosion. The soil, especially the surface, was compacted after the initial rainfall cycles, and therefore, the erosion of soil was generally negligible after cycle 4. The soil loss in filtration water for all samples was not detected, and therefore the results are not shown.

The reduction rate of soil loss was within 37.0 -74.7% for biochar treatments at cycle 1, and cumulatively within 43.5 -77.2% from cycle 1 -10. BC700M resulted in the lowest soil loss with the highest cumulative  erosion  reduction  rate  (77.2%)  among  all  biochars,  whereas BC300 revealed the lowest cumulative erosion reduction rate of 43.5%. The biochars produced at 500 ◦ C also revealed good resistance to soil erosion, with cumulative erosion reduction rates of 65.7 -71.3%. The effect of biochar particle size on soil loss reduction was not as significant as pyrolysis temperature. Neither pyrolysis temperature nor particle size was a significant factor after 4 cycles.

It can be found that the particle size of the sediments for all treatments (including control group) are significantly smaller than the parent soil (Fig. 7a and b). The d50 of the sediments was within 10.8 -15.7 µ m, while that of the parent soil was 92.1 µ m (Fig. 7c and d). The parent soil predominantly comprises sand (75%) (Fig. 7e and f). In contrast, the sediments mainly contain silt (78 -89%), and the clay fraction (6 -10%) of the sediments was also significantly higher than the parent soil (2%). This coincides with previous finding that silt and clay are more likely to be eroded than sand (Lin et al., 2017). The influence of biochar on the particle size of sediments was not significant.

Compared with the parent soil (20.2 mg/g), all sediments showed higher  Hg  concentration  (39.7 -46.8 mg/kg)  at  cycle  1  (p &lt; 0.05) (Fig. 8). This is related to their higher clay and silt contents. Clay and silt have stronger ability to retain Hg than sand, and therefore, the majority of Hg in the parent soil was on those small particles (Quinton and Catt, 2007). The detached Hg in the runoff was also enriched in the silt and clay (Quinton and Catt, 2007). Our finding that metals concentrated in the sediments coincides with (Quinton and Catt, 2007), in which it was found that the mean concentrations of Cr, Cu, Pb, and Ni were up to 3.98 times higher in the sediment than in the parent soil. The bioavailable fraction of the Hg in the sediments was not detected, suggesting that although the sediments enriched Hg, their bioavailability and mobility were low. This may be due to the relatively strong binding between Hg and clay and silt compared to sand due to the higher surface area of clay and silt. The effect of biochar on the Hg concentration of sediments was not significant.

## 3.4. Discussion

Soil pollution by heavy metals such as Hg is ubiquitous (Hou et al., 2020b). The wide distribution of such toxic elements pose a challenge for the study of their mobility, transport and spatial distribution (Wang et al., 2021a; Jia et al., 2021), as well as their immobilization mechanisms and long term effectiveness (Zhao et al., 2020b; Hou et al., 2020). This study aims to understand 1) whether the rice husk biochar aided the chemical immobilization of Hg in the soil, 2) how the biochars affected the erosion of the Hg contaminated soil and its associated environmental risks,  and  3)  what  are  the  optimized  production  temperature  and particle size for the rice husk biochar for Hg immobilization and erosion prevention.

All biochar amendments reduced the Hg concentration in runoff and infiltration except BC300M which increased Hg concentration in infiltration,  suggesting  a  generally  good  chemical  immobilization  of  Hg caused by the rice husk biochars. Biochar can adsorb Hg through cation exchange, complexation, and cationπ interaction  (Guo  et  al.,  2020). Although a range of previous studies used modified biochars (especially sulfur modified biochars) to enhance Hg immobilization (e.g., through forming HgS precipitation)(Zhao et al., 2020a; O ' Connor et al., 2018; Park et al., 2019), this work demonstrates that raw biochars produced from rice husks can be an effective Hg immobilizer, which is in line with previous findings that hull biochar, wheat straw biochar, and hardwood biochar  without  modification  can  effectively  immobilize/adsorb  Hg (Xing et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019). This finding is profound for practical application because the absence of modification can significantly lower the cost and life-cycle carbon footprint during production compared with those modified biochars. In line with previous findings (Liu et al., 2019; Beckers et al., 2019b), this work observed that  the  DOC  leached  by  low-temperature  biochar  may  result  in  the mobilization of Hg. When the DOC concentration of biochar exceeded a certain value, this mobilization effect can counter those immobilization effects and result in an increased Hg concentration in soil water. This phenomenon did not happen for surface runoff, because the dissolution of organic matter from biochar to surface runoff is limited. The majority of the organic matter dissolved during infiltration, and therefore this risk may finally pose to groundwater in field conditions. Biochars produced at low temperatures (e.g., 300 ◦ C) typically contain relatively high DOC concentration, and therefore may not be suitable for Hg immobilization. Those  produced  at  500 ◦ C  or  above  may  be  more  suitable  for  Hg immobilization for practical applications based on this work.

The  sediments  in  runoff  are  fine  particles  (predominantly  silt). Although  Hg  was  enriched  in  the  sediments  regardless  of  biochar amendment,  its  bioavailability  was  low,  probably  due  to  its  tight complexation with soil organic matter and clay fraction (Zhao et al., 2020a). This suggests the environmental risks of Hg migration caused by surface runoff may be lower than previously expected. However, these relatively stable fraction may be mobilized during field ageing in the long term (J. Wang et al., 2020; L. Wang et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2019b; Wang  et  al.,  2021b).  All  biochar  amendments  significantly  reduced runoff  volume and soil loss,  suggesting  a  superior  prevention  of  soil erosion. However, due to the enhanced infiltration volume, although Hg concentration  in  infiltration  was  reduced  by  biochar  amendments (except BC300M), its total mobilized amount was increased compared with control. This means more Hg may be released to groundwater after biochar amendments. However, it should be noted that there are typically  an  unsaturated  zone  between  the  biochar  amended  zone  and saturated groundwater zone under field conditions except paddy rice fields. These increased Hg in infiltration may be adsorbed by the soils in the unsaturated zone, therefore reducing its migration to groundwater. The soil mass in infiltration was not detected, therefore, the migration of Hg through soil particle via groundwater can be generally neglected.

We found that higher temperature biochars performed better in Hg immobilization and erosion prevention. The higher Hg immobilization ability is related to its higher surface area and more developed aromatic structure aiding the physical adsorption and cation-Hg interaction (Guo et al., 2020). The more developed porous structure may also aid soil aggregation and prevent soil physical erosion by rainfall. The porous structure enhanced water infiltration, thus reducing runoff and consequently soil erosion. In general, the effect of particle size of biochar on Hg  immobilization  and  erosion  prevention  was  less  significant  than production temperature.
