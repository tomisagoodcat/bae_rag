## A B S T R A C T

Methylmercury (MeHg) accumulation in rice is an emerging food safety issue in China and other countries; however, mitigation methods are scarce. Here, the effects of selenium (Se) and multiple applications of Se and biochar on rice MeHg bioaccumulation were investigated using pot and microcosm experiments. We report that Se amendment was still effective in reducing MeHg levels in paddy soil and rice grain after three years of aging. Biochar amendment (0.5% w/w ) further decreased grain (brown rice) MeHg levels by 82-87%. The grain MeHg level decrease following the combination of Se and biochar amendment could be partly attributed to inhibition of net MeHg production in soil by Se. In addition, biochar decreased not only net MeHg production but also MeHg bioavailability in the soil, which could be due to organosulfur compounds in the biochar. Our findings suggest that multiple applications of Se and biochar could be a novel remediation strategy to mitigate MeHg accumulation in rice.

## 1. Introduction

Concerns about methylmercury (MeHg) accumulation in rice and its potential health risk to human beings are increasing [1-3], especially in certain mercury (Hg)-contaminated areas in Asian countries [4-7], e.g., China. Similar to other wetlands, paddy soil serves as a hotspot of MeHg production [2,8-10] and is the main source of MeHg in rice [11,12]. Therefore, reducing MeHg net production and the phytoavailability of MeHg in paddy soils could be critical for mitigating the risk of MeHg accumulation in rice and thus dietary MeHg exposure to Asian populations.

Remediation technologies for effective stabilization or removal of Hg in soils/sediments have been developed over the years [7,13]. However, cost-effective and green in situ remediation technologies for Hg-contaminated paddy soil have been scarcely reported. Recent studies using pot experiments have shown that the application of sulfurcontaining agents, such as elemental sulfur, thiosulfate and sulfate, could have an inhibitory effect on rice MeHg accumulation [14,15]. Because reduced selenium (Se) species have higher affinity for Hg than reduced sulfur species under anoxic conditions [16], Se amendment might effectively reduce soil MeHg levels and rice MeHg accumulation. Indeed, Se, either as ambient Se in soils or amended Se, has been reported to reduce MeHg accumulation in rice [17-20]. For instance, a decrease in MeHg levels in rice grain was positively related to levels of Se in the soil [17]. The results of pot experiments also indicated that the addition of Se could reduce MeHg levels in soil and rice grain [18,18,19,20]. The proposed mechanisms include (1) the formation of less bioavailable Hg-Se nanoparticles and a subsequent reduction in Hg methylation in soils [19-21], and (2) the formation of insoluble Hg-Se complexes on rice roots, which could limit the uptake and translocation of MeHg to the aerial parts of rice plants [17]. This recent progress advances our understanding of the relatively low bioaccumulation of MeHg in rice in some Hg-contaminated areas with high ambient Se levels, e.g., the Wanshan Hg mining area [17], and suggests the possibility of in situ remediation of Hg-contaminated soils by Se amendment, e.g., soil fertilization with Se. Moreover, Se is an essential trace element for plants, animals and human beings, although high levels of Se in soil could lead to potential environmental risk [22,23], e.g., &gt; 10 mg/kg of Se amendment into soil resulted in a decreased dry matter yield of rice shoots [24]. Studies have indicated that Se can protect mammals, aquatic organisms and humans from Hg toxicity and bioaccumulation [16,25-27], and Se intake/supplementation may be beneficial for mitigating the health risk of Hg exposure [27,28]. Thus, applying a certain amount of Se in Hg-contaminated paddy soil could be helpful for increasing Se levels in rice grain which may further reduce the health risk of dietary MeHg exposure from rice consumption.

However, knowledge gaps exist for further understanding the interactions between MeHg and Se in soils. (1) While Hg-Se interactions in soil have been documented on the time scale of days to months [18-21], the long-term effects of Se amendment on MeHg production and bioaccumulation are unclear. (2) The potential of Se to control the mobility or phytoavailability of MeHg in soils is not well understood. If Se is not effective for immobilizing MeHg in soils, the application of other agents (coapplication) to immobilize MeHg in soils could be necessary. Coapplication is also important for reducing the amended Se dose because of the narrow range between Se deficiency and Se toxicity [22,23]. The abovementioned knowledge gaps should be filled before Se can be used in practice to mitigate the risk of MeHg bioaccumulation in rice.

Here, biochar was considered for coapplication with Se to immobilize MeHg in soils. Biochar has also been reported to remove MeHg from solution [29] and reduce MeHg accumulation in rice [30]. Straw biochar has been documented to immobilize MeHg in paddy soils in a few pioneering studies. For example, the MeHg mobility/phytoavailability in flooded paddy soils, which was quantified by the ammonium thiosulfate extraction method [31], decreased by 49-92% following amendment with biochar (1-4% w/w ) [30]. In addition to mitigating MeHg mobility, biochar amendment could possibly facilitate Se-induced MeHg reduction in soils via mediating Se biogeochemical cycles. The benefits of the Se and biochar coapplication have mainly been attributed to (1) the redox properties of biochar [32,33], which can enhance the transformation of Se in paddy soil because Se is a redoxsensitive trace element [20,34,35] and (2) the enhanced mobility of anionic Se(IV)/Se(VI) in soil via electrostatic repulsion interactions because the surface of biochar is negatively charged at approximately neutral pH [36]. Therefore, we hypothesize that the coapplication of biochar with Se may further reduce MeHg accumulation in rice compared to the reduction achieved with a single application of Se by reducing microbial MeHg production in soils.

In this study, we aimed to accomplish the following: (1) investigate the effects of Se amendment with aging on MeHg production, mobility/ phytoavailability in paddy soils and accumulation in rice; (2) explore the possibility and mechanisms of further reducing MeHg production and immobilizing MeHg in Se-amended or Se-rich soils by biochar application. Rice cultivation (i.e., pot experiment) and soil incubation under anoxic or subanoxic conditions (i.e., microcosm experiments) were conducted to quantify the respective or combined effects of Se and biochar amendment on soil MeHg levels, MeHg mobility/phytoavailability, and rice MeHg levels. In particular, the chemical speciation of sulfur (S) in biochar was determined using S K-edge synchrotron radiation X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy to explore the possible mechanisms of Hg-biochar interactions. The results and mechanistic information gained in this study will enhance the understanding of Hg-Se and Hg-biochar interactions in paddy soils and indicate future avenues for mitigating MeHg production and bioaccumulation in Hg-contaminated soil-rice systems.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Soil and biochar

The aged (three years) Hg-spiked and/or Se-spiked soil was used in this study. The original soil was collected from a rice paddy field (0-20 cm) in Yixing, Jiangsu Province of China and spiked with inorganic Hg (Hg(NO3)2·H2O) to simulate Hg pollution that occurs in paddy soil from wastewater discharge, and then, Se (Na2SeO3 (Se(IV)) or Na2SeO4 (Se(VI))) was added to the soil to obtain a series of Se concentrations in soil (3 and 6 mg/kg Se(IV) and Se(VI), respectively). The prepared spiked soil samples were used for the pot experiments, which were designed originally in 2014 to investigate the effects of Se amendment on MeHg accumulation in rice (2014.06-2014.10), as presented in our previous study [20]. After 120 days under flooded rice cultivation conditions in pot experiments, the soil was air-dried and stored in plastic containers for approximately three years (2014.11-2017.05). Thus, the spiked soil used in this study was aged for a total of three years.

In the present study, the soil samples amended with 3.0 mg/kg Se (IV) or Se(VI) in our previous study were mixed together to ensure a sufficient soil amount for rice growth, and the soil samples with 6.0 mg/ kg Se(IV) or Se(VI) were also mixed. Our previous study showed that the initial Se species (i.e., Se(IV) and Se(VI)) had minimal effects on reducing soil MeHg levels, which was mainly due to the rapid transformation of Se in soils [20,21].

Biochar was produced by pyrolysis of bamboo ( Phyllostachys edulis ) in a muffle furnace. The bamboo was cut into ∼2 × 2 cm squares and placed in a crucible. Subsequently, the crucible was covered with aluminum foil and heated at a rate of 10 °C/min until 600 °C and held at that temperature for 1 h in a muffle furnace. Then, the bamboo-derived biochar was ground, sieved (≤0.28 mm) and mixed homogeneously.

## 2.2. Pot experiments

In the pot experiments, the aged soils were divided into two groups (Table 1, Fig. 1). One group contained aged Se soil without biochar amendment to examine the effects of Se amendment with aging on MeHg bioaccumulation in rice. Another group contained aged Se soil with biochar amendment (0.5% w/w , field application rate of approximately 10 t/ha) to investigate the combined effects of Se and biochar on MeHg bioaccumulation in rice. For each group, three treatments were conducted in triplicate. The rice cultivation details are presented in SI Text 1.1.

Soil samples were collected during the rice growth period following the seedling transplant stage (15 days after flooding), heading stage (70 days after flooding) and harvest (132 days after flooding). Two soil core samples (∼3 cm high and 1 cm in diameter) were sampled from each pot and immediately mixed and vacuum-packed. All samples were transferred to the laboratory in a box with ice and sectioned in an anaerobic workstation (Electrotek AW300SG, UK).

At harvest, grains, straws and roots were collected and rinsed thoroughly with tap water and deionized water (Milli-Q, Millipore). Rice hulls were removed from the grains using a mortar and pestle to obtain unpolished brown rice. Straw is a mixture of stem and leaves.

## 2.3. Microcosm experiments

To obtain insight into the combined effects of Se and biochar on soil MeHg levels and MeHg mobility/phytoavailability, two microcosm experiments were conducted (Table 1, Fig. 1). Experiment 1 was conducted to evaluate the effects of biochar dose on net MeHg production in anoxic soil. Different levels of biochar (0.5, 1, 2, and 5% w/w ) were added to soil without or with Se (i.e., CK or Se treatments) and incubated anaerobically for 6 days. Experiment 2 was conducted to examine the dynamics of soil MeHg concentrations over time under anoxic conditions followed by sequential oxidation on day 30 in the presence of biochar (5% w/w ) (Table 1). Briefly, 6 g of air-dried soil was placed into 50 mL centrifuge tubes (Corning, USA) and suspended in 30 mL of deionized water with biochar and Na-lactate (final concentration 5 M) amendment. All treatments in triplicate were incubated at 28 °C in the dark [37,38]. Samples of the soil and overlying water were collected at different time points. A more detailed description of the sampling technique and chemical analysis is given SI Text 1.2 and 1.3.

## 2.4. Analytical methods

The levels of total mercury (THg) and MeHg in the soil, plant and biochar samples were digested and analyzed according to USEPA methods 7473 and 1630, respectively. The Se levels in the soils, plants and overlying water samples were determined using the methods described previously [20]. The certified reference materials were digested and measured for quality control, and the recoveries are shown in Table S2. All concentrations are reported on a dry weight basis. The details of the chemical analysis are presented in SI Text 1.3.

The mobility/phytoavailability of MeHg in soil was assessed by ammonium thiosulfate ((NH4)2S2O3) extraction, as reported by Zhu et al. [31]. The fraction of extractable MeHg in soil, presented as %, was calculated by dividing the (NH4)2S2O3 extraction fraction by the total soil MeHg. Sulfur species in biochar were determined by S K-edge XANES, and the experimental details are presented in SI Text 1.4.

## 2.5. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using one-way ANOVA analysis of variance ( p &lt; 0.05) to compare data. Significant effects from the ANOVA results were further examined with Tukey's post hoc multiple comparisons test after homogeneity of variance test (SPSS, version 16.0).

## 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1. Effects of Se amendment with aging on soil MeHg levels, MeHg mobility/phytoavailability and MeHg accumulation in rice

After three years of aging, Se amendment was still effective in reducing soil MeHg levels and MeHg accumulation in rice (Fig. 2A, B). Soil MeHg levels decreased by 27-60% on day 15, 42-54% on day 70 and 31-51% on day 132 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A). Moreover, MeHg concentrations in rice tissues were significantly lower in the presence of aged Se (Fig. 2B). For example, the MeHg levels in rice grain (brown rice) decreased from 338 ± 12 μg/kg in the control to 157 ± 5 μg/kg with a Se dose of 6.0 mg/kg. Because no significant differences were observed in the tissue biomass among the treatments (Table S3), the overall uptake of MeHg by rice plants (μg/pot) amended with Se was lower than that of the control (Fig. 3A).

It should be noted that multiple applications of Se and biochar reduced the brown rice MeHg by 88% (i.e., 3.0Se + BC verses Control), which is far superior to the results with the application of sulfur-containing agents (∼60% decrease with 100 mg/kg elemental S amendment) [14], rice straw-derived biochar (∼50% decrease with 1% w/w amendment) [30], or the coapplication of Se with sulfate (∼64% decrease with 3 mg/kg Se and 960 mg/kg sulfate amendment) reported previously [41]. Moreover, the application of Se or biochar alone reduced the brown rice MeHg by up to 53% (6.0 mg/kg Se) or 85% (0.5% w/w ) in this study. The reduction percentages of rice MeHg were lower than those following the coapplication of Se and biochar (up to 92%, 6.0Se + BC verses Control), indicating the synergistic inhibitory effects of Se and biochar on MeHg bioaccumulation.

The decline in MeHg accumulation in the presence of Se and biochar is unlikely to be associated with the variation in Se mobility/phytoavailability because the Se accumulation in rice from the 3.0Se + BC and 6.0Se + BC treatment groups did not differ significantly from that of the counterparts without biochar addition (i.e., 3.0Se and 6.0Se, Fig. S1). Such a reduction is likely due to a decrease in the bioavailable faction of MeHg in soil, as demonstrated by the decreases in the fraction of extractable MeHg in soil under biochar amendment (Fig. 3D), and consequently, the decrease in MeHg uptake (Fig. 3C). Indeed, a positive relationship was observed between the extractable MeHg levels in soil and the MeHg concentrations in brown rice (r 2 =0.96, p &lt; 0.001, Fig. S2). In addition, the decrease in the soil MeHg levels with biochar amendment (Fig. 2C) could be another reason for reduced MeHg accumulation in rice.

Our results also showed that bamboo-derived biochar is effective in reducing soil MeHg concentrations (Fig. 2C), which is in agreement with previous results for switchgrass-derived biochar [42]. However, increased MeHg concentrations in soil have been reported with the addition of rice straw-derived biochar [30]. This discrepancy could be due to differences in the biochar compositions and changes in soil redox chemistry, which merits further investigation.

3.3. Mechanistic understanding of the combined effects of Se and biochar amendment on the production and mobility of MeHg in soil

A 6-day microcosm experiment was conducted to evaluate the effects of biochar on net MeHg production in anoxic soil without or with Se (i.e., CK and Se treatments) with a wide range of added doses (0.5, 1, 2, and 5% w/w , Experiment 1). The variation in the soil MeHg concentrations in response to biochar amendment was similar in both the CK and Se treatment groups (Fig. 4A). The concentrations of MeHg in the soil decreased with increasing doses of biochar, although these two series of treatments differed in their initial soil MeHg concentrations (Fig. 4A). For example, the soil MeHg concentrations decreased by 73% and 71% in the CK and Se treatment series, respectively, when the biochar content in soil increased to 5%. In particular, a sharp decrease was observed in the soil MeHg concentration, i.e., a decrease of ∼58% at a dose of 1%, followed by a gentle decrease of ∼15% at doses of 1-5% in the CK and Se treatment groups (Fig. 4B). The MeHg concentration in the overlying water also showed a dramatic decrease with increasing biochar doses, i.e., approximately a 78% decrease at the lower dose (1%) and up to a 95% reduction at a higher dose (5%) (Fig. S3 A), indicating that bamboo-derived biochar strongly adsorbs MeHg.

Interestingly, the fraction of extractable MeHg exhibited a similar decreasing trend with increasing biochar dose (Fig. 4C), i.e., ∼77% reduction at a lower dose (1%) and up to ∼95% reduction at a high dose (5%). These results suggest that biochar is effective in reducing soil MeHg levels and the mobile/phytoavailable fraction of soil MeHg even at a low application rate. Our results indicated that in the examined system, biochar, rather than Se, played the dominant role in reducing the production and mobility of MeHg in soil. In addition, the dissolved Se and iron (Fe) contents in the overlying water slightly increased and then decreased with increasing biochar doses (Fig. S4), even though the redox potential (Eh) in the overlying water greatly decreased (Fig. S5B). This result indicated that biochar has a minimal impact on Se mobility/phytoavailability or Fe reduction in the examined system.

To distinguish the roles of Se and biochar in reducing the soil MeHg concentration and the extractable fraction of soil MeHg, another microcosm experiment was conducted at a biochar dose of 5% under anoxic conditions followed by sequential oxidation on day 30 (Experiment 2). This experiment simulated the episodic flooding and draining of paddy soils during rice growth. In the absence of biochar, the MeHg concentrations in the soil (Fig. 5A) and overlying water (Fig. S6) peaked on day 8 and declined thereafter. After day 30, i.e., when the soil was reoxidized (Fig. S7A), the soil MeHg concentrations increased and then leveled off (Fig. 5A). Similar to the results in Experiment 1, the soil with added Se had lower MeHg concentrations than the soil without Se. The addition of biochar decreased the soil MeHg concentrations dramatically, i.e., 63-83% in CK + BC relative to that of CK treatment and 31-75% in Se + BC relative to that of the corresponding Se treatment from 6 to 50 days. Again, the differences in the soil MeHg levels between the treatment groups with or without Se were less evident in the presence of biochar (Fig. 5A).

## 4. Conclusions and environmental implications

Our results show that the inhibitory effect of Se on soil MeHg levels may persist long-term, i.e., at least three years. However, the inhibition efficiency decreased due to the aging effect. We showed that biochar amendment with a dose as low as 0.5% w/w could further decrease the net MeHg production and mobility. Although inhibition of MeHg accumulation in rice grain was observed with both Se and biochar amendment, the inhibition mechanisms differ. Se decreases the soil MeHg concentration via the formation of Hg-Se complexes, which are less unfavorable for microbial methylation. In contrast, biochar decreases the soil MeHg levels via the complexation of IHg with organosulfur groups and increased MeHg partitioning into soil solids via adsorption and complexation.

Given that the application rate of Se should be carefully considered due to its potential environmental risk at high levels, a remediation strategy combining both Se and bamboo-derived biochar, as shown here, could be an alternative approach for Hg-contaminated soil or sediment. Nevertheless, further research is necessary to investigate the long-term effects of biochar on decreasing MeHg in paddy soil and rice.
