## A B S T R A C T

Mercury (Hg) alkylation and methane (CH4) emissions pose significant global concerns. Paddy soil, due to its long-term anaerobic conditions and abundant organic matter, is hotspots for soil Hg alkylation and CH4  emissions. However, the relevance between Hg alkylation and CH4 emissions, especially their simultaneous reduction strategies, remains poorly understood. Here, we investigated the effects of biochar (BC), selenium (Se) and rice straw (RS) amendments on Hg alkylation and CH4  emissions in paddy soil, and the accumulation of Hg speciation. Results found that both BC and RS amendments significantly increased the levels of soil organic carbon (SOC) and humification index (HIX). Furthermore, BC decreased the concentrations of Hg(II), methylmercury (MeHg) and ethylmercury (EtHg) by 63.1%, 53.6% and 100% in rice grains. However, RS increased Hg(II) concentration but decreased the total Hg (THg), MeHg and EtHg concentrations in rice grains. Compared to the CK, RS significantly increased CH4 emissions, while BC decreased CH4 emissions, and Se showed no significant difference. Se amendment increased the Hg(II) and EtHg concentrations by 20.3% and 17.0% respectively, and decreased the MeHg concentration in grains by 58.3%. Both BC and RS impacted the abundance of methanogens by enhancing SOC and HIX, subsequently modulating the relevance between Hg alkylation and CH4 emissions.

## Environmental implication

Alkylmercury primarily consists of methylmercury (MeHg), which is one of the most hazardous pollutants due to its prevalence, persistence, and extreme toxicity. Our study demonstrated that many rice fields still suffered from MeHg pollution and excessive methane (CH4) emissions. Further  analysis  suggested  that  processes  of  MeHg  transformation interact  with  CH4 emissions.  The  results  confirmed  that  biochar amendment decreased the bioconcentration and accumulation of MeHg, promoted soil carbon sequestration, and weakened the association between mercury alkylation and CH4  emissions. Collectively, these findings  offer  valuable  ecological  insights  into  the  relationship  between mercury alkylation and CH4 emissions, proposing remediation strategies for rice fields.

## 1. Introduction

Mercury  (Hg)  is  a  highly  toxic  and  globally  pervasive  pollutant (Wang et al., 2021; Cossa et al., 2022; Lei et al., 2023). Methylmercury (MeHg), one of its most fatal compounds, can bioaccumulate and biomagnify by up to 10 7 times along the food chain, making it a significant global environmental and health concern (Tang et al., 2019; Blanchfield et al., 2022; Ran et al., 2022). Rice ( Oryza sativa L.) serves as a primary food source for most people, and paddy soil is a significant source of MeHg production. MeHg formed in the soil can be transported into rice plants and primarily accumulate in rice grains (Sun et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). Meanwhile, methane (CH4) is a highly potent greenhouse gas (GHG)(Ernst et al., 2022; Sabrekov et al., 2023) with a 20-year global warming potential (GWP) 80 times greater than that of carbon dioxide (CO2) (IPCC, 2022). Paddy fields contribute about 20% of global anthropogenic CH4 emissions and are one of the greatest anthropogenic sources (Luo et al., 2023). The CH4 emission from paddy soil is predominantly influenced by the activity of methanogens in the anaerobic soil. These microorganisms convert simple substrates such as formic acid, ethanol, and acetic acid, into CH4  (Edwards et al., 2019). Although a significant amount of CH4 is consumed by methanotrophic bacteria, the surplus CH4 is mainly released into the atmosphere through various pathways, including emissions through rice plants, the production and release of bubbles, and interfacial diffusion.

Currently, common agricultural management practices, such as the return of rice straw (RS) and the application of biochar (BC), have been found to contribute a significant amount of organic matter to paddy soils, and are widely used in paddy fields. Moreover, both RS and BC have received increasing attention in the environmental field as an integrated solution for soil amendment and carbon sequestration (Beckers et  al.,  2019;  Tang  et  al.,  2019;  Kan  et  al.,  2023;  Xia  et  al.,  2023). Additionally, selenium (Se) amendment has been demonstrated as an effective  approach  for  inhibiting  Hg  methylation  in  soils,  and  the reduction in MeHg production by Se was attributed to the formation of bulk HgSe (Ksp = 10 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 58 ) (Dang et al., 2019; Cai et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021; Ran et al.,  2023). Subsequent research also identified discrete nanoparticles of HgSe in the cell membrane of Hg -methylating bacteria, plant root  and  soil,  and  proposed  that  these  nanoparticles  also contribute  to  the  immobilization  of  Hg  and  the  reduction  in  MeHg production (Wang et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2020).

Methanogens  contribute  to  the  process  of  Hg  methylation,  and methanogenesis is associated with Hg methylation in paddy soil (Gilmour et al., 2018). ## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Experiments design

The  physicochemical  properties  of  the  initial  paddy  soil  in  pot experiment are summarized in Table 1. Notably, the total Hg concentration of the initial paddy soil in pot experiment was 0.97 ± 0.08 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 . Soil samples were collected from the Hg-contaminated paddy field at a depth of 0 -20 cm. The experiment was conducted in white pots (18 cm high, 14 cm diameter), each containing 3.0 kg of soil with three replicates.  Each  treatment  was  planted  with  the  local  rice  variety, Yongyou 1540 (a  hybrid  indica-japonica  rice)  followed  local  recommendations. The pots were placed in a greenhouse with an ambient air temperature  ranging  from  15  to  37 ◦ C.  The  rice  pot  experiment encompassed  a  complete  rice  growth  season,  spanning  from  transplantation to maturity and harvest, over a duration of 129 days. In pot experiment, rice cultivation with four distinct treatments: BC, Se, RS and CK (the control). The percentages of BC and RS in the soil were both 1% (w/w), and the equivalent dose of Se was calculated to be 1.0 mg Se/kg soil. Rice straw, collected from an Hg-free location, was cleaned, airdried,  and  then  ground.  The  fertilization  and  irrigation  management for the pot experiment are shown in Text S1.

## 2.3. Chemical analysis

Total  Hg  (THg)  concentrations  in  soils  were  measured  using  a Multifunctional  Zeeman  effect  analyzer  (RA-915 + ,  Russia).  Hg(II), MeHg and EtHg in soil and plant samples were prepared as described (Leopold et al., 2010; Song et al., 2015). Soil (~5.00 g wet weight) and plant (~1.50 g dry weight) tissue samples were dried by lyophilization, weighed, and digested in an ampule containing a 3 M HNO3  solution. The samples were then subjected to ultrasonic bath treatment at 70 kHz; Elma for 1 h at room temperature. The resulting mixture was filtered through a membrane (pore size 0.22 μ m), and transfer the sample to a 2 mL sample bottle (Agilent) for HPLC-ICP-MS analysis. Quality control of Hg speciation analysis included triplicate sample analysis, method/reagent blanks, matrix spikes, and certified reference materials. Matrix spike recoveries of MeHg, obtained by spiking MeHg into soil samples, was approximately 83%. Hg speciation (Hg(II), MeHg and EtHg) were quantified using HPLC-ICP-MS analysis as described (Wu et al., 2022). More detailed descriptions of the quantification of Hg(II), MeHg and ethylmercury (EtHg) are described in Text S2. DOC was measured by an automated  total  organic  carbon  analyzer  (Multi  N/C  3100,  Analytik Jena AG, Jena, Germany) after extraction by Milli-Q water. Soil total organic carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur (TS) and total carbon content in rice plants were quantified using an elemental analyzer (Elementar Vario EL Cube, Germany). Data related to quantitative Real-Time PCR and Hg speciation are provided in Fig. 2. Details of the specific chemicals used in this study are provided in Table S1. After rice harvested, the rice roots, straw (stalks and leaves), and grains (brown rice) were cleaned, ovendried, weighed, and then pulverized for analysis. The analysis included determining concentrations of THg, MeHg, total C, total N, and total  S.  The  resulting  data  of  bio-concentration  factor  (BCF)  and  the translocation factor (TF) analysis are presented in Table S2. The analysis results of Hg -methylation genes and Hg speciation in soil are detailed in Text S3.

## 2.6. GHG sampling and analysis

GHG (CO2,  CH4,  N2O)  were  collected  throughout  the  whole  rice season employing the static closed-chamber method. The chamber dimensions were 11 cm in diameter and 50 or 100 cm in height, based on the respective plant height. The chamber was equipped with a pressurebalancing port to ensure gas exchange and maintain the stability of internal pressure. To ensure thorough mixing and extraction, a syringe was pulled back and forth 10 times, and 20 mL of gas was withdrawn from the chamber at intervals of 0, 20, 40 and 60 min after closure. Water-filled bottom troughs ensured an airtight seal within the chamber, eliminating air exchange (Fang et al., 2022). External solar heating was minimized, and temperature fluctuations were controlled by insulating the chamber using sponge and aluminum foil. Sampling was regularly conducted between 08:00 am and 10:00 am throughout the rice growth period. These samples were then promptly analyzed in the laboratory to measure GHG concentrations using a gas chromatograph (GC-2010 Plus Shimadzu, Japan) as described (Fang et al., 2022). The IPCC postulates that the carbon fixed and emitted as CO2  in paddy field ecosystems is in equilibrium, based on extensive empirical evidence that may not always align with individual experimental conditions. Therefore, to accurately depict the carbon  footprint,  it  is  imperative  to  recalculate  the  absorption  and release of CO2. ## 2.8. Fluorescence spectroscopy analysis

Fluorescence EEMs spectra of soil organic carbon (SOC) components were measured using a Hitachi F-4600 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi High Technologies, Japan) at room temperature (25 ± 2 ◦ C). Excitation wavelengths ranged gradually from 220 to 450 nm, collected in 5 nm increments, whereas emission wavelengths spanned gradually from 240 to 620 nm, also in 5 nm increments. The instrument used a constant scan speed of 12,000 nm min GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 .  The  modeling process was facilitated using the drEEM Toolbox (accessible at http://dreem.openf luor.org/),  within  MATLAB  R2022a  (MathWorks,  Natick,  MA).  To delve deeper into the dissolved organic matter (DOM) optical characteristics,  we  used  various  indices,  including  the  humification  index (HIX), biological index (BIX), fluorescence index (FI), freshness index (Fr), fluorescent dissolved organic matter (FDOM), and the fluorescence peak ratio of fulvic acid across ultraviolet and visible spectra (r (A/C)) (Jia et al., 2017; Fouche et al., 2020).

## 2.9. Statistical analysis

Statistical differences among different treatments were determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS 26.0 (IBM, IL, USA). Subsequent post hoc evaluations employed Duncan ' s tests, with statistical significance defined at P &lt; 0.05 (two-tailed). ## 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1. Accumulation of Hg speciation in rice grains and PDI

The influence of the BC amendment on Hg behaviors in paddy soils and rice grains has been investigated previously (Xing et al., 2020; Man et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; Xing et al., 2022). Although extensive studies have been carried out, the BC amendment on Hg methylation and its  migration  and  accumulation  in  the  soil-rice  system  with  Hgcontaminated  paddy  soil  is  not  fully  understood.  In  this  study,  we found that the BC amendment significantly decreased Hg(II), MeHg and EtHg concentrations by 33.34, 8.32 and 0.61 ng g GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , respectively, with reductions of 63.1%, 53.6% and 85.9% in rice grains compared to the CK (Fig. 3b-d). Our findings were consistent with a previous study where the MeHg concentration in rice grains decreased by 52.1% -85.0% with a similar application rate of BC (Man et al., 2021). Possible reasons for the reduced MeHg accumulation in rice grains due to BC modification are discussed below.

Under the anaerobic conditions  of  paddy  soil,  Hg(II)  was  readily converted to MeHg by Hg -methylating bacteria (Beckers et al., 2019). Notably, our results indicated that BC  amendment  significantly increased soil Hg(II) concentration ( P &lt; 0.05), so soil MeHg concentration should have increased significantly, but in fact it did not. The reason might be that both Delta and Archaea can cause strong methylation of Hg (Liu et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2019; Du et al., 2021), and BC significantly reduced the abundance of the Delta and Archaea genes by 46.4% and 10.1% compared to the CK (Fig. 2a,b). Additionally, the study revealed that BC increased the HIX (Fig. 4d), which was known to potentially hinder the release and bioaccumulation of Hg(II) in soil, as corroborated by Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2020a). BC amendment decreased the BCF of Hg(II) and MeHg in root/soil by 71.3% and 59.4% (Fig. 3b,c), and the TF of Hg(II) and MeHg in grain/stalk by 73.3% and 89.3%, respectively (Table S2), so the movement of Hg(II) and MeHg from these tissues  to  grains  was  inhibited  (Man  et  al.,  2021).  Therefore,  the mechanisms responsible for the lower MeHg concentration in rice grains could be related to the decreased BCF (by 73.3%) of MeHg in grain/soil (Fig. 3c).

Recently,  Se  amendment  in  paddy  soil  also  has  been  found  to decrease MeHg levels in both soil and rice grains in pot experiments (Li et al., 2019). Our study demonstrated that Se application significantly decreased MeHg in rice grains by 58.3% ( P &lt; 0.05) compared to the CK (Fig. 3c), which was consistent with previous results based on pot experiments (Wang et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2019). The MeHg decreased in rice grains after Se amendment can likely be attributed to several factors. Although the abundance of Delta in the soil decreased by 45.3% (Fig.  2a),  the  concentration  of  Hg(II)  in  the  soil  increased  (Fig.  2g) during  the  maturity  period,  resulting  in  no  significant  difference  in MeHg content when Se amendment was compared to the CK (Fig. 2h). In addition, Hg easily binds to organic matter in soils, resulting in a high proportion of organically bound Hg species due to its high affinity for sulfhydryl  groups  (Li  et  al.,  2019).  Our  study  found  Se  amendment increased HIX but with no significant difference (Fig. 4d). ## 3.2. Enhancing carbon sequestration in rice production

The CH4  emissions fluxes in paddy soil exhibited significant variations throughout the different periods of rice growth (Fig. 4a). The CH4 emissions increased during the rice tillering and booting periods but notably decreased during the seedling and maturity periods (Kan et al., 2023). Throughout the four growth periods of rice, CH4 emissions fluxes in  the  RS  treatment  surpassed  those  of  the  other  treatments,  mainly because RS addition could expedite turnover of organic carbon (Jiang et al., 2019; Kan et al., 2023). By magnitude, the ranking of CH4 emission fluxes was: RS &gt; Se &gt; CK &gt; BC (Fig. 4a). The changes in GWP across the rice growth periods reflected the patterns observed in CH4 emissions. The peak GWP value was observed at the rice tillering stage, succeeded by  the  booting  stage,  while  the  least  value  emerged  at  the  maturity stage.

Generally, the sequence of GWP values followed the order: RS &gt; Se &gt; CK &gt; BC (Fig. 4b). Compared to the CK and Se treatments, both the BC and  RS  amendments  increased  the  concentration  of  the  previously identified terrestrial humic-like peak A. Significantly, BC amendment also increased the concentrations of humic-like peaks C and M (Fig. 4c). HIX showed a substantial increase in BC and RS, corresponding to the observed  characteristic  fluorescence  peaks  of  humic-like  substances presented in Fig. 4d. Within the carbon indices, CH4  displayed a significant correlation with FI, Fr, HIX, BIX, and r (A/C) in all treatments except the CK. In the BC treatment, CH4  showed positive correlations with FIuI, Frel, and BIX, while displaying a negative correlation with HIX  and  r(A/C).  Conversely,  in  the  CK,  Se,  and  RS  treatments,  CH4 exhibited a positive correlation with HIX and r(A/C), but a negative correlation with FIeI, FreI and BIX (Fig. 4e). Within the GHG category,

The application of BC and Se amendments significantly enhanced carbon sequestration, achieving levels of 67.0% (1460.65 kg C-eq ha GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ) and 58.0% (996.56 kg C-eq ha GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ) respectively ( P &lt; 0.05). Concurrently, RS application increased carbon sequestration by 35.2% (392.19 kg Ceq  ha GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ),  as  illustrated  in  Fig.  4f.  Compared  to  the  CK,  soil  carbon sequestration was increased by the application of BC and Se. The GHG emissions decreased by nearly 120 kg C-eq ha GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 with BC amendment. However, RS incorporation could  not  increase  carbon  sequestration  due  to  the  significant  CH4 emissions.  The  possible  explanation  for  this  phenomenon  is  that  BC amendment reduces methanogenic growth, which is often stimulated by RS addition, thus decreasing CH4 emissions from organic carbon sources (Jeffery  et  al.,  2016).  Furthermore,  the  carbon  components  in  BC decompose  more  slowly  than  those  in  rice  straw,  which  gives  them relatively  greater  long-term  carbon  sequestration  potential  (Schmidt et al., 2021).

## 3.4. Optimal reduction strategies and recommendations

The application of BC amendment has led to significant reductions (20 -40% on average) in N2O and CH4  emissions, as reported by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC, 2022). Potential cobenefits  of  BC  amendment  comprise  enhanced  yields,  specifically  in sandy and acidic soils with low cation exchange capacity; improved soil water-holding  capacity,  nitrogen  use  efficiency,  biological  nitrogen fixation, adsorption of organic pollutants and heavy metals, as well as the  reduction  of  odours  from  manure  handling.  Our  research  data demonstrated a significant reduction of 53.6% ( P &lt; 0.05) in MeHg levels in rice grains with the BC amendment compared to the CK (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, BC amendment resulted in a reduction of GHG emissions and  a  significant  increase  in  carbon  sequestration  by  approximately 67.0%  (2181.31  kg  C-eq  ha GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ).  Despite  the  economic  investment necessary for biochar production, it emerges as a promising land-based mitigation strategy owing to its numerous benefits. These advantages render biochar a potentially cost-effective approach, potentially enabling economic viability at a lower carbon price compared to bioenergy coupled with carbon capture and storage (Karan et al., 2023). Through a comprehensive life-cycle assessment, biochar emerges as a financially feasible solution for mitigating climate change (Bossio et al., 2020; Lehmann et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2023). Due to the simple and cost-effective  benefits  of  selenite  as  a  soil amendment, its application holds significant potential for implementation in paddy soils globally (Dang et al., 2019). Se amendment could decrease  Hg  bioaccumulation  in  rice  grains,  but  it  also  could  cause excessive Se accumulation in grains and potential Se exposure risks for humans (Ran et al., 2023). Previous study showed that when Se was applied  to  soil  at  3 -6  mg  kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ,  the  Se  concentration  in  brown  rice increased by 7 -13 times (1.3 -2.3 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ) compared to the CK (Tang et al., 2017). Elevated Se levels in rice grains may lead to excessive daily Se intake ( &gt; 400 μ g day GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ) in humans, potentially posing health risks such as nail and hair loss, skin lesions, and even death (Dang et al., 2019; Hadrup and Ravn-Haren, 2020). For this reason, Se is a micronutrient with a ' double-edged sword ' , and the application of Se amendment in Hg-contaminated  paddy  soils  has  been  severely  limited  (Qin  et  al., 2023). In our study, we also found that the use of sodium selenite as a selenium supplement can be harmful to organic organisms in the soil due to its significant toxicity. Therefore, Se amendment might not be the optimal solution for remediating Hg-contaminated paddy soil.

Previous studies have cautioned that returning RS to the paddy field along may result in excessive MeHg accumulation in rice grains (Tang et  al.,  2019;  Sun  et  al.,  2022;  Yang  et  al.,  2023),  and  significantly accelerated  CH4 emissions  (Jiang  et  al.,  2019;  Kan  et  al.,  2023). Therefore, it is important to consider proper field returning techniques of RS to mitigate these potential risks. Based on our results, we recommend  an  enhanced  strategy  for  returning  RS  to  the  field,  including conversion  into  biochar  (lowering  global  CH4 emissions  by  12  Tg year GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ) (Wang et al., 2023a).

## 4. Conclusions

Our study focuses on a ubiquitous but previously overlooked new mechanism of Hg alkylation and derived CH4  within soil amendments decreasing the relevance between Hg alkylation and CH4  emissions by influencing  methanogenic  community.  The  principal  carbon  components crucial for soil carbon sequestration, closely associated with Hg speciation  transformation,  were  SOC  and  HIX,  and  their  levels  also increased with BC amendment. Thus, reduction of MeHg and EtHg cooccurred  with  increase  of  carbon  sequestration,  benefiting  establish the BC amendment as the most advantageous environmental strategy when compared to other alternative approaches (in our cases, amendments of Se or RS). Especially, the dominant methanogens identified included Methanosaeta , Methanocella and Methanobacterium , with Methanocellaceae as  the  most  predominant  functional  group  driving  the concurrent processes of Hg alkylation and CH4 emissions.

This study reduced the accumulation of MeHg and EtHg levels in rice grains  and the PDI of THg and MeHg by applying soil amendments, particularly  with  BC  treatment.  Furthermore,  the  BC  amendment significantly  reduced  CH4 emissions  from  paddy  soil,  improved  soil carbon sequestration, thereby contributing to climate warming mitigation. Our study presents an innovative approach for the safe farming in Hg-contaminated paddy soil while simultaneously enhancing soil carbon sequestration. Future work should further investigate the relevance between Hg alkylation and CH4 emissions, considering different levels of Hg contamination and rice varieties, and develop strategies to reduce their environmental footprints.
