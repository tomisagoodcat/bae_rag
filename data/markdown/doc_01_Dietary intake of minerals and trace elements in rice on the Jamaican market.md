Keywords:

Rice Trace elements Dietary intake Food composition Food analysis Jamaica Mineral nutrition Food safety AAS ICP-OES INAA TXRF

Twenty-five rice brands, available on the Jamaican market, and a local field trial sample were collected and analyzed for 36 essential, non-essential and toxic elements using four techniques: flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry (F-AAS), inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICPOES), instrumental neutron activation analysis (INAA), and total reflection X-ray fluorescence (TXRF). The mean values for both white and brown rice respectively for calcium (127 mg/kg; 104 mg/kg), chromium (0.08 mg/kg; 0.157 mg/kg), copper (1.65 mg/kg; 2.96 mg/kg), iron (22.3 mg/kg; 20.1 mg/kg), magnesium (371 mg/kg; 1205 mg/kg), manganese (10.5 mg/kg; 26.5 mg/kg), molybdenum (0.790 mg/ kg; 0.770 mg/kg), phosphorus (1203 mg/kg; 3361 mg/kg), potassium (913 mg/kg; 2157 mg/kg), selenium (0.108 mg/kg; 0.131 mg/kg), sodium (6.00 mg/kg; 15.1 mg/kg), sulfur (1131 mg/kg; 1291 mg/kg) and zinc (15.6 mg/kg; 20.2 mg/kg) were used to calculate intake values. The percentage contribution to Provisional Tolerable Weekly Intakes were also estimated for the toxic elements aluminum (6.6-17.9%), arsenic (7.8-10.2%), cadmium (5.1-9.0%) and mercury (3.2-12.0%) with ranges dependent on white or brown rice consumption and gender. It was concluded that, for most essential elements, rice does not significantly contribute to mineral nutrition. The local field trial sample yielded encouraging results in terms of uptake of toxic elements.

## 1. Introduction

Rice ( Oryza sativa ) is a widely consumed staple in Jamaica (Samuda et al., 1998a,b), and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) lists Jamaica among the countries that are significant per capita consumers of rice (Kennedy et al., 2002). Both plain rice and rice and peas were ranked among the top 10 most consumed foods in the country with rice being the only starch staple in the top 10. Although cooked rice and peas (composite sample from several Jamaican households) has been analyzed for some nutrients by the Caribbean Food and Nutrition Institute (Samuda et al., 1998b), plain rice was not analyzed and was found to be more popularly consumed in the Kingston Metropolitan Area where 28% of Jamaica's population lives; there has been no comprehensive elemental analysis of this predominant staple of the Jamaican population.

Large-scale, local cultivation of rice has been discontinued for more than two decades and the rice now available on the market is all imported. Some 44,882 metric tonnes of this (termed ''bulk rice'', which is polished white rice packaged without branding) was imported from Guyana in 2009 accounting for 57% of rice imports.

Another 25,266 tonnes was imported from the United States of America (JIS, 2010). In an effort to reduce the dependence on foreign supply of this important staple and consequently to lower the food import bill there has been a recent push to reintroduce farmers to rice cultivation.

A burgeoning health consciousness in some sectors of the public has seen a greater availability of brown rice on the Jamaican market. The fiber and higher vitamins and minerals as a result of the retention of the bran have increased the popularity of this version of the commodity. In addition, parboiled and/or enriched white rice is also available and these methods of processing can be expected to affect the elemental profile of white rice.

Food security is of growing global concern. These concerns include the reliability of supply and the quality of food. Food composition data is necessary when estimating a population's intake of nutrients and dietary exposure to toxins and indeed, assessing diet quality. This paper examines the elemental content of rice available on the market and therefore, its contribution to the Jamaican public's nutrition. Some Jamaican soils are naturally enriched in toxic elements such as arsenic, cadmium and mercury (Lalor et al., 1999). If local rice cultivation is to resume, it is vital that the uptake of toxic elements and nutrients is assessed. The analysis of locally grown rice from field trials allows preliminary estimates to be made toward achieving this objective.

## 2. Methodology

Commercially available rice samples were purchased from supermarkets in the parish of St. Andrew, Jamaica in February 2010. This parish was ideal for sampling as a significant portion of the Jamaican population resides there. While bulk rice is readily available across the island, other rice brands sampled are typically not sold outside the Kingston Metropolitan Area (which includes the parishes of Kingston and St. Andrew), and indeed the other brands of rice available on the market change from time to time but were representative of the brands available at the time of sampling. A minimum of 500 g of each rice brand was collected. A composite sample of locally grown rice produced in the parish of St. Catherine was also obtained from the Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries.

The husk was removed from the sample of locally grown rice, and the brown rice was washed with deionized water and ovendried at 65 8 C. All samples were ground to a fine powder for analysis using a Fritsch Pulverisette 2 automated agate mortar and pestle (Fritsch, Germany). Samples were prepared for analysis by ICP-OES, F-AAS and TXRF by acid digestion. 20 mL of 1:3 HCl:HNO3 (Fisher TraceMetal Grade acids; Fisher Chemical, MA, USA) was added to 1 g of sample in a 70 mL graduated polyethylene vial, covered with a polyethylene watch glass and allowed to stand overnight. The following day the samples were digested at 110 8 C for 2 h using a graphite heating block (ModBlock; CPI International, CA, USA) and made up to 50 mL with 2% HNO3 (Howe et al., 2005).

The analytical techniques used were flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry (F-AAS) using a PerkinElmer 5100PC Spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, MA, USA); inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) using a PerkinElmer Optima 7000DV (PerkinElmer, MA, USA); instrumental neutron activation analysis (INAA) using a SLOWPOKE II research reactor (Atomic Energy of Canada Limited, ON, Canada); and total reflection X-ray fluorescence (TXRF) using a Wobistrax /C223 TXRF spectrometer (Atominstitut der O ¨ sterreichischen Universita ¨ten, Vienna University of Technology, Vienna, Austria). The technique selected for each element was dependent on availability at the institution, detection limit and robustness. Measurements by AAS and ICP-OES were done in triplicate, with the mean results being presented. The uncertainty of the measurements by INAA and TXRF are a function of acquisition time.

Calibration standards for F-AAS and ICP-OES analyses were prepared using Certiprep solutions (SPEX Certiprep, NJ, USA) in 2% HNO3. Deuterium lamp background corrections were performed for AAS. An internal standard containing 0.04 mg/L scandium and 0.2 mg/L yttrium was used for ICP-OES analyses.

For analyses using TXRF, the calibration and internal standards were prepared by serial dilutions of a solution containing 10,000 mg/L (Certiprep solutions) in 5% HNO3. An aliquot of a cobalt (Co) solution was added as an internal standard to achieve a final concentration of 10 mg/L. The samples were then homogenized with a vortex mixer and analyzed after pipetting 10 m L onto a quartz sample carrier/reflector and evaporating the solvent on a hot plate at low power. The samples were irradiated under vacuum for 1000 s at 50 kV and 40 mA in a Wobistrax /C223 TXRF spectrometer (Streli et al., 2004). Blanks were treated identically to the samples.

INAA was performed using the SLOWPOKE-2 nuclear reactor. For the determination of short-lived radioisotopes, approximately 700 mg of sample was weighed out into pre-cleaned double polyethylene bags and heat sealed in pre-cleaned 7 cm 3 polyethylene vials (Lalor et al., 2000). Each sample was irradiated for 3 min at a neutron flux of 5 /C2 10 11 n cm /C0 2 s /C0 1 and allowed decay periods of approximately 5 and 60 min before counting. For intermediate and long lived radioisotopes approximately 1300 mg of sample was weighed out in pre-cleaned polyethylene capsules which were then heat sealed in 7 cm 3 polyethylene vials and irradiated for 4 h at a neutron flux of 1 /C2 10 12 n cm /C0 2 s /C0 1 and allowed a decay period of 4 days and 14 days respectively before counting. For short and intermediate-lived radioisotopes gamma ray spectroscopy was performed using an Ortec High-Purity germanium coaxial gamma photon detector system with an efficiency of 71% and a resolution of 1.8 keV at the 60 Co 1332 keV gamma line. Long lived radioisotopes were counted on an EG&amp;G Ortec High-Purity Germanium detector with an efficiency of 20% and a resolution of 1.9 keV at the 60 Co 1332 keV gamma line.

## 2.1. Quality control

Approximately 10% of the samples were analyzed in duplicate, with the differences between duplicates being less than 15%; at least one reagent blank and a certified reference material were also included in each analysis batch.

Reference materials used were NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology, MD, USA) 8436 -Durum Wheat Flour, NIST 1568a -Rice Flour, NIST 1547 -Peach Leaves, NIST 1570a -Spinach Leaves, and IAEA (International Atomic Energy Agency, Vienna, Austria) 336 -Lichen. Reference material data are presented in Table 1.

For most elements the relative standard deviation of duplicate analyses was less than 10%, with a few (Al, Mo, S, U) having RSD between 10 and 20%; elements with RSDs greater than 20% were those which approached the detection limit of the technique (Ca, V, Ba, Sb, Sm). The agreement between techniques was excellent. Recovery for all reference materials used (were within acceptable limits of /C6 15%. Reference values were not available for B, Hf, Mo, and Ti in any appropriate reference materials. For B analysis by ICP-OES, the data was validated by the use of the internal standards (Sc, Y) and an independent B standard solution; the mean recovery was 95%. For the remaining elements, analyzed by INAA, validation was achieved by the irradiation and counting of single element standards (Certiprep) with identical geometry to that used for samples (Grant et al., 1998) employing the improved relative standardization method (Kennedy and St-Pierre, 1993). Recoveries were 96%, 95% and 98% for Hf, Mo and Ti respectively. The linear range for elements analyzed using F-AAS, ICP-OES and TXRF is low mg/kg to 10 3 mg/kg in the samples. ## 3.1. Toxic elements

## 3.1.1. Aluminum

The range of values for aluminum in white rice was 6.1023.4 mg/kg while the range for brown rice was 7.40-43.0 mg/kg. The mean values for white and brown rice were 9.30 and 14.7 mg/ kg, respectively. The maximum value of 43.0 mg/kg was found in the brown basmati rice brand. The bulk rice had 23.4 mg/kg and a brand of long grained parboiled rice had 15.6 mg/kg. A diet high in rice would not be expected to expose humans to toxic levels of aluminum, but it is of note that the preference in the Jamaican diet is bulk rice which has the second highest quantity of Al in the survey. Aluminum has not been shown to perform any essential function in humans (McDowell, 1992). There has been growing evidence of toxicity however and the World Health Organization revised the Provisional Tolerable Weekly Intake (PTWI) from 7 mg/ kg of body weight to 1 mg/kg of body weight in 2006 based on the conclusions that levels lower than the previous PTWI (Provisional Tolerable Weekly Intake) may affect the developing nervous and reproductive systems (JECFA, 2007). There have been several studies linking an accumulation of aluminum in the brain and Alzheimer's disease but no causal relationship has as yet been established (Frisardi et al., 2010).

The arsenic content of the white rice samples had a range of 0.110-0.487 mg/kg, while the range for brown rice was 0.0820.250 mg/kg. The mean arsenic content of the white rice samples was 0.20 mg/kg, and 0.165 mg/kg for brown rice. The highest sample measured, Roland Arborio rice from Italy, contained 0.49 mg/kg of arsenic. The likely source of arsenic is the water with which the paddies are flooded. Some arsenic compounds are used in herbicides and fungicides. A limit for arsenic in rice has not been established by CODEX; however the WHO has established a PTWI of 0.015 mg/kg bw for inorganic arsenic. Chronic arsenic overexposure results in clinical manifestations in almost every system of the body. Arsenic in groundwater has been well publicized because of the elevated levels found in some countries (Chowdhury et al., 2000). Arsenic in foods occurs predominantly as non-toxic arsenobetaine and arsenocholine which are excreted unchanged from the human body (Ratnaike, 2003). The origin of rice may affect the dominant form of arsenic present and it appears that rice from the United States of America has more dimethyl arsenic acid which may be less of a health risk than rice from Asia and Europe which may have more inorganic arsenic (Zavala et al., 2008).

## 3.1.4. Mercury

The average mercury content of the white rice samples ranged from &lt; 0.015 to &lt; 0.085 mg/kg while the range for brown rice was &lt; 0.024 to &lt; 0.112 mg/kg. The mean values in white rice and brown rice were &lt; 0.049 mg/kg and &lt; 0.062 mg/kg respectively. Mercury has no known essentiality in humans and the toxic effects of mercury compounds and in particular methylmercury are well known. Methylmercury poisoning through the ingestion of fish from industrially contaminated waters was the cause of mass poisoning in Minamata, Japan (Ekino et al., 2007). The symptoms of mercury poisoning range from the neurological to the renal, reproductive and cardiovascular systems and its effect on the nervous system of the developing fetus have been described (McDowell, 1992; Berry, 2008).

## 3.2.2. Cobalt

The cobalt concentration ranged from 0.073 to 0.132 mg/kg for white rice and 0.081 to 0.136 mg/kg for brown rice. The mean concentration was found to be 0.097 mg/kg in white rice and 0.105 mg/kg in brown rice. Cobalt is an essential trace nutrient, particularly as a constituent of vitamin B12 and some metalloproteins. As it is present in small amounts in most foods deficiency is rare; a lack however may lead to pernicious anemia which can be fatal (McDowell, 1992).

The chromium content of the white rice samples ranged from &lt; 0.030 to 0.159 mg/kg; the range for brown rice samples was &lt; 0.030-0.487 mg/kg. Humans require chromium in trace amounts. Its mechanisms in the body are not well defined but it is known to enhance the action of insulin, and appears to aid in the metabolism and storage of protein, carbohydrates and fats. ## 3.2.4. Copper

The copper concentration of white rice samples ranged from &lt; 0.770 to 2.72 mg/kg and the brown rice samples from 1.60 to 4.74 mg/kg. The mean concentration found in white rice was 1.65 mg/kg; in brown rice the mean was 2.96 mg/kg. Copper is essential to humans in small quantities as a key component of redox enzymes and hemocyanin; it is involved in mitochondrial function, cellular metabolism, connective tissue formation, and the absorption, storage and metabolism of iron (NIH). The copper content of rice varies greatly with different cultivars (Yan et al., 2006) and so this could be a function of the variety selected for the local field trials.

There are several recognized factors related to this deficiency; low bioavailability of iron in foods, inability to meet iron demand during growth spurts such as infancy and pregnancy, blood loss in women of childbearing age through menstruation and childbirth, blood losses from parasitic infections and loss from diseases like malaria. Most of the iron in the body is found in heme proteins such as hemoglobin and myoglobin, both involved in oxygen transport. Iron is also found as a constituent of heme enzymes such as cytochromes, catalases and peroxidizes and other non heme compounds (McDowell, 1992). The amount of bioavailable iron in rice and other staple plants is low, and the phytic acid present in rice may add to this lack of bioavailability (Glahn et al., 2002). This is a likely contributor to the global iron deficiency problem. The polishing of rice may remove phytic acid and polyphenols, and enrichment may be an important tool in combating iron deficiency (Liang, 2007).

## 3.2.6. Magnesium

Magnesium concentrations in white rice ranged from 176 to 562 mg/kg, and 340 to 1450 mg/kg in brown rice. The mean concentration found in white rice was 371 mg/kg and the mean in brown rice was 1205 mg/kg. Bulk rice had a value of 381 mg/kg and as such may not be an adequate source of dietary magnesium, but several types of Jamaican foods do contain significant levels of magnesium (Howe et al., 2005). Magnesium toxicity whether by dietary intake or parenteral administration is improbable as excessive magnesium is excreted (Rude, 1998). Magnesium

deficiency in animals and humans however, is well known and described in literature. Symptoms range from growth retardation, nausea, muscle weakness, tetany and this deficiency may affect cardiac function (McDowell, 1992). It was thought the typical westernized diet provided adequate magnesium; however a significant amount of the US population may be falling short of recommended intake amounts (Rude, 1998). This may suggest that a tendency toward deficiency is more likely than previously estimated. According to the NIH, a cup of cooked brown rice contains approximately 80 mg of magnesium, which would be about 20% of RDA.

## 3.2.7. Manganese

The manganese concentrations in white rice samples ranged from 5.02 to 17.1 mg/kg; the range for brown rice was 5.1943.5 mg/kg. The mean concentration of manganese in the white rice samples analyzed was 10.5 mg/kg and 26.5 mg/kg in brown rice samples, with Roland Black Japonica rice having the highest levels. Brown rice is known as a good source of manganese and several of the samples had manganese levels in excess of 30 mg/ kg. Manganese is essential to human beings. It can function as an enzyme activator for several enzymes and as a component of some metalloenzymes. It is also important for bone growth, carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. Because of its ubiquity in foods, deficiency is unlikely in an adequate diet. Its toxicity, however, is also well documented, but is unlikely from dietary exposure except under special circumstances (McDowell, 1992; Crossgrove and Zheng, 2004). Although there is no established recommended daily allowance for manganese it is estimated that adult males and females require 2.3 and 1.8 mg per day respectively. An upper limit of 11 mg per day for adults has been determined (NIH).

## 3.2.8. Molybdenum

The range of molybdenum values found in white rice samples was 0.438-1.62 mg/kg and the range for brown rice samples was 0.447-1.20 mg/kg. The mean values for white rice and brown rice were 0.790 and 0.770 mg/kg respectively. The highest value was found in the Mahatma long grain enriched rice. The bulk rice most consumed by Jamaicans had a value of 0.48 mg/kg. Molybdenum is an essential trace nutrient. In humans it plays an important role in the regulation of calcium, magnesium and copper metabolism. It is biologically active as a co factor to several enzymes as well as the oxidation of sulfite and the formation of uric acid (McDowell, 1992). Because of the relatively low daily requirements of this nutrient (RDA, 45 m g/day for adults) and high tolerance (up to 10 mg/day) deficiency and toxicity from balanced dietary intake is unlikely.

## 3.2.9. Phosphorus

Phosphorus in the white rice samples ranged from 335 to 2591 mg/kg, and from 931 to 4339 mg/kg in brown rice. The mean total phosphorus in the white rice samples analyzed was 1203 mg/ kg; the mean in the brown rice samples was 3361 mg/kg. Not surprisingly the highest result overall was obtained in a sample of brown rice. Also of note is the black japonica variety, which was found to contain 4137 mg/kg of phosphorus. Several brands of brown rice were found to have levels closer to the maximum value observed in this study.

Phosphorus, as phosphates, is among the most abundant of mineral elements in the human body. Deficiencies in phosphorus nutrition have historically been seen in rickets and osteomalacia (which may also be caused by an inadequacy in calcium and/or vitamin D) as well as other bone symptoms and skeletal deformities. It does not appear that the typical adult diet is deficient in phosphorus. Of great importance is the presence of phosphorus in its storage form phytic acid. Phytic acid is considered an anti-nutrient because of its binding affinity for several elements of nutritional consequence such as iron, zinc and calcium. About 80% of phosphorus in rice is stored as phytic acid (Mohammed, 2008). About 50% of the mineral content is retained in the bran. This is removed when polishing rice. It is for this reason that brown rice would have more phosphorus and consequently more phytic acid. In cases where rice is an important source of calories the already low mineral content may be further reduced by the relatively high levels of phytic acid.

## 3.2.10. Potassium

The potassium content of white rice samples was found to range from 465 to 1659 mg/kg while the range for brown rice samples was 833-3097 mg/kg. The mean for white rice was 913 mg/kg and 2157 mg/kg for brown rice. The average for white rice content is somewhat lower than that quoted in other publications while the mean for brown rice was similar (Mohammed and Spyrou, 2009; Phuong et al., 1999). Roland Black Japonica rice had the highest concentration of potassium while the lowest concentration was found in Mahatma Jasmine rice. Potassium is the third most abundant mineral in the human body and is essential to human life. Potassium is the dominant intracellular cation and functions as a regulator in the acid-base and the osmotic balance of the cell. Potassium plays an important role in muscle contraction and nerve impulse transmission through the movement of potassium and sodium ions across voltage gated ion channels during action potential. Potassium is also known as a cofactor in many enzymes involved in protein synthesis, energy transfer and the storage of carbohydrates for use as fuel in muscles (McDowell, 1992; Schmidt-Nielsen, 1997).

## 3.2.11. Selenium

Selenium values for white rice ranged from &lt; 0.028 to 0.236 mg/ kg and those for brown rice from &lt; 0.058 to 0.204 mg/kg. Seventysix percent of the samples had detectable levels of selenium with a mean value of 0.108 mg/kg in white rice and 0.131 mg/kg in brown rice. It has been shown that staples grown in soils with high Se bioavailability will have relatively high selenium content, with the converse being the case in soils with low selenium content (IPCS, 1987). The ability of selenium to bind toxic heavy metals, especially mercury, mitigates its various pathological effects, especially those related to the brain of the developing fetus, has been known for some time if not always highlighted in studies on risk of mercury exposure (Berry, 2008).

## 3.2.12. Sodium

The sodium content of the white rice samples had a range of 1.65-11.4 mg/kg while the brown rice samples ranged from 5.07 to 48.1 mg/kg. The mean concentration of sodium in the white rice samples was 6.00 mg/kg; the mean for brown rice is 15.1 mg/kg. The highest sodium was found in Uncle Ben Whole Grain Brown Rice. Sodium is essential to humans, used for regulating blood pressure and blood volume, and critical for muscle and nerve function. Excess dietary sodium can lead to hypertension, and may have more serious implications in people with congestive heart failure, cirrhosis or renal disease. Sodium occurs naturally in most foods but most dietary sodium is added, particularly to processed foods. By some estimates, as much as 75% of dietary sodium comes from processed foods (United States Department of Health and Human Services, HHS, 2005), and purchased meals may also be high in sodium. The Recommended Daily Intake for sodium is 2300 mg/day for healthy adults and 1500 mg/day for persons with hypertension and other health considerations. Salt is always added to the local dish rice and peas during the cooking process (Samuda et al., 1998a,b) and many households also add salt and sometimes

butter or margarine to plain rice during or after cooking. The sodium content of such a widely consumed staple as rice becomes of utmost importance. Even at the upper end of the range, the sodium content of rice is relatively low and without addition of salt would not contribute significantly to dietary sodium intake.

## 3.2.13. Sulfur

The range of sulfur values for white rice was 912-1328 mg/kg, while for brown rice it was 1118-1671 mg/kg. The mean concentration of sulfur found in white rice was 1131 mg/kg. The mean in brown rice was 1291 mg/kg. Sulfur is a minor constituent of fats, body fluids, and skeletal minerals and is essential to life. It is a component of many proteins due to its presence in the amino acids methionine and cysteine and is present in biotin and thiamine. Hemoglobin also contains sulfur (McDowell, 1992). Dietary intake guidelines have not been established for sulfur but it is generally accepted that a diet sufficient in protein provides sufficient sulfur, about 850 mg/day.

## 3.2.14. Zinc

Zinc concentrations in white rice samples ranged from 12.6 to 19.0 mg/kg; concentrations in brown rice samples ranged from 16.6 to 24.5 mg/kg. The mean zinc content of white rice samples analyzed was 15.6 mg/kg and the mean value in brown rice was 20.2 mg/kg. It is likely that the relatively low concentrations in rice are a function of zinc deficient soils or soils with properties that make this element biologically unavailable for uptake by the plant. It has been reported that 50% of soils cultivated with cereals are low in bioavailable zinc (Cakmak, 2008). It is known that rice and other cereals contain phytic acid, a chelating agent of several trace elements including zinc. The low Zn concentrations found in the rice samples tested and the potential for further reduction in the bioavailability of zinc because of phytate requires special attention because of the prevalence of zinc deficiency in the developing world and the high consumption of this staple in Jamaica. The WHO lists zinc deficiency as the 5th leading cause of illness and disease in low income countries, with some estimates suggesting that up to a third of the world's population is affected (Hotz and Brown, 2004).

## 3.3. Other elements

Boron, barium, hafnium, thorium and titanium were undetected in all samples. Silver was detected in the brown rice sample from Jamaica and was below detection limit for all other samples; the silver content of the sample was still quite low at 1.3 m g/kg. Only one sample contained cerium above detection limit.

Many of the elements detected in the rice samples such as some rare earth elements (cerium, europium, lanthanum and samarium), scandium and antimony appear to have no definitive physiological function in the human body. Some, such as scandium are considered non-toxic while others like antimony and some of its compounds can be toxic. It is possible however that emerging research may change the known essentiality of these trace elements.

## 3.3.1. Bromine

The bromine content of the rice samples varied greatly, from 0.273 to 113 mg/kg in white rice and 0.467 to 19.97 mg/kg in brown rice. The mean concentration was 10.7 mg/kg in white rice and 3.39 mg/kg in brown rice. The sample containing 113 mg/kg was Kendel Jasmine Rice; most of the samples contain less than 1 mg/kg bromine, making this value remarkable. The second highest sample contained 29.2 mg/kg and is also jasmine rice; however no inferences may be drawn based on variety or origin.

## 3.3.2. Rubidium

The range of rubidium in white rice was 0.70-9.10 mg/kg with a mean of 4.75 mg/kg; for brown rice the range was 2.13-16.4 mg/kg with a mean of 7.6 mg/kg. The Jamaican field trial composite sample was 2.92 mg/kg. The essentiality of rubidium has not been determined although evidence suggests a number of roles in various human systems. As an alkali metal rubidium has the same oxidative state as potassium and has been suggested, is exchangeable with potassium in biological systems because of this chemical similarity. Interest in rubidium's role as an element with antidepressant properties has come to the fore with the discovery of deficiencies in uremic patients undergoing dialysis (Canavese et al., 2008; Nielsen, 2003). Although deficiency or excess states in humans have yet to be described, a tentative requirement of 400 m g/day has been suggested. Cereals are not considered to be significant sources of rubidium but the ubiquity of the element in foodstuffs, especially raw fruits, vegetables and meat make deficiency unlikely (Anke et al., 2005). The rubidium found in the rice samples analyzed suggests that the contribution would be significant from this source. More research is necessary to determine the biological role of this element.

## 3.3.3. Strontium

Strontium ranged from 1.48 to 2.73 mg/kg and 2.01 to 4.19 mg/ kg in white rice and brown rice samples respectively. Strontium is an alkali earth metal with chemical similarity to calcium. Because of this it can exchange with calcium in bone (IPCS, 2010). Studies suggest that strontium has bone density increasing properties (Neuprez and Reginster, 2008) but at higher concentrations this element appears to impair normal bone development (Verberckmoes et al., 2003). Strontium has no clearly established biological role and no daily intake has been established as a deficiency state has yet to be identified. Strontium is also generally regarded as non toxic (Bonjour et al., 2009).

## 3.3.4. Vanadium

The range of vanadium concentrations in white rice was &lt; 0.017-0.072 mg/kg; for brown rice the range was &lt; 0.0210.091 mg/kg. Vanadium was detected in approximated 73% of the samples analyzed with a mean value of 0.037 mg/kg in white rice and 0.057 mg/kg in brown rice. The highest overall value of 0.091 mg/kg was found in the Kendel Brown Gold Basmati Rice. Studies have suggested that vanadium has a role in the regulation of several enzymes and also as a cofactor in hormone, lipid and glucose metabolism as well as the building of bones and teeth. However the essentiality of vanadium has as yet not been established. Vanadium compounds are regarded as toxic but the generally low concentrations of vanadium found in the foods and the low absorption of vanadium in the gastrointestinal tract means that toxicity from the ingestion of uncontaminated food is unlikely.

Bulk rice provides on average 6% and 8% of the RDI for magnesium for males and females respectively, the same as white rice. Brown rice contributes 20% and 27% and this is in line with NIH estimates of a cup of brown rice providing approximately 20% of the RDI.

Brown rice is a significant source of copper with the average intake accounting for 23% of the RDI. The same holds true for molybdenum, with brown rice providing as much as 122% of the RDI. Copper is found in a variety of foods and its relatively low RDI means that deficiency, although reported, is rare. Deficiency may be as a result of interfering factors affecting bioavailability such as iron deficiency, molybdenum and excessive zinc ingestion (Kumar, 2007). Unpublished data generated at ICENS, showed relatively high blood levels of zinc and this was shown to have implications on the copper status. The symptoms of molybdenum toxicity are generally those of copper deficiency (McDowell, 1992) and so the effect of zinc on copper may require further investigation.

A significant portion of this phosphorus is as the anti-nutrient phytic acid and this has implications for the bioavailability of zinc, iron and manganese.

Rice appears to be a significant source of manganese for the Jamaican population with white rice contributing to 32% and 41% of the Adequate Intake (AI) for males and females respectively; bulk rice contributes 16 and 20% but of note is brown rice which on average supplies 82% of the male AI and 105% of the female AI. Although the upper limit established is 11 mg/day for adults it bears noting that the Jamaican diet has other significant sources of manganese including legumes and leafy vegetables (Howe et al., 2005) and although toxicity by dietary means is still unlikely, deficiency does not appear to be an issue.

The contribution to dietary sodium intake is insignificant, which may be advantageous given the propensity for excess sodium in the modern diet. Jamaica has a point prevalence of hypertension of 30.8% in the 15-and-over age group (Ragoobirsingh et al., 2002) and potassium is known to have an effect on the development and management of hypertension (Sica, 2008). It is therefore noteworthy that rice is not a rich source of potassium with brown rice contributing the most to the RDI at 3.3%.

Zinc and iron deficiencies have been well described in literature and the WHO lists these as leading causes of illness and disease in low income countries. Rice provides between 10 and 18% of the RDI for zinc however, unpublished data generated at ICENS, showed zinc levels in a survey of Jamaican blood to be among the highest in the world for men and women; about 42% above a world mean

presented in a literature review. While this warrants further investigation this may suggest that zinc deficiency is not a concern in the Jamaican diet. The RDI for iron is significantly different for males and females, thus, while males may obtain as much as 20% of their RDI from rice (using average white rice data) the contribution to RDI for females is, at best, only 9%. The relatively high levels of iron in white rice suggested by the mean are likely the result of enrichment and/or parboiling which puts the iron content on par with brown rice values. Although cereals such as rice are generally considered low in iron the values for both brown and white rice are similar to the iron content of locally grown Jamaican foods. Jamaican fruits, legumes, leafy vegetables, root vegetables and other root crops had mean iron levels of 6.7, 30.0, 15.5, 12.9 and 9.6 mg/kg respectively (Howe et al., 2005).

Rice provides between 14 and 17% of the RDI for selenium. Selenium forms complexes with cadmium and mercury (McDowell, 1992) and with the potential for the Jamaican population to be exposed to both cadmium and mercury, selenium in the diet may take on added significance.

No RDI has been established for sulfur, but an adequate intake of 850 mg/day has been suggested. Although no more than 11% of this would come from rice, a diet with sufficient protein should provide adequate sulfur. Organic sulfur compounds have a low degree of toxicity (Parcell, 2002) and since protein is the major source of sulfur a deficiency of this element is likely a protein deficiency.

The daily intake of aluminum from rice is estimated to be highest from bulk rice, with 16.7% of the PTWI for males and 17.9% of the PTWI for females. The estimated contribution from arsenic is highest from the mean concentration for white rice, and is 9.5% of the PTWI for males and 10.2% of the PTWI for females. The greatest contribution from cadmium is 8.4% of the PTWI for males and 9.0% for females, estimated from mean brown rice concentration. The highest estimate for mercury intake is obtained from brown rice, at 11.1% of the PTWI for males and 12.0% for females.

Although none of these numbers exceed the PTWI it is noteworthy that these estimates are only for rice consumption. Contributions to the PTWI of these toxic elements from other food sources and drinking water will need to be considered to give a more robust picture of dietary exposure. The contribution of aluminum from bulk rice is of particular concern as it is the most widely consumed brand of rice in Jamaica. It can be argued that the PTWI estimates for Cd and Hg represent what is likely the maximum exposure based on the detection limits of the technique applied and that the actual intake of these elements may be lower.

## 3.5. Rice from local field trial

Jamaica's Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries has undertaken field trials of rice over the past few years. As recently as January 2009, there were nine varieties of rice being compared for yield, time to maturity and nutritive value. The test sample analyzed for this work represented a composite sample of Prosequisa 4, a Dominican variety, being tested at the Amity Hall farms in the parish of St. Catherine.

The levels of many of the elements in the rice from the local field trial are not remarkable, either in that they would not contribute significantly to dietary intake, or they do not deviate much from the mean values obtained for all samples. Of note however are Ag, Cu and Se; of all the samples analyzed, the Jamaican-grown rice contains the highest levels of these elements. The local rice also contains the second highest levels of Co, Mo and Zn. The lowest levels of As and Br were also detected in this sample. While the high level of Zn found in the local rice cannot at this time be related to the high blood levels found in Jamaica (as it is not yet commercially available), it may be related to the considerable levels of Zn in Jamaican soils, which have a mean Zn content of 205 mg/kg (Lalor, 1996).

For many elements, including S, Ca, P, Zn, Cu, Na and Se, the content of the Jamaican rice was higher than that of white rice samples collected, and higher than the mean value found in brown rice samples in this survey. The experimental Jamaican brown rice had 3705 mg/kg of phosphorus. Although when polishing occurs it can be expected that this number will be reduced significantly it may be prudent to consider phosphorus in prospective growing soils and the impact of phytic acid on mineral nutrition when selecting growing areas.

For several elements, including Br, As, Cr, Mo, Fe, Mn and Cd, the content of the Jamaican rice sample was markedly lower than the mean values found in both white and brown rice. The most profound discrepancy is seen in the Br value for white rice. Fe in the Jamaican rice sample may be lower due to enrichment of the commercial rice samples. The lower values for As and Cd are encouraging given that some Jamaican soils are known to have naturally elevated levels of these elements (Lalor, 1996), but until further sampling from field trials can be done, no definite conclusions can be drawn.

## 4. Conclusions

While rice is considered to be one of the major staples consumed in Jamaica, the actual contribution to elemental nutrition may not be significant; especially considering that the most widely consumed variety is white bulk rice which is not enriched.

It is noteworthy that the brown rice analyzed contains, on average, significantly greater concentrations of, Cr, Cu, K, Mg, Mn, Na, P, and Zn in comparison with the white rice samples. It is also of note that the mean Ca and Fe values are lower for brown rice than for white, and is likely due to the number of white rice samples which are enriched and/or parboiled.

Average per capita consumption of rice by Jamaicans is estimated at 71.2 g/day (FAOSTAT, 2007). This is far less than in major rice consuming countries (Kennedy et al., 2002) such as Vietnam (464.7 g/day), Laos (470.0 g/day) and Myanmar (577.9 g/ day). Rice in the Jamaican diet is said to contribute 11.0% of dietary energy, 9.2% of protein and 1.5% of fat. Additionally, unlike in high consuming countries (particularly those in Asia), rice is not eaten at every meal and indeed may only be eaten at one meal per day, as ground provisions and other cereals or cereal products are also commonly consumed, and meats and vegetables likely play a more significant role in the Jamaican diet than in those of other major rice consuming nations. Notwithstanding, the pending shift toward rice cultivation in Jamaica warrants further study of the elemental content of the locally grown product, not only from a nutritive standpoint, but also because of the natural enrichment of heavy metals in some Jamaican soils. If rice production is to commence on a scale large enough to impact Jamaica's reliance on the importation of this staple this is among the criteria that should be used to select appropriate soils for cultivation.
