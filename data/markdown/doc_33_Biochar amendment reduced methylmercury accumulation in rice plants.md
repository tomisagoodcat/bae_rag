## a b s t r a c t

There is growing concern about methylmercury (MeHg) accumulation in rice grains and thus enhanced dietary exposure to MeHg in Asian countries. Here, we explored the possibility of reducing grain MeHg levels by biochar amendment, and the underlying mechanisms. Pot (i.e., rice cultivation in biochar amended soils) and batch experiments (i.e., incubation of amended soils under laboratory conditions) were carried out, to investigate MeHg dynamics (i.e., MeHg production, partitioning and phytoavailability in paddy soils, and MeHg uptake by rice) under biochar amendment (1-4% of soil mass). We demonstrate for the first time that biochar amendment could evidently reduce grain MeHg levels (49-92%).

The declines could be attributed to the combined effects of: (1) increased soil MeHg concentrations, probably explained by the release of sulfate from biochar and thus enhanced microbial production of MeHg (e.g., by sulfate-reducing bacteria), (2) MeHg immobilization in soils, facilitated by the large surface areas and high organosulfur content of biochar, and (3) biodilution of MeHg in rice grains, due to the increased grain biomass under biochar amendment (35-79%). These observations together with mechanistic explanations improve understanding of MeHg dynamics in soil-rice systems, and support the possibility of reducing MeHg phytoaccumulation under biochar amendment.

## 1. Introduction

Mercury (Hg) contamination in soils is a widespread problem in the world [1-3]. In China, 1.6% of soils exceed the limit for Hg, according to a recent national-scale survey by the Ministry of Environmental Protection and the Ministry of Land and Resources of China [4]. Meanwhile, large areas of Hg-contaminated soils are still being used for rice cultivation in many Asian countries including China [5], due to lack of arable lands. Recent studies revealed that rice, in addition to fish and shellfish, could also be an important route of human exposure to methylmercury (MeHg), which is highly toxic and bioaccumulative [6]. Therefore, there is a great demand for reducing MeHg phytoavailability and its accumulation in rice, a staple food for approximately 40% of the world's population [7].

Recently, there is increasing interest in using biochar to reduce bioavailability of organic contaminants and their uptake by plants, partly due to its relatively high specific surface area [8,9], as well as its ability to enhance microbial activity and thus facilitate degradation of organic contaminants in soils [10]. Meanwhile, more attentions have been paid to the potential effects of biochar amendment on metal biogeochemistry and phytoavailability (e.g., Cd, Cu, Pb and Zn [11-13]). Unfortunately for MeHg, potential effects of biochar amendment on MeHg phytoavailability and bioaccumulation are less understood. According to a latest research, biochar may contain elevated levels of organosulfur (due to transformation of sulfate to organosulfur under pyrolysis conditions [14]), with high affinity to Hg [15]. Besides, decreased porewater MeHg levels [9] and reduced MeHg mobility in oxic soils [16] under biochar amendment have been reported in recent laboratory experiments, which could be partly attributed to the strong binding between biochar and MeHg. Those studies provide initial evidence that biochar could immobilize MeHg in soils. However, little is known about the potential effects of biochar amendment on MeHg dynamics (e.g., production, phytoavailability and biological uptake of MeHg) in soil-rice systems. Soil-rice systems are characterized by relatively low but fluctuating Eh [17], elevated dissolved organic matter levels due to root excretion [18], massive production of MeHg in soil [18,19], as well as iron reduction and thus formation of iron plaque on rice root [20]. All these processes could largely affect MeHg biogeochemistry and thus interactions between MeHg and biochar, which warrant investigation.

Meanwhile, incorporating crop straw into soils has recently been reported to be an economic and effective way to reduce mobility of metals [16,21,22], possibly due to the high affinity of metals to organic matter within straw. For MeHg, not only its mobility but also MeHg production (i.e., transformation of inorganic Hg to MeHg) could be affected by input of organic matter via soil amendment ([22-24]). Therefore, it would be interesting to explore the co-effects of straw and straw-derived biochar on MeHg biogeochemistry and its accumulation in rice.

To answer these questions, pot experiments were carried out, and MeHg phytoavailability in soils during rice growth was assessed by using ammonium thiosulfate extraction method, which has recently been validated to predict MeHg phytoavailability [25]. Relatively low application rates of biochar (1-4%, within the range of those used in normal practice, e.g., 1-10%, [26-28]) were used in this study to explore the possibility of remediating Hg-contaminated farmland at relatively low cost. Meanwhile, batch experiments were carried out to further explore the underlying mechanisms of modified MeHg phytoavailability under biochar amendment: soils were monitored for (1) net MeHg production, (2) sulfate concentrations and activities of sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB, responsible for MeHg production in soils [29]), as well as (3) MeHg partitioning between solid and dissolved phases.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Soil and biochar

Mercury-contaminated soil used in this study was collected from a drained rice paddy field in Xunyang mercury mining area (Shanxi Province, China), which is one of the biggest mercury mining areas in China [30]. The soil was air-dried, ground, and sieved through a 2 mm mesh before use. Rice straw was collected from Yixing City (Jiangsu Province, China), where the soil is free of any known mercury contamination. Rice straw-derived biochar was produced from the Yixing rice straw by pyrolysis at 600 ◦ C for 2 h under oxygen-limited condition [31]. Both rice straw and biochar were oven-dried, ground into fine powders and then sieved through 0.28 mm mesh before being incorporated into soils. Concentrations of total Hg, MeHg or particulate organic carbon (POC) in the soil, rice straw, and biochar are shown in Table 1. The use of low-mercury rice straw and biochar would minimize input of straw-contained Hg into soils and its subsequent uptake by rice, which was not the focus of this study.

## 2.2. Pot experiments

A total of 15 pots (3 replicates for each treatment) in 5 treatments were used in this study (Table 2). Different amounts of biochar (10 or 40 g/kg soil, i.e., 1% or 4%) or rice straw (10 g/kg soil, i.e., 1%) was incorporated into the soil on May 24, 2014. The application rates were comparable with those used in previous studies or farming practices (biochar: [26-28]; straw: [16,24,32,33]). The amended soils were mixed thoroughly and placed into polypropylene pots (2 kg soil/pot). Soil in each pot was added with basic fertilizers (128 mg N/kg soil as CO(NH2)2, 82 mg P/kg and 103 mg K/kg soil as KH2PO4) and saturated with deionized water. On June 17, 2014 (herein referred to as day 0), rice seedlings of Wufengyou2168 ( indica , 30-day-old, cultivated in lowHg soil) were transplanted into the soils (two seedlings/pot, and three replicate pots/treatment). Rice seedlings were transplanted into soils 24 days after biochar/straw amendment, which are commonly used for straw return in normal practice, to avoid potential harmful effects of straw decomposition on plant growth. All pots were placed in a greenhouse at ambient temperature (15-38 ◦ C). During the whole period of rice cultivation, the soils were flooded with deionized water (around 3 cm above soil surface).

The soil MeHg levels, MeHg phytoavailability, as well as dissolved organic carbon (DOC) levels in soils were quantified at different time through the rice growing period: On day 0 (i.e., the day of seedling transplantation), 56 (i.e., beginning of heading stage), 121 (i.e., the day of rice harvest), soils (2-10 cm) were sampled into polypropylene centrifuge tubes, immediately vacuum-packed and transferred to the laboratory in an ice box within 2 h. The tubes were then placed into an anaerobic glove box filled with nitrogen gas, where the sampled soils were randomly separated into subsamples for determining DOC concentrations (calcium chloride extraction method [34], TOC-5000A, Shimadzu, Japan), MeHg levels (automatic Brooks Rand model III MMHg analyzer, Brooks Rand, USA, details in Supplementary data, text) and MeHg phytoavailability (assessed by extraction rate of MeHg by ammonium thiosulfate [25], details in Supplementary data, text). Soil pH was also measured on day 42, 56 and 121 by a pH analyzer (FJA-6, Nanjing Chuan-Di instrument &amp; equipment, China). At harvest, rice tissues including grain, root and straw (stem and leaf) were collected, washed by deionized water, oven-dried, weighed, ground into fine powders and determined for MeHg concentrations (Supplementary data, text). Iron plaque on root was removed with dithionite-citrate-bicarbonate based on a published method [35].

## 2.3. Batch experiments

Batch experiments were conducted to further explore the dynamics of MeHg in biochar amended soil under anoxic conditions. Specifically, air-dried soil (the same soil like that used in pot experiments) was added into centrifuge tubes (10 g soil/tube). Then the soils were amended or not with biochar (0.4 g, i.e., 4% w/w) and/or sodium lactate. Amendment of lactate (an electron donor, 5 mM in final concentration) could enhance microbial activities [36] (including those responsible for MeHg production), which may help explore mechanisms of modified MeHg production under biochar amendment. Consequently, totally three treatments (two replicates per treatment) were used in the batch experiments, including 'blank' (without lactate amendment), 'control' (with lactate amendment) and '+ Biochar' (soil amended with biochar and lactate). Afterwards, each tube was added with deionized water to a final volume of 30 mL, sealed and then incubated in a growth chamber (dark, 28 ◦ C), simulating environment of flooded rice paddy soil. Sealed tubes were shaken twice per day during incubation.

On day 0 (i.e., 4 h after biochar/lactate amendment), 2, 6, 10 and 20, tubes were centrifuged (2900 g ) to separate the soil particles and overlying water. Redox potential (Eh) and pH (by HQ30d multiparameter meters, HACH, USA), total dissolved Fe (by Varian 710-ES ICP-OES, Agilent, USA), sulfate and MeHg in the overlying water, as well as MeHg levels in particles were determined. Moreover, at the end of incubation (i.e., on day 20), populations of SRB in soils were estimated based on the Most Probable Number (MPN) method [37], which has been commonly used to quantify SRB in soils and sediments [37-40]. Partitioning coefficients (Kd ) of MeHg, calculated as Concentrations of MeHg in the soil/Concentrations of MeHg in the overlying water, were then calculated. A total of 30 tubes, i.e, 3 treatments × 2 replicates × 5 time points, were used in the batch experiments.

Surface areas, cation exchange capacity (CEC), as well as element composition of soil and biochar are compared in Table 1. Surface area and CEC of the biochar were 52.8 m 2 /g and 16.5 cmol/kg, respectively, which were evidently higher than those of the soil. Meanwhile, the biochar contained relatively high level of sulfur (2565 ± 417 mg/kg) and sulfur in the form of sulfate accounted for approximately 17% of total sulfur in the biochar (Table 1). The peak at 1634 cm -1 may represent C C or C O stretching vibration associated with ketones, carboxylic C components [42]. The peak around 1050 cm -1 could suggest presence of C O and C -O -C groups [43].

Biomasses of rice grain, straw and root, as well whole plants (i.e., grain + straw + root) are shown in Supplementary data, Fig. S4A. Biomass of grain was generally higher in biochar amended treatments. Co-application of straw with biochar did not further increase grain biomass.

## 4. Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate for the first time that biochar amendment could evidently reduce MeHg accumulation in rice plants (grain: 49-92%, straw: 28-83%, and root: 29-61%, compared to the control, Fig. 4). The reduced grain MeHg levels under biochar amendment could be mainly attributed to the combined effects of: (1) the enhanced net MeHg production in soils, (2) immobilization of MeHg in soils, and (3) the increased grain biomass and thus biodilution of MeHg in rice grain.

## 4.1. Biochar amendment enhanced net MeHg production in soils

Results of our pot and batch experiments (Figs. 1 and 3) first demonstrate that biochar amendment could evidently enhance net MeHg production in rice paddy soils. Changes in microbial activities and thus microbial production of MeHg could be responsible for the enhanced soil MeHg concentrations. Our recent study [44] revealed that SRB played a key role in MeHg production in the examined soil, while methanogen was much less important. Therefore, sulfate levels, which could greatly control activities of SRB [45], would subsequently affect MeHg production in the soils. Addition of lactate into the batch reactors could enhance microbial activities. Therefore, the 20-day batch experiment may simulate, on an accelerated time-scale, dynamics of soil parameters (e.g., Eh, sulfate and dissolved Fe) and MeHg in flooded soils (i.e., rice paddy soils). Results of the batch experiments indicate evidently higher sulfate levels in biochar amended soils than the control. This could be partly attributed to the high dissolvable sulfate levels in the biochar (1291 mg/kg, compared to 75 mg/kg in the examined soil, Table 1). Consequently, the enhanced dissolvable sulfate levels in biochar amended soils (e.g., ∼ 90% higher than the control on day 0, Fig. 2C) would enhance SRB activities and favor microbial production of MeHg by SRB. In fact, we did find that the density of SRB in biochar amended soils was 15 times higher than that in the control, coinciding with the higher rates of sulfate reduction under biochar amendment within the 20 day incubation time (especially from day 10 to day 20). This would further support the possibility that biochar-induced increase in SRB activity could be partly responsible for the enhanced soil MeHg levels. Microbial studies, e.g., determination of other mercury methylation microbes (e.g., iron reducing bacteria) as well as hgcA / hgcB gene in biochar amended soils, are needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms of the enhanced soil MeHg levels under biochar amendment.

Positive effects of co-application of rice straw with biochar on soil MeHg levels (Fig. 1A) could be attributed to the supply of labile carbon to mercury methylating microbes, which has been documented in our previous studies [22,24] and were further supported by the results of the batch experiments (Fig. 3A): The addition of labile carbon source (i.e., lactate) resulted in evidently higher net MeHg production in the control (added with lactate) than the blank (without addition). Besides, soil MeHg levels increased with time in the control while remained relatively constant in the blank. These observations suggest that labile carbon sources, as important energy for the respiration of bacteria [46], could be a limiting factor for the microbial production of MeHg in the examined soil. In fact, it has been reported that high levels of sugars, alcohols, low-molecular-weight organic acids and phenolic aldehydes could be released during straw decomposition [47]. Besides, rice straw contains water soluble contents, e.g., glucose and some low-molecular-weight proteins [48], which could possibly serve as labile carbon sources for bacteria. Therefore, the straw amendment-induced increases in soil MeHg levels could possibly be attributed to the enhanced DOC levels (Supplementary data, Fig. S2A), resulting from dissolution and/or microbial decomposition of organic matter in rice straw.

## 4.2. Immobilization of MeHg in soils under biochar amendment

Extractable MeHg by (NH4)2S2O3 could be indicative of phytoavailable MeHg in paddy soils, which has been validated in this (Supplementary data, Fig. S5) and previous studies [25]. The method is based on the assumption that (1) MeHg desorption from soil particles and its subsequent uptake by plants is a dynamic process; (2) MeHg desorption in the rhizosphere could be the limiting step of MeHg bioaccumulation. Therefore, uptake of MeHg by rice in the pot experiments may not affect phytoavailable MeHg quantified in this study. Despite of the increases in soil MeHg concentrations under biochar amendment, significantly lower phytoavailable MeHg levels were found in biochar amended soils (Supplementary data, Fig. S3), resulting in evidently lower MeHg accumulation in rice plants (Fig. 4).

The decline in MeHg phytoavailability, indicated by the reduced MeHg extraction rates and decreased MeHg bioaccumulation factors (Fig. 1B and Fig. S4B), could be attributed to the immobilization of MeHg in biochar amended soils and thus reduced partitioning of MeHg in pore water. Results of our batch experiments indicate higher MeHg Kd under biochar amendment (Fig. 3C), resulting in significant decreases in MeHg levels in overlying water (Fig. 3B). The immobilization of MeHg under biochar amendment could possibly be due to the relatively high surface area of the biochar (52.8 m 2 /g), comparable with those reported for rice straw-derived biochar (10.7-115.9 m 2 /g [49]). This could facilitate sorption of MeHg with biochar [9]. Besides, it has been reported that high levels of organosulfur groups are contained in biochar. For example, 0-23% (pyrolysis at 500 ◦ C) or 73% (pyrolysis at 800 ◦ C) of sulfur in corn straw-derived biochar was detected to be organosulfur by XANES analysis [14]. Considering that sulfate and organosulfur are the main species of sulfur in biochar [14], we roughly estimated that organosulfur could be up to 83% of total sulfur in the biochar used in this study ((Total sulfur-Sulfur in the form of sulfate)/Total sulfur × 100%, Table 1). The estimated percentage of organosulfur is comparable with that reported for oak biochar produced at the same temperature (i.e., 600 ◦ C) [14]. In view of the high affinity of MeHg to organosulfur groups [15], we may expect that biochar-sorbed MeHg could hardly desorb from soil particles and be taken up by rice root. Determination of sulfur species (especially organosulfur) in rice straw-derived biochar are needed to further confirm the proposed mechanism. Meanwhile, high levels of reduced sulfur species (e.g., iron sulfides) could be produced under anoxic conditions in flooded paddy soils [50]. The reduced sulfur species could possibly be accumulated in biochar, e.g., through adsorption, and thus immobilize MeHg, in view of the high affinity of MeHg to reduced sulfur species [51]. In addition, the binding of MeHg with functional groups within biochar, e.g., OH, C C and C O detected by FTIR in this study, may also be partly responsible for the immobilization of MeHg in biochar. The mechanisms of MeHg association with biochar and thus the low phytoavailability of MeHg are far from clear at this stage, and should be further explored in future studies.

It has been reported that biochar amendment may affect availability of Cu and Pb by increasing soil pH [11]. However, pH values were rather comparable between amended soils and the control in this study (Supplementary data, Fig. S2B), which may be due to the relatively low application rates of biochar in this study (1% or 4%). Another possibility is that rice cultivation and thus root exudation could decrease soil pH [18,52], which may neutralize the effects of biochar amendment on soil pH. Therefore, changes in pH may not be a key reason for the MeHg immobilization under biochar amendment in this study. Besides, DOC levels were also documented to have great effects on Hg mobility in soils [15]. Considering the relatively large specific surface area of biochar used in this study (52.8 m 2 /g), biochar may adsorb DOM. This would possibly affect MeHg availability in amended soils. However, DOC levels were less variable among treatments during the rice growth period (except on day 0) and no significant relationship was found between MeHg extraction rates and DOC levels (figure not shown), suggesting that variations in DOC levels should not be mainly responsible for the observed reduction in MeHg phytoavailability. The relatively higher DOC levels on day 0 in straw amended soils could be attributed to decomposition or dissolution of organic matter in straw ([22,24]). Meanwhile, the less variable DOC levels during the

rice growth period (e.g., on day 56 and 121) may be due to root exudation, which would enhance DOC levels in all treatments.

In this study, co-application of straw had insignificant effects on plant MeHg levels (Fig. 4). This could be attributed to the less evident effects of straw amendment on MeHg phytoavailability: MeHg extraction rates were not significantly different between biochar amended and biochar + straw treatments (except on day 56). The more evident effects of straw-derived biochar than straw on MeHg phytoavailability could possibly be due to the transformation of sulfate into organosulfur during biochar preparation and thus stronger binding between MeHg and biochar.

## 4.3. Increased grain biomass under biochar amendment

In addition to the decreased phytoavailable MeHg levels in soils, increased grain biomass could also partly account for the reduced MeHg concentrations in grain. Increases in grain yield under biochar amendment have been widely reported [53-55], which could possibly be because that amended biochar could improve soil properties [53], increase microbial activities and thus nutrient cycling [56], enhance N efficiency [55,57] and P availability [58], or adsorb phytotoxic compounds [59]. In this study, biochar amendment would increase organic carbon, sulfate and nitrogen contents in soils (Table 1), which may account for the increased grain biomass. Consequently, the higher grain biomass (35-79% higher than the control) in biochar amended treatments would also be partly responsible for the lower grain MeHg concentrations, through biodiluting MeHg in grain.

## 5. Conclusions

To conclude, biochar amendment resulted in moderate increases in soil MeHg levels but would immobilize MeHg in soils. Consequently, MeHg uptake and bioaccumulation decreased sharply under biochar amendment. The reduced MeHg uptake by rice plants together with the increases in grain biomass resulted in decreased grain MeHg levels under biochar amendment, as observed in this study. Co-application of straw with biochar would further increase soil MeHg concentrations but reduce MeHg extraction rates at the earlier stage of rice growth, and finally had minor effects on phytoavailable MeHg levels and MeHg accumulation in rice grain. As to our knowledge, this is the first time evident effects of biochar amendment on MeHg accumulation in rice are reported.

Though we provided initial evidence of the inhibitory effects of biochar on MeHg bioaccumulation, more studies are needed to fully understand the underlying mechanisms. For instance, it would be interesting to quantify the organosulfur levels and other Hg binding sites in biochar produced from different plants, considering the large variations in sulfur contents in different plants [14]. Besides, systematic investigations on key factors (e.g., fluctuating soil Eh due to water management or wheat-rice rotation) controlling MeHg dynamics under biochar amendment are necessary, before the conclusions of this study could be extrapolated to other soil-plant systems. While the reduced MeHg phytoavailability under biochar amendment could be the key process triggering the declines in grain MeHg levels, potential changes in plant physiology (e.g., enhanced xylem sap flow [53], and enhanced root growth [60]) due to biochar amendment may also play a role in MeHg uptake by plants and its translocation within plants. More studies are necessary to investigate changes in plant physiology under biochar amendment and their effects on MeHg accumulation in rice grain. Meanwhile, more field studies are needed before conclusions drawn in this study could be extrapolated to rice field fields, in view of the potential differences in cultivation conditions (e.g., soil types, and field management).
