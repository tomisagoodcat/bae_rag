## A B S T R A C T

Keywords:

Biochar  amendment  has  the  potential  to  reduce  methylmercury  (MeHg)  uptake  by  rice  grains  in  soil-rice ecosystem. Considering that sulfur can strongly bind Hg and thus reduce its bioavailability, S-modified biochar has been used to immobilize Hg in soils. However, whether natural S-enriched biochar can further reduce Hg and MeHg phytoavailability remains  unknown. Moreover,  the  rhizosphere  is  one  of  the  most  important microbial hotspots regulating the pollutant dynamics in terrestrial ecosystems. Therefore, it is of greater practical significance to examine the impact of biochar amendment on MeHg production and phytoavailability in the rhizosphere versus nonrhizosphere. Here, by conducting a pot experiment, we evaluated the efficacy of biochar derived from sulfur-enriched oilseed rape straw to reduce MeHg accumulation in rice. The results demonstrated that: (1) biochar-induced enhancement of chloride ion and sulfate levels in the overlying water and pore water facilitate microbial methylation of Hg and thus MeHg production in rhizosphere soil. (2) biochar amendment increased rhizosphere soil sulfur content and humic acid-like substances, strengthening MeHg binding to soil, and thus reducing grain MeHg levels by 47% -75%. Our results highlight the necessity to applying natural sulfurrich biochar accompanied with exogenous sulfur to further reduce MeHg phytoavailability.

## 1. Introduction

Methylmercury (MeHg) has received global attention due to its high toxicity  and strong bioaccumulation (Mergler et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2019b).  The  paddy  field  is  a  typical  wetland  ecosystem,  where  the long-term flooded anaerobic environment is highly susceptible to microbial methylation of mercury (Hg) (Ullrich et al., 2001; Zhao et al., 2016b), and moreover, the rice plant accumulates the maximum MeHg compared with most of the crops (Qiu et al., 2008; Meng et al., 2011). Therefore,  rice  cultivated  in  Hg-contaminated  rice  fields  would  be enriched  in  MeHg  (Meng  et  al.,  2014).  Several  studies  have  demonstrated that in countries and regions where rice is the staple food, consumption of MeHg-contaminated rice is the key route for exposure to MeHg risks in humans (Feng et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2010). Consequently,  the  issues  of  microbial  methylation  of  Hg  in  rice  fields  and MeHg accumulation in rice have always received extensive attention.

Clarifying the factors influencing the microbial methylation of Hg in paddy soils  would  be  of  great  significance  in  controlling  MeHg  production.  Previous  studies  have  demonstrated  that  the  process  of  Hg microbial  methylation  in  rice  fields  is  affected  by  several  factors, including  soil  properties  (such  as  pH,  Eh,  soil  organic  matter,  and moisture) (Beckers et al., 2019a; Wang et al., 2020b; Zhao et al., 2020b), Hg speciation (Bishop et al.,  1998;  Liu  et  al.,  2019a),  soil  microbial abundance/activity  (Ullrich  et  al.,  2001;  Liu  et  al.,  2018b),  and  the speciation of iron and sulfur (S) in soil (Zhong et al., 2018). Among these factors, the effect of soil S species on the microbial methylation process of Hg is of paramount importance (Shu et al., 2016a; Xing et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021). Some studies have reported that under reducing conditions, reduced S (S 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; ) can decrease the mobility and bioavailability of Hg by complexing with Hg to form insoluble HgS (Beckers and Rinklebe,  2017),  thereby  slowing  down  the  process  of  Hg  microbial methylation (Liu et al., 2018a). In contrast, inorganic sulfate (SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; ) can serve as an electron acceptor for Hg-methylating microbes (sulfate-reducing  bacteria  (SRB))  and  promote  the  Hg  methylation process (Gilmour et al., 1992), thereby boosting MeHg content in paddy soils (Zhao et al., 2016a). The reduced S in the soil also has an important effect on MeHg mobility (Qian et al., 2002; Liem-Nguyen et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021). Studies have also observed that thiol ligands bound to natural organic matter have a strong ability to bind MeHg, which plays a significantly important role in the complexation of MeHg in the soil and sediment (Karlsson and Skyllberg, 2003; Liem-Nguyen et al., 2017). This finding shows that the reduced organic S in the soil can reduce  the  solubility  of  soil  MeHg  and  its  migration  to  the  solution phase.

The remediation cost of soil Hg pollution is expensive. A number of studies have shown that in-situ immobilization is an efficient and largescale applicable method to better control the risks of Hg and MeHg in farmlands (Gilmour et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019; Xing et al., 2019). Carbon-based materials are eco-friendly immobilization agents which have  been  applied  to  paddy  field  to  treat  Hg  pollution.  It  has  been proved that the reduced mobility of Hg in paddy field might be attributed to Hg speciation transformation coupled to the reduction of sulfoxide to reduced sulfur species (S 0 ) induced by nanoactivated carbon amendment (Wang et al., 2020a). Particularly, biochar has been widely used to immobilize Hg and remediate Hg-polluted soil due to its relatively low cost and its high efficiency for Hg immobilization (Liu et al., 2017; O ' Connor et al., 2018). Studies have demonstrated that biochar modified with the element S can more effectively immobilize soil Hg and reduce its mobility (O ' Connor et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2020a), indicating that S in biochar is crucial for biochar to stabilize Hg in soil. Moreover,  some  studies  have  shown  that  implementation  of  biochar increases the MeHg content in rice fields, which implies that biochar can promote the microbial methylation of Hg in the soil (Shu et al., 2016b; Zhang et al., 2019). However, some studies have also demonstrated that biochar has little effect on Hg methylation in rice fields (Xing et al., 2020). Nevertheless, all these studies have found that biochar amendment significantly reduced the absorption and accumulation of MeHg by rice. The following three reasons have been speculated for this finding: first, biochar can reduce the phytoavailability of MeHg through mechanism wherein a higher amount of organic S in biochar complexes with MeHg and immobilizes it; second, the MeHg content in rice decreases due to the biological dilution effect, because biochar amendment can improve the rice biomass (Shu et al., 2016b; Xing et al., 2020; Wang et  al.,  2020c);  and  third,  biochar  can  immobilize  MeHg  via  surface binding and pore diffusion, thereby reducing the MeHg content in pore water (Gomez-Eyles et al., 2013). To summarize, S in biochar can reduce not  only  the  mobility  of  Hg  in  soil  but  also  the  mobility  of  MeHg. However, because thiol S compound and large amounts of element S can harm crops, biochar modified with thiol S compound or large amounts of element S (13.04% S in biochar) is not suitable for the remediation of MeHg pollution in paddy fields. Therefore, the use of S-rich biochar by selecting  feedstocks  rich  in  S  content  can  be  ecologically  friendly  in reducing MeHg mobility and controlling MeHg uptake by rice in paddy fields. Although previous work provided evidence for the reduction of MeHg levels in rice induced by biochar amendment, whether natural sulfur-rich biochar can more effectively reduce uptake of MeHg by rice has not been studied. Clearly, the impact of natural sulfur-rich biochar treatment  on  MeHg  bioaccumulation  warrant  investigation  given  its importance  to  further  elucidating  the  complex  interaction  between various sulfur species and MeHg, and testing the practicality of natural sulfur-rich biochar amendment as a solution to mitigate the risk of MeHg uptake  by  rice  from  Hg-contaminated  soils  at  locations  around  the world.

The absorption of nutrients and pollutants by plants is closely related to  the  rhizosphere  soil  environment,  and  a  special  rhizosphere  environment has a significant impact on the microbial methylation of Hg and

MeHg uptake by rice roots (Wang et al., 2015). Studies have found that the reduction of iron in the rhizosphere soil of paddy fields was higher, and  the  MeHg  content  was  increased  in  comparison  to  that  in  nonrhizosphere  soil.  This  observation  may  be  due  to  the  reduction  of rhizosphere  iron  that  can  promote  the  Hg  methylation  process  by dissimilatory iron -reducing bacteria (Warner et al., 2003). Moreover, due to the flow of free oxygen from the rice roots to the rhizosphere, the redox  of  rhizosphere  soil  is  higher  than  that  of  nonrhizosphere  soil, which  can  affect  the  Hg  microbial  methylation  process  (Sun  et  al., 2011). Collectively, the factors influencing the microbial methylation process  of  Hg  in  the  rhizosphere  soil  environment  are  extremely different  from  those  in  the  nonrhizosphere  soil  environment,  and moreover, the mobility of MeHg in the rhizosphere soil is directly related to the absorption of MeHg by plants. However, only very few studies have explored the impact of biochar amendment on MeHg mobility and phytoavailability in the rhizosphere soil.

Therefore, we speculate that the increased S in feedstocks can not only inhibit the promotion of Hg methylation by biochar but also further reduce the absorption of MeHg by rice. Therefore, we conducted a rice pot  experiment  using  biochars  with  varied  S  contents  to  treat  Hgcontaminated paddy soil, with the following aims: (1) to test the practicality of S-rich biochar amendment as a potential tool to further reduce the microbial methylation of Hg in rhizosphere soil and the immobilization of produced MeHg, (2) to examine the effect of various S species contained in biochar on the microbial methylation process of Hg, and (3) to further clarify the interaction mechanism between biochar/soil S and MeHg.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Preparation of biochar

Common oilseed rape  straw  (S  content,  0.31%  (w/w))  and  highsulfur oilseed rape straw (S content, 0.76% (w/w)) were used to prepare biochar. Common oilseed rape straw was purchased online, and high-sulfur  oilseed  rape  straw  was  collected  from  an  agricultural experimental  station  in  Anqing  City,  Anhui  Province,  owned  by  the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Sulfur fertilizer (ammonium sulfate) was applied to elevate the S content in rape. The collected oilseed rape straw was air-dried, crushed, and then passed through a 0.5-mm sieve. Pretreated  oilseed  rape  straw  was  pyrolyzed  with  a  vacuum  annealing furnace (KJ-T1200-S10440-LK, Kejia Electric Furnace, China) under N2 atmosphere to produce biochar. The pyrolysis temperature program was as follows: heating rate of 10 ◦ C/min, final temperature of 600 ◦ C for 2 h, and N2  flow as 2 -4 ml min GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 . The produced biochar was ground and passed through a 0.5-mm sieve, washed several times with deionized water to remove the salts and reduce its pH to approximately 8.5, and then air-dried for later use. The two produced biochar samples were designated as low-sulfur oilseed rape straw-derived biochar (LSBC) and high-sulfur oilseed rape straw-derived biochar (HSBC), respectively.

## 2.2. Pot experiment

After removing large gravels and plant debris from the soil, it was air-dried, crushed, passed through a 2-mm sieve, and used for pot experiment.

The pot experiment was conducted in the greenhouse of the Chinese Research Academy of Environmental Sciences. The temperature was set at 30 ◦ C ± 2 ◦ C during the day and 25 ◦ C ± 2 ◦ C during the night, with air humidity maintained at 40% -60%. In the late stage of rice heading (day 129), the rice field was drained and no water was added anymore.

## 2.3. Sample collection and pretreatment

Overlying water, soil pore water, and rhizosphere soil were collected respectively in the typical rice growth stages, including seedling stage (day 0), tillering stage (day 60), and heading stage (day 129). Soil pore water and rhizosphere soil were also collected at rice maturity stage (day 153). The whole plant sample was collected on the very last day when the rice matured.

Soil pore water was sampled using a soil moisture sampler (Macro Rhizon, Rhizosphere, Netherlands). Both the soil pore water samples and overlying water samples were divided into two parts; one part was used to determine the dissolved organic carbon (DOC) content and its spectroscopic properties, and the other part was used to determine the related anion contents (Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; and SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; ).

The rhizosphere soil was sampled carefully from the rhizosphere box, ensuring to leave the root away from the soil sample. Two to three spots in the rhizosphere box were collected and mixed together to generate a soil  sample.  The  collected  rhizosphere  soil  samples  were  air-dried, ground to powder, passed through a 100-mesh sieve, and divided into two portions. One portion  was used to determine the basic  physicochemical properties and for the characterization of chemical functional groups, and the other portion was used to determine the MeHg content.

The plant samples were separated as four rice tissues, viz., grain, stem, leaf, and root. The roots, stems, and leaves were first washed with tap water to remove silt and dirt and then rinsed three times with ultrapure water, after which the plant tissues were oven-dried at 40 ◦ C to a constant weight. The pretreated rice tissues were weighed, crushed with a crusher and ground to powder, and then passed through a 100-mesh sieve.  Rice  grains  were  dried  at  40 ◦ C  to  constant  weight,  dehulled manually, weighed, ground to powder, and then passed through a 100mesh sieve before analysis.

## 2.4. Analytical methods

The BET surface areas of biochar and Hg-contaminated paddy soil were  measured  using  a  mercury  porosimeter  (AutoPore  IV,  Micromeritics, USA). C, N, H, S, and organic carbon (OC) contents of biochar, Hg-contaminated paddy soil, and rhizosphere soil samples were determined  using  an  elemental  analyzer  (vario  EL  cube,  Elementar,  Germany). The Hg content in biochar, all soil samples, and rice plant tissues was  measured  using  a  direct  mercury  analyzer  (DMA-80,  Milestone, Italy)  following  the  method  of  National  environmental  protection standard of The People ' s Republic of China (HJ 923-2017), the instrument detection limit is 0.2 ng g GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 . The pH and Eh of biochar and all soil samples were measured using a pH meter (S210-K, METTLER TOLEDO, Switzerland) at a solid -liquid ratio of 1:2.5.

The chemical functional groups in the rhizosphere soil were characterized using an infrared spectrometer (TENSON II, Bruker, Germany). Before analysis, soil samples and potassium bromide were mixed and pressed at a ratio of 1:150 to prepare thin slices. The scanning wavelength was 4000 -400 cm GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 ,  with  a  resolution  of  4  cm GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 and a scan number of 32. The infrared spectra were processed using the OPUS 7.5 software (Bruker, Germany). Water-extractable organic matter (WEOM) in the rhizosphere soils was extracted using ultrapure water at a solid--liquid ratio of 1:10 according to previously published methods (Jones and Willett, 2006). Briefly, 1 g of soil and 10 ml of deionized water were mixed and shaken for 1 h, centrifuged for 10 min, and then the supernatant was collected and filtered using a 0.45μ m filter membrane to obtain WEOM solution. The WEOM solutions were then characterized using TENSON II infrared spectrometer (Bruker, Germany), which was equipped with a Zn/Se plate; the spectrum analysis was the same as that used for the rhizosphere soil.

The  DOC  contents  in  soil  pore  water  and  overlying  water  were measured using an OC analyzer (TOC-L, Shimadzu, Japan). The Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; and SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; contents in soil pore water and overlying water were determined by ion chromatography (IC6200B, Anhui Wanyi, China), where SI-52 4E was used as the chromatographic column, and the eluent was 3.6 mM anhydrous sodium carbonate.

MeHg in  the  rhizosphere  soil  was  extracted  using  potassium  hydroxide  (KOH-)-methanol  solution  (Liang  et  al.,  1996).  Specifically, approximately  0.5  g  of  air-dried  rhizosphere  soil  was  weighed  and poured  into a 30-ml  Teflon bottle, after which  2 ml  of  25% KOH-methanol solution  was added,  and  then  the  mixture  was  ultrasonicated for 3 h for digestion. After cooling the digestion solution, 6 ml of HPLC-grade dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) and 1.5 ml of concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl) were added to the solution in sequence. Then, the mixture was shaken for 15 min using a constant temperature oscillator and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min to separate the solution phases. Next, the CH2Cl2 solution at the bottom of the Teflon tube was collected,  transferred  to  a  new  30-ml  Teflon  bottle,  and  accurately weighed. Then, 5 ml of deionized water was added to the CH2Cl2 solution, the mixture was placed in a water bath at 45 ◦ C -50 ◦ C to volatize CH2Cl2, and residual CH2Cl2 was removed with nitrogen blowing. The MeHg content in the remaining water solution was measured by liquid chromatography (LP) (SA-20, TITAN, China) -and an atomic fluorescence  spectrometer  (AFS-933,  TITAN,  China).  The  chromatographic column was a C18 column, the mobile phase for LP was 5% methanol + 0.462%  ammonium  acetate + 0.12%  L-cysteine,  and  the  oxidant, reducing agent, and carrier were deionized water, 2% KBH4 + 0.35% KOH, and 5% nitric acid, respectively. Instrument measurement conditions were as follows: mercury lamp current 40 mA, photomultiplier tube negative high voltage 290 mV, and flow rates of carrier gas and shielding gas 600 and 800 ml/min, respectively.

The MeHg content in various plant tissues (roots, stem, leaves, and grains) was extracted using KOH-methanol solution (Zhang et al., 2010). Specifically, 0.1 g of roots, stems, and leaves and 0.06 g of grains were weighed and taken in a 30-ml Teflon tube, followed by the addition of 2 ml of 25% KOH-methanol solution to digest the solid materials. Analysis of replicates for the soil and plant were conducted and the relative percentage differences between replicates were &lt; 11% and &lt; 7%, respectively.

## 2.6. Statistical analyses

Differences  in  all  variables  among  treatments  were  evaluated  by analysis  of  variance  using  Minitab  18.  Tukey ' s  hypothesis  test  was applied to conduct the post-hoc test with a significance level ( α ) of 0.05. All variables were tested for normal distribution.

At the seedling stage (day 0), the pH value of rhizosphere soil in different treatment groups ranged from 6.51 to 6.86, and it was slightly higher in the biochar treatment groups than that in the CK group. At the rice tillering stage (day 60), the pH values in all four groups generally increased, and the pH of the biochar treatment group was much higher than that of CK (Fig. 2A). The difference of rhizosphere soil pH changes under  biochar  treatments  between  seedling  stage  and  tillering  stage could be attributed to the combination of direct and indirect effects of biochar treatment. Specifically, at the seedling stage, the added alkaline biochar (pH = 8.51 -8.52) directly and slightly increased the pH of the soil. However, the indirect effect of biochar amendment on rhizosphere soil pH gradually came into play, that is, biochar treatment changed the soil  pH  by  affecting  rhizosphere  soil  microenvironment,  resulting  in significant  increases  in  rhizosphere  pH  values  due  to  the  combined direct and indirect effects of biochar amendment. Subsequently, the pH of the rhizosphere soil in all groups dropped to 6.44 -6.99 at the maturity period  (day  157),  which  was  comparable  to  the  initial  pH  values (Fig. 2A). This may be due to the fact that rice roots secreted organic acids  and  lowered  the  pH  of  the  rhizosphere  soil  as  they  gradually developed (Wang et al., 2015). Moreover, the pH of rhizosphere soils with biochar treatments hardly differed from that in the CK group at the rice maturity stage (Fig. 2A). The Eh of rhizosphere soil in all four groups decreased initially and then increased slightly (except for the CK group) and decreased dramatically (Fig. 2B). The initial Eh of the rhizosphere soil ranged from 35.5 to 57.5 mV, but it decreased to a level in the range of GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 26.5 to 4 mV at the maturity stage. At the maturity stage, the Eh values in the H-HSBC and H-LSBC groups were much lower than that in the CK group, whereas the value was comparable between L-HSBC and CK groups (Fig. 2B). The initial total nitrogen (TN) content in the rhizosphere soils was 1.04 -1.32 g kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , which did not change significantly during rice growth. The TN contents in the rhizosphere soil with biochar amendment  were  higher  than  those  without  biochar  amendment, whereas  the  values  were  approximately  similar  among  the  biochar amendment groups (Fig. 2D). The ratio of carbon to nitrogen (C:N) in the rhizosphere soil ranged from 22.7 to 32.9 at day 0, and it was lower with biochar amendment than that in the CK group. However, no obvious differences were detected in C:N values among the four treatment groups from the tillering stage to maturity stage (Fig. 2F). In summary, biochar amendments could significantly increase the TN and TS contents in the rhizosphere  soil  by  14% -35%  and  24% -66%,  respectively,  but  had  little effects on the OC content and C:N ratio.

4.2. The effect of biochar amendments on Hg and MeHg accumulation and transfer in rice

At the initial stage, biochar amendment significantly increased the Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentration in overlying water and soil pore water. By the 60th day,  the  Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentration  in  the  four  treatment  groups  decreased sharply  (Fig.  5C  and  F),  demonstrating  that  biochar  contained  a considerable amount of salt containing Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; , that was released to the soil solution through dissolution, thereby increasing the Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; content in soil pore water and overlying water (Li et al., 2020). Moreover, Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; can increase the mobility of Hg by complexing with Hg and producing soluble Hg-Cl2 complex (Gabriel and Williamson, 2004), thereby promoting the microbial methylation process and causing an increase in MeHg content in the rhizosphere soil. However, the moderate increase in Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; level may not result in the obvious increase in MeHg content in rhizosphere soil, it cannot be excluded that Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; had a positive effect on MeHg production in rhizosphere soil due to the increase of Hg mobility. The variation pattern of SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentration in overlying water and soil pore water was similar to that of Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; (Fig. 5B and E). Briefly, at the initial stage, the SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentrations in overlying water and pore water under biochar amendments were higher than those in the CK group, and then the concentrations decreased sharply in all four treatment groups until the final maturity stage (Fig. 5B and E). In addition, at the maturity stage, the SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentrations in the soil pore water of L-HSBC and H-LSBC groups were significantly higher than those of the CK group (Fig. 5E), which indicated the presence of a large amount of inorganic sulfate  in  biochar.  Moreover,  inorganic  sulfate  in  the  biochar  was gradually released into the liquid phase when biochar was applied to paddy soils, resulting in a significant increase in SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; concentration in overlying water and soil pore water (Zhao et al., 2018). The reason for the decrease in SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; content in overlying water and soil pore water with the growth of rice may be due to its uptake by the rice plant for growth. Meanwhile, at the maturity stage, the MeHg content in the rhizosphere soils  was  enhanced  by  biochar  treatments,  especially  with  H-HSBC treatment, which promoted the maximum MeHg content (Fig. 6). These data indicate that the increased SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; content in the soil liquid phase induced  by  biochar  treatments  may  partly  explain  the  enhanced methylation rate of Hg in the rhizosphere soil, because SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; can promote microbe-mediated MeHg production through two pathways; first, by increasing the activity and abundance of Hg methylator, viz., SRB (Jeremiasion  et  al.,  2006;  Liu  et  al.,  2018b),  and  the  second,  by improving Hg mobility and bioavailability to microbes (Li et al., 2019).

In this study, it was observed that biochar treatment had no significant effect on the OC content of rhizosphere soil (Fig. 2C), indicating that the change in the organic matter content in the rhizosphere soil with biochar amendments was trivial and thus hardly had an effect on Hg  microbial  methylation.  In  addition,  HSBC  and  LSBC  treatment induced opposite changes in functional group contents of soil organic matter, (Fig. 1A), which is not in agreement with the variation of MeHg contents in rhizosphere soils under HSBC and LSBC treatments (Fig. 6A), and this indicated that the effect of sulfur-enriched biochar treatment on the production and concentration of MeHg in rhizosphere soil does not seem to be achieved by changing the functional groups of soil organic matter.  However,  biochar  treatments  increased  the  humic  acid-like components of WEOM but decreased the fulvic acid-like and proteinlike  components  of  WEOM  (Fig.  4),  which  may  contribute  to  the immobilization effect of biochar amendment on rhizosphere soil MeHg, as it has been found that humic acids have a stronger binding capacity for MeHg than fulvic acids due to the fact that humic acids are more refractory and complex in structure than fulvic acids (O ' Driscoll and Evans, 2000). Furthermore, HSBC amendment significantly increased the content of soluble microbial by-products in WEOM (Fig. 4), implying that HSBC amendment enhanced microbial activity, thus promoting the microbial  methylation  process  of  Hg,  and  hence,  the  net  MeHg  production increased in the rhizosphere soil (Fig. 6).

Biochar amendments significantly reduced Hg levels in rice roots and grains (Fig. S1), and HSBC amendment reduced Hg contents in roots the most. The decline of Hg in rice roots and grains under biochar amendment was likely due to the immobilization of Hg by biochar through Hg binding with reduced S species (S 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; ), which might be from the reduction of SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; in pore water and overlying water, whose levels were enhanced by  biochar  amendments  (Fig.  5)  (Beckers  and  Rinklebe,  2017;  Xing et al., 2019).

Biochar  amendment  in  Hg-contaminated  rice  fields  significantly reduced  the  MeHg  content  in  rice  tissues  (roots,  stems,  leaves,  and grains), which is consistent with previous findings (Shu et al., 2016a). The extent of MeHg decline in grains ranged from 47% to 75%, which is lower than the results found in previous studies (Shu et al., 2016a; Wang et al., 2020c). In general, MeHg immobilization in the rhizosphere soil by biochar application may be attributed to some factors. First, biochar can  directly  absorb  MeHg  from  soil  solution  through  pore  diffusion, benefiting  from  its  large  specific  surface  area  and  porous  structures (Table 1) (Gomez-Eyles et al., 2013). Second, it has been shown that biochar contains a higher content of organic sulfur (Cheah et al., 2014), which can strongly bind MeHg, thus reducing its mobility and phytoavailability for the rice plant. Similarly, this study also found that biochar amendment enhanced the TS content in the rhizosphere soil (Fig. 2E), which can corroborate to some extent that elevated S in soil due to biochar S addition consolidates the complexing effect of S with MeHg (Figs.  8  and  9).  Third,  biochar  amendment  increased  the  content  of humic acid-like substances in the rhizosphere WEOM (Figs. 3 and 4), and studies  have  demonstrated  that  humic  acids  can  strongly  bind  Hg (Khwaja et al., 2006). Hence, the increased content of humic acid-like substances in WEOM may reinforce the immobilization effect of biochar on MeHg in the rhizosphere soil by tightly binding with MeHg, thereby decreasing MeHg accumulation in rice.

Furthermore,  because  biochar  promotes  the  growth  and  development of rice, the dry weights of rice plants in the H-HSBC, H-LSBC, and L-HSBC groups at maturity stage were 37.0 ± 1.7, 35.4 ± 0.8, and 32.8 ± 3.4 g, respectively, which are significantly larger than the CK group (29.9 ± 0.3 g). Therefore, due to the biological dilution effect, the MeHg content of rice in the biochar amendment groups at the maturity stage also  decreased  significantly  (Fig.  9).  Moreover,  biochar  amendments enhanced the contents of Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; and SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; in soil pore water and overlying water at the beginning of rice growth, both of which declined sharply at the  tillering  stage  of  rice  (60th  day)  in  all  four  treatment  groups (Fig. 5B -C and 5E-5F). At the tillering stage, the contents of SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; in the overlying water and pore water in the biochar amendment groups were almost similar to those in the CK group, except for the H-HSBC group, in which their values were higher than that in CK group (Fig. 5B and E). These data show that (1) Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; and SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; in the solution are consumed by rice for growth and development, and thus, their contents declined with rice growth (Li et al., 2001; Geilfus, 2018), and (2) biochar amendment can increase rice biomass by providing nutrients that are essential for plant growth and development (Mukherjee and Zimmerman, 2013), and these findings provide evidence for the promotion of rice growth by biochar amendments, especially by sulfur-rich biochar amendment.

MeHg was primarily distributed in roots, stems, and grains, which accounted for 94.2% -96.3% of the total MeHg in the rice plant, and the MeHg content in grains accounted for 18.6% -21.5% of the total content (Fig. 9), indicating that rice grains, roots, and stems have considerable MeHg  accumulation  ability,  whereas  leaves  accumulate  much  less MeHg. This result is slightly different from previous studies, where it was observed that compared with other rice tissues, rice grains have the strongest ability to accumulate MeHg (Meng et al., 2010). This may be due to the fact that MeHg distribution in rice tissues differs in soils with different biogeochemical properties. However, it is commonly agreed that rice grains can tremendously accumulate MeHg.

## 4.3. Implications for remediation

Biochar amendment increased the MeHg content in the rhizosphere soil,  but  the  MeHg  content  in  rice  tissues  (roots,  stems,  leaves,  and grains) decreased significantly. Moreover, compared with L-HSBC, HHSBC enhanced MeHg production in the rhizosphere soil as well as the MeHg/Hg ratio; however, it reduced MeHg accumulation in rice grains. These data implied that application of sulfur-rich biochar amendment was an effective tool to immobilize MeHg and reduce MeHg uptake in rice in Hg-contaminated paddy fields. Moreover, the biochar application rate  should  be  reasonably  increased  considering  the  safety  of  cereal crops  to  maximize  the  immobilization  effect  of  biochar.  Both  MeHg content in rice grains and BAFs and TF values of rice grains were slightly lower in the H-HSBC group than in the H-LSBC group, which indicates that elevated S in feedstocks cannot further strengthen the inhibitive effect of biochar on MeHg accumulation in rice grains as well as MeHg translocation to rice grains. Therefore, to control the risk of MeHg uptake by rice grains in Hg-contaminated paddy field to a larger extent, it is necessary to add exogenous sulfur when deploying biochar amendment. Moreover, biochar stability can be affected by many factors during the aging  of  biochar  in  field  experiments,  thereby,  further  studies  are needed  to  study  the  long-term  immobilization  effect  of  biochar amendments on Hg and MeHg in paddy fields (Yang et al., 2021).

This study mainly investigates in depth the mechanism of the effects of the application of natural sulfur-enriched biochar amendments on the formation of MeHg and its enrichment in rice plants in Hg-contaminated paddy soil. The mass balance of Hg and MeHg in biochar-soil-rice system under  sulfur-enriched  biochar  amendments  should  be  conducted  to provide better guidance for the engineering application of this method in our further research.

## 4.4. Cost-effective analysis of this method

Assuming that the depth of the restored paddy soil is 20 cm based on the typical arable layer thickness of farmland, the soil density is 1.0 g cm GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 3 (Wang et al., 2020a), and the amount of sulfur-enriched biochar amendment applied is 1.2 kg biochar per 100 kg soil (1.2%), then the total application amount of biochar for 1 ha of soil is about 24 tons. And the  rough  capital  cost  of  this  method  for  Hg-contaminated  farmland would be 7200 US$ per hectare soil assuming the average price of biochar is about 300 US$/ton (referring to alibaba.com). Compared with the  average  cost  of  the  existing  techniques  for  heavy  metal-polluted farmland  remediation  (about  40,000  US/ton)  in  China  (Wang  et  al., 2020a), it is economical to use sulfur-enriched biochar amendment to remediate Hg-contaminated paddy soil.

## 5. Conclusions

This  study compared the efficacy of high-sulfur biochar and lowsulfur  biochar  to  remediate  mercury-contaminated  paddy  soils,  and examined the effects of biochars with different sulfur contents on Hg methylation and MeHg accumulation in rice in paddy soils. The results showed that biochar amendments significantly promoted the microbial methylation of Hg in the rhizosphere soil, which may be due to the increased Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; and SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; contents in the rhizosphere soil environment with  biochar  treatments.  Cl GLYPH&lt;0&gt; stimulated  the  microbial  methylation process  by  complexing  with  Hg  and  increasing  its  bioavailability  to methylating  microbes,  and  SO4 2 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; was  beneficial  for  the  microbial methylation process of SRB. In addition, high-sulfur biochar amendment was not effective in further reducing MeHg uptake by rice compared with low-sulfur biochar amendment. However, all biochar amendments obviously reduced the Hg and MeHg contents in rice roots and grains with  the  assistance  of  sulfur  contained  in  biochar.  The  increased  S content in the rhizosphere soil under biochar treatments strengthened MeHg immobilization by complexing with MeHg. Moreover, biochar can bind MeHg through pore diffusion, thus decreasing MeHg uptake and accumulation in rice tissues. Finally, the elevated content of humic acidlike substances in the soil organic matter under biochar amendments reinforced MeHg immobilization and phytoavalability due to the formation of the complex of MeHg and humic acid-like substances.
