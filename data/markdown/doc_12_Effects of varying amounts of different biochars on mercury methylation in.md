## 1. Introduction

TheGlobal Mercury Assessment 2018 report notes that both current and past anthropogenic mercury (Hg) emissions and releases are the major contributors to increased Hg levels and exposure (Global Mercury Assessment 2018, 2019). Rice grains with elevated methylmercury (MeHg) were observed in polluted sites affected by Hg release and emission from mining, industrial activities or wastewater (Horvat et al., 2003; Rothenberg et al., 2014; Hsu-Kim et al., 2018; Feng and Qiu, 2008; Feng et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020), resulting in exposure of local residents to MeHg. In addition, a recent modeling study suggested that rice could be a signi /uniFB01 cant global dietary source of human MeHg exposure, especially in South and Southeast Asia, and this rice-derived risk will increase in Africa, Central Asia, and Europe with the increase in the international rice trade (Liu et al., 2019a). Therefore, mitigation strategies are needed for the remediation of speci /uniFB01 c Hg-contaminated paddy soils, which may be bene /uniFB01 cial for local and regional food security.

Paddy rice /uniFB01 elds facilitate the growth of microbial methylators, which results in the biogeochemical transformation of inorganic mercury compounds Hg(II) to MeHg (Rothenberg et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2016) and the uptake of MeHg by rice grains, as supported by Hg isotopic evidence (Qin et al., 2020). To reduce Hg accumulation in rice, adsorbing agents (e.g., biochar (BC) or nanoactivated carbon) (Shu et al., 2016; Xing et al., 2019; J. Wang et al., 2020) have been developed to mitigate Hg accumulation in rice grains. For example, based on pot experiments, Shu et al. (2016) reported that the rice MeHg concentrations were signi /uniFB01 cantly decreased by 49 -92 % following rice straw-derived BC amendment (1 -4 %, w/w), although BC enhanced MeHg levels in the soil. The addition of sewage sludge-derived BC (5 %, w/w) resulted in a 73.4 % decrease in grain MeHg (Zhang et al., 2019). Similar results in which grain MeHg decreased by 47 -53 % were observed in a /uniFB01 eld experiment with the application of rice shell BC (24 -72 t/ha, which is approximately equal to 1.2 -3.6 %, w/w) (Xing et al., 2020). In addition, the application of 1 -3 % nanoactivated carbon signi /uniFB01 cantly reduced THg bioaccumulation in rice tissues by 47 -63 % (J. Wang et al., 2020). These results have provided initial evidence of the role of BC and activated carbon in reducing MeHg accumulation in rice grains. However, the amount of BC required to achieve these effects is large (1 -5 %, w/w), corresponding to a /uniFB01 eld application rate of approximately 20 -100 t/ha, which is calculated from a depth of 15 cm and a bulk density of 1.3 g/cm 3 , thus raising the issue of cost. Recent studies have shown that a relatively low application rate (0.3 -0.5 %, w/w) of bamboo-derived BC can result in a 56 -88 % reduction in rice grain MeHg (Y. Wang et al., 2020; Y. Wang et al., 2019), and 0.6 % (w/w) BCs derived from the mixture of wheat and rice straw and rice hull resulted in a 50 -80 % reduction in rice grain MeHg (Man et al., 2021). These results suggest that BC is effective in reducing MeHg levels in rice grains even when applied at a low rate. However, the current understanding of the ef /uniFB01 ciency of the relatively low application rate (e.g., &lt; 1 %, w/w) of BCs on reducing MeHg accumulation in rice is limited; thus, more research is required to further assess and develop BC as a high-ef /uniFB01 ciency material for Hg remediation in soils.

BCs can be produced using various feedstocks and pyrolysis methods, which in /uniFB02 uence the physical and chemical properties of BCs (Liu et al., 2015; Xiao et al., 2018). We hypothesize that the sorption properties of BCs toward Hg play a key role in controlling Hg immobilization in soils and sediments. However, the effect of various BCs on the transformation and bioavailability of Hg in soils is extremely complex, and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

The speci /uniFB01 c goals of this study were to (1) investigate the in /uniFB02 uence of different types of BC with different doses on MeHg production and its bioavailability in Hg-contaminated paddy soils by using microcosm experiments and (2) further explore the effect of the relatively low dose of BC amendment on MeHg accumulation in rice grains through pot experiments. The information gained in the present study provides a better understanding of the potential for BC application to mitigate the health risks of rice MeHg exposure.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Soils and biochars

TwoHg-contaminated soils were selected for the microcosm and pot experiments. One soil was collected from paddy /uniFB01 elds near an abandoned mining site, Wukeng, in mining areas in Wanshan, Guizhou Province, China (hereafter referred to as Soilws). The other soil was collected from paddy /uniFB01 elds along the Zhutong River in mining areas of Xunyang, Shanxi Province (hereafter referred to Soilxy). Detailed information on the two Hg mine areas can be found in the literature (Meng et al., 2010; Qiu et al., 2012). Soils were randomly sampled from paddy /uniFB01 elds by the 5-point sampling method, and approximately 70 kg topsoil (0 -20 cm) samples were collected and then transferred to the laboratory. The soils were air-dried, sieved ( ≤ 2 mm), and homogenized for further experiments.

The low costs and accessibility of two straw feedstocks (i.e., corn stalk and wheat straw) and three woody plant feedstocks (i.e., bamboo, oak and poplar) were used to prepare BC (hereafter referred to as CB, WB, BB, OB, and PB, respectively). These raw materials were thoroughly cleaned with tap water, dried in an oven for 48 h (70 °C), and /uniFB01 nally cut into 3 -5 cm pieces. These feedstocks were placed in a crucible (500 mL) with a lid, enclosed in aluminum foil (four layers), and then pyrolyzed at 600 °C for 1 h in a muf /uniFB02 e furnace (heating rate of 10 °C/min). The BCs were ground, sieved ( ≤ 0.28 mm) and homogenized prior to use in the experiments. The detailed characteristics of the soils and BCs are presented in Table 1.

## 2.3. Pot experiments

To further investigate the effects of BCs on reducing MeHg accumulation in rice plants, pot experiments were designed with seven treatments in triplicate (Table 2). BB, CB and WB were used for pot experiments due to their abundance and low-cost feedstocks. Soilxy was amended with BB, CB, and WB at relatively low application rates of 0.3 % and 0.6 % (w/w). The treatments were as follows: (1) no amendment (CK); (2) amendment with BB (0.3BB, 0.6BB); (3) amendment with CB (0.3CB, 0.6CB); and (4) amendment with WB (0.3WB, 0.6WB). Brie /uniFB02 y, plastic pots (14 × 10 × 16 cm, length × width × height) were /uniFB01 lled with 1.6 kg of soil thoroughly homogenized with BCs and basal fertilizers (159 mg/kg N, CO(NH2)2; 98 mg/kg P and 124 mg/kg K, KH2PO4). Two 30-day-old rice seedlings (Wufengyou 2168, indica ) were transplanted into each plastic pot when the mixed soils were /uniFB02 ooded with tap water for 15 days. Rice was cultivated for 126 days from June to October (01/06 -05/10, 2017) under /uniFB02 ooded conditions (tap water ~4 cm above the soil surface) at ambient temperature (16 -40 °C) in a rain shelter greenhouse located at the biological station of East China Normal University in Shanghai.

During rice cultivation, two small cores (1 cm in diameter and 3 cm in length) of rhizosphere soil were sampled from each pot on days 15, 65 and 126 after rice transplantation. At harvest, rice grains, straw (the mixture of stems and leaves) and roots were collected, treated and determined for biomass, THg and MeHg in rice tissues. The details are given in the Supporting Data.

## 2.4. Sample analysis

Thechanges in MeHg bioavailability or phytoavailability in soil affected by BCs were assessed using the ammonium thiosulfate ((NH4)2S2O3) extraction method, as demonstrated in previous studies (Zhu et al., 2015; Shu et al., 2016). The subsamples of soils in the microcosm and pot experiments were lyophilized and extracted with 2 mL of extractant solution

(2.0 g/L (NH4)2S2O3). The fraction of extractable MeHg in the soil, presented as %, was calculated by dividing the (NH4)2S2O3 extraction fraction by the total soil MeHg. During analysis, the solution was spiked with 1 ng/mL (0.2 mL) MeHg quality-control sample (QCS), and the recoveries were 79 -93 % (n = 6). Furthermore, the recoveries of MeHg extraction in soil were 71.6 -134.4 % (relative standard deviation, RSD = 17.2 %, n = 69). The chemical analysis details are presented in the Supporting Data.

Total mercury (THg) in rice tissues (0.01 -0.02 g) and BCs (~0.02 g) was analyzed by a direct Hg analyzer (DMA-80, Milestone, Italy) according to US EPA method 7473. THg in soils (~0.05 g) was digested with aqua regia (HCl/HNO3) and analyzed by cold vapor atomic /uniFB02 uorescence spectrometry (CVAFS, Brooks Rand, USA) according to US EPA method 1631. MeHg in soils, BCs, rice tissues and the extracted solution was digested with 25 % KOH-CH3OH and analyzed by CVAFS (Brooks Rand mode III, USA) according to US EPA method 1630. Standard reference materials, including soil, rice, sediment and /uniFB01 sh protein, QCS, and blanks, were adopted for quality control during analysis. The average recoveries of THg were 105.9 % for rice (RSD = 15.5 %, n = 7) and 92.2 % for soil (RSD = 6.0 %, n = 7). For MeHg determination, 0.1 ng/mL QCS (50 μ L) was measured every 40 samples during analysis, and the recoveries were 86 -97 % (n = 7). The average recoveries of MeHg were 87.4 % for /uniFB01 sh protein (RSD = 2.3 %, n = 5) and 80.2 % for sediment (RSD = 4.6 %, n = 5). Method blanks (n = 3) were included in each sample digestion batch. Detailed information is given in Table S1 in the Supporting Data.

The THg concentrations in Soilws and Soilxy were 41.6 ± 4.5 mg/kg and 31.2 ± 3.4 mg/kg (n = 3), respectively. The MeHg/THg (%MeHg) in air-dried soils was much lower than 0.01 %. The total carbon values were similar in the two soils. The different BCs were characterized by different surface areas, with the largest values in WB. The pH values ranged from 8.6 to 10.7 in water suspension (solid: water = 1:10). THg in BCs is very low, and MeHg in BCs cannot be detected (Table 1).

There are two possible mechanisms for this: (1) The adsorption of Hg(II) onto BC via complexation and precipitation has been proposed to decrease Hg(II) bioavailability for methylating microorganisms (Bussan et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2018a, 2019b; A. O. Wang et al., 2019). (2) BC-induced biogeochemical processes involving the redox elements S and Fe in soil could stabilize Hg(II) through the formation of solid-phase speciation of Hg(II) (e.g., Hg-sul /uniFB01 de minerals and Hg(II) sorbed to FeS (s) minerals) and precipitation on BC and soil particles (Li et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2021). In addition, our results indicated that the effects of BC amendment on net MeHg production in soil varied with BC type (Figs. 1A, 2A) and over time (Fig. 3). A larger reduction in soil MeHg was observed with the addition of BB than with the addition of the other BCs; however, WB exhibited a weak ability to inhibit MeHg net production and even enhanced MeHg production. A signi /uniFB01 cant increase in soil MeHg was found when the doses of WB exceeded 1 % in both 7-day (Fig. 1A) and 14-day incubations (Fig. 2A). Similar results were observed in microcosm and pot studies where BCs derived from rice straw, sewage sludge or a mixture of wheat and rice straw were used with addition levels of 1 -5 % (w/w) (Shu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019; Man et al., 2021), possibly due to the higher abundance of microbial methylators under high levels of BC amendment (Shu et al., 2016; Man et al., 2021). A long-term microcosm study also suggested that the variation in MeHg could be closely related to the microbial community shifts associated with changes in the concentrations of carbon sources and electron acceptors during soil incubation (Liu et al., 2018a). Thus, we propose that the main factors include not only the types of BC but also biogeochemical changes at different time scales during rice cultivation that could in /uniFB02 uence MeHg production following BC amendment; these effects warrant further study.

## 4.2. Biochar amendment reduced the bioavailability of MeHg to rice

Our results clearly showed that the application of BCs signi /uniFB01 cantly decreased the fraction of extractable MeHg in soils in both microcosm and pot experiments (Figs. 1D, 2D, 5B). Furthermore, the decrease in MeHg contents in brown rice and straw (Fig. 4) and the mass of MeHg accumulation in aboveground tissues in the amended treatments (Fig. 5A) showed a declining trend very similar to that of extractable soil MeHg (Fig. 5B). Moreover, MeHg BAFs also signi /uniFB01 cantly decreased in the amended treatments (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, a positive correlation was observed between the extractable MeHg in the soil and MeHg BAFs (r 2 =0.714, p &lt; 0.05, Fig. 6A), which indicates that the decreased extractable MeHg following BC amendment can effectively reduce MeHg bioavailability in soil. These results indicate that the decline in MeHg accumulation in rice could be mainly attributable to the decreased MeHg bioavailability in soil following BC amendment. These results are consistent with previous observations (Shu et al., 2016; Y. Wang et al., 2020) and suggest that the different types of BC can reduce MeHg bioavailability, thereby decreasing MeHg accumulation in rice (Y. Wang et al., 2020; Xing et al., 2020).

4.3. Environmental implications of biochar application in controlling Hg contamination in paddy soils

In the present study, a more effective reduction in rice grain MeHg was observed under a higher application rate (0.6 %, w/w) of BCs (Fig. 4), indicating that a high application rate of BC could further reduce the MeHg contents in rice grains. However, the reduction in MeHg in rice grains appeared to decrease with increasing doses of BC, which was re /uniFB02 ected by the roughly equivalent reductions in rice grain MeHg between the high (1 -5%)andlow (0.3 -0.6 %) application rates (Table 3). The results suggest that the high application rate of BC (such as &gt; 1%)couldhave a limited effect on further decreasing MeHg levels in rice grains. Furthermore, the cost of remediation associated with the high application rate of BC will increase greatly, resulting in more dif /uniFB01 culties in the application of BC in the /uniFB01 eld. A more recent study showed that the current BC production cost ranges between US $200 and US$1500 per ton (Mood et al., 2022), depending on the types of feedstock and pyrolysis methods. Thus, we can make a rough estimate of the cost of BCs for soil remediation. In this study, the bamboo-derived BC exhibited a better capacity for reducing the amount of MeHg in aboveground tissues among the three BCs (Fig. 5A). The low costs and accessibility of bamboo will be bene /uniFB01 cial for BC production. Assuming US$300 -700 per ton for producing, transporting, and applying bamboo-derived BC in the /uniFB01 eld, the cost of application rate of 0.6 % ( /uniFB01 eld application rate of approximately 12 t/ha) would be US$3600 -8400 per hectare. In general, the application of BCs may provide a remedial option for reducing Hg accumulation in rice and hold great promise in mitigating MeHg health risks in paddy soils impacted by Hg. Field studies are needed to develop costeffective soil remediation by using various BCs with a low application rate.
