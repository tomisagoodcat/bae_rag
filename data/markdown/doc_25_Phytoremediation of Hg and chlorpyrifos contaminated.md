## Phytoremediation of Hg and chlorpyrifos contaminated soils using Phaseolus vulgaris L. with biochar, mycorrhizae, and compost amendments

Abstract Anthropogenic  activities,  encompassing vast agricultural and industrial operations around the world, exert substantial pressure on the environment, culminating  in  profound  ecological  impacts.  These activities exacerbate soil contamination problems with pollutants such as mercury (Hg) and chlorpyrifos (CPF) that are notable for their widespread presence and detrimental effects. The objective of this study is to evaluate the phytoremediation potential of Phaseolus vulgaris L. , augmented with various combinations of biochar, mycorrhizal, and compost amendments, as a  sustainable  alternative  for  the  remediation  of  soils contaminated with Hg and CPF. For this purpose, soil from a mining area with mercury contamination has been taken, to which CPF has been added in different concentrations. Then, previously germinated Phaseolus  vulgaris  L. seedlings  with  an  average  height of  10  cm  were  planted. ## Introduction

Anthropogenic activities, particularly agriculture and  industry,  are  causing  significant  environmental issues, notably soil pollution from heavy metals and agrochemicals  (Alengebawy  et  al.,  2021;  Zwolak et  al.,  2019).  This  contamination  alters  soil  composition,  reducing  fertility  and  increasing  vulnerability to  drought,  making  it  unsuitable  for  agriculture  and industrial  use  (Saha  et  al.,  2017).  The  use  of  pesticides, fertilizers, and  industrial  waste  contributes to  soil  degradation,  harming  ecosystems  and  posing health risks to humans (Ahmad et al., 2022; Doula &amp; Sarris, 2016; Nieder et al., 2018). Soil is a vital but nonrenewable resource, impacting environmental, economic, and cultural activities. Its quality must be preserved throughout development projects (Doula &amp; Sarris, 2016; Garcia et al., 2018; Nieder et al., 2018). Soil  quality  is  the  capacity  to  support  productivity, maintain water and air quality, act as a living system, and support health (Doran &amp; Zeiss, 2000). Sustaining

soil  quality  is  essential  for  long-term  human  activities,  economies,  and  national  development  (Bastida et  al.,  2008;  Doran  &amp;  Zeiss,  2000).  The  primary causes  of  soil  pollution  are  human  activity  and  the introduction  of  new  organic  and  inorganic  compounds, such as microorganisms, heavy metals (like mercury,  Hg-),  pesticides  (like  chlorpyrifos,  CPF-), and fertilizers.  As  a  result,  soil  pollution  is  a  growing concern due to its effects on both the environment and human health (Bastida et al., 2008; Doran, 2002; Schoenholtz et al., 2000).

Inorganic pollutants such as Hg are a global concern due to their high concentrations in some ecosystems. For a very long time, the previously mentioned problem  has  continuously  resulted  in  soil  contamination. Atmospheric Hg can be deposited in land or re-emitted  into  the  atmosphere  through  various  processes (Bishop et al., 2020; Cooke et al., 2020). Recognized as a toxic contaminant, Hg does not degrade and is listed among the top ten chemicals of concern (Cruz  et  al.,  2021;  Leudo  et  al.,  2020;  Saldarriaga et  al.,  2023).  Its  volatility  complicates  remediation efforts,  and  its  long  half-life  means  existing  levels can  pose  prolonged  risks  even  after  emissions  are reduced.  Human  activities  have  increased  atmospheric  Hg  by  300-500%,  leading  to  significant  soil contamination,  particularly  in  areas  with  industrial and  mining  history  (Gworek  et  al.,  2020;  Pais  and Jones 1997).

High  concentrations  of  Hg  in  soil,  directly  correlated  with  population  demands,  pose  significant health concerns due to Hg's tendency to bioaccumulate and biomagnify in living organisms. This leads to increased Hg concentrations through the food chain, ultimately entering the human body and causing serious damage (Kim et al., 2016). Organic forms of Hg can  inhibit  enzyme  activity,  disrupt  biological  processes, and generate neurological and cardiovascular diseases  (Zhang,  2017).  Hg  can  also  be  transferred to  fetuses  by  pregnant  women,  affecting  newborn intelligence  and  increasing  the  possibility  of  future diseases  (Zhang,  2017).  Inorganic  Hg  forms  can  be corrosive  and  induce  kidney  toxicity,  neurological symptoms,  insomnia,  headaches,  and  cognitive  and motor  dysfunctions.  While  Hg  toxicity  depends  on the  chemical  form,  with  organic  forms  being  more toxic,  there  is  a  critical  need  for  soil  remediation  to reduce Hg concentrations and mitigate environmental impacts and threats to humanity humanity (Graeme &amp;

Nonetheless, soil concentrations are often two to four orders of magnitude greater in Hg-contaminated locations (Li &amp; Jia, 2018; J. Wang et  al.,  2012).  Although  some  volcanic  and  organic soils, particularly in Canada, may have greater values and  concentrations  may  be  exceedingly  high  in  the proximity  of  industrial  emission  sources,  (KabataPendias &amp; Pendias, 2000) defined a narrower range of 0.05 to 0.3 mg   kg -1 . The data set showed median Hg concentrations  of  0.019  mg    kg -1 and  a  mean  value  of 24 mg   kg -1 .

An additional concern in soil pollution is the introduction of organic compounds, particularly from pesticide use, which includes a variety of chemicals such as insecticides, fungicides, and herbicides (Dar et al., 2019).  Organophosphates  are  among  the  most  commonly used pesticides in agriculture due to their high efficiency.  Initially  developed  as  insecticides  in  the 1930s, these compounds were later adapted as neurotoxins  during  World  War  II  (Chambers  et  al.,  2010; Ecobichon  &amp;  Joy,  1993).  One  significant  organic compound of concern is CPF, widely used since the 1960s  on  various  crops.  CPF  is  moderately  toxic, with a soil half-life ranging from 10 to 120 days (Dar et al., 2019). Its excessive use can lead to soil accumulation, increasing the risk of exposure to harmful health effects for humans (Dhananjayan et al., 2020). Many nations have already seen substantial environmental damage because of the ongoing and excessive use of CPF. According to several studies, this insecticide  has  been  found  in  marine  sediments,  streams, sinkholes, swamps, rivers, urban storm drains, freshwater  lakes,  groundwater,  fog,  rain,  and  air  (Gebremariam  et  al.,  2012;  Gilliom  et  al.,  2006;  Glotfelty et al., 1987; Kolpin et al., 2000; Kuang et al., 2003; Wightwick &amp; Allinson, 2007). Significant health concerns  have  been  raised  by  the  detection  of  this  substance  in  food  samples,  both  liquid  and  solid,  from both urban and rural locations. Indoor soil dust and indoor air have been found to contain CPF at values of  up  to  15.1  μg    kg -1 and  0.4  μg    m -3 ,  respectively (Gebremariam  et  al.,  2012).  In  children,  the  major

pathways of exposure to CPF were thought to be inhalation,  cutaneous  absorption,  and  oral  consumption. It has also been found in the urine of these children, with  a  concentration  of  117  ng    kg -1 (Fenske  et  al., 2002). According to other authors, CPF has also been found in household dust at amounts varying from 100 to 400 μg   kg -1 (Fenske et al., 2002). A different study found CPF in solid meals at concentrations of up to 263 ng   day -1 (Clayton et al., 2003).

Pests and illnesses are a persistent barrier to agricultural production, and while the world's food supply  is  expected  to  increase  over  the  coming  years to  satisfy  demand  from  an  expanding  population,  a new  solution  is  currently  needed  in  the  application of  chemical  pesticides  such  as  CPF,  which  are  persistent and degrade soils and put food at risk (Foong et  al.,  2020;  HEAL,  2019).  CPF  presents  serious health  risks,  particularly  to  agricultural  workers  and consumers of contaminated food. Acute toxicity can lead  to  symptoms  such  as  headaches,  nausea,  and convulsions,  with  severe  cases  potentially  resulting in  death  (Nandi  et  al.,  2022).  CPF's  rapid  absorption  into  the  bloodstream  necessitates  early  diagnosis  within 24-48 h post-exposure. Its inhibition of a central brain enzyme makes it particularly harmful to neuro-endocrine development in unborn and newborn children, with individuals having pre-existing conditions being more vulnerable (Nandi et al., 2022). The health  impacts  of  CPF  exposure  vary  based  on  age, genetic predisposition, and exposure type. Key at-risk groups include chemical producers, farmers applying CPF, and consumers unaware of pesticide residues in their food. These exposures can cause both acute and chronic toxicity, highlighting the urgent need for soil remediation  efforts  to  mitigate  these  dangers  (Eaton et al., 2008; Saunders et al., 2012).

Given the pressing necessity for a viable remedial strategy for these increasing concentrations of Hg and CPF,  biological  treatments  such  as  bioremediation and  phytoremediation  have  been  explored  to  reduce soil  contamination.  Phytoremediation  has  garnered extensive  attention  and  research  due  to  its  ability  to harness  plant  and  soil  microbial  interactions,  effectively  diminishing  contaminant  concentrations  and mitigating  their  toxic  impacts  on  the  environment. Regarded  as  a  cost-efficient  environmental  restoration method, it stands as an alternative to more intrusive engineering approaches, which often disrupt soil integrity and resilience. However, given the dynamic and multifaceted nature of plant-environment interactions, influenced by processes such as growth, reproduction,  and  photosynthesis,  these  factors  must  be carefully  assessed  when  considering  a  plant's  suitability  for  phytoremediation  purposes  (Deng  et  al., 2024;  Hassan  et  al.,  2024).  Recent  research  has focused on improving phytoremediation effectiveness through soil amendments like biochar, compost, and mycorrhizae.  Biochar  enhances  soil  structure,  water retention, and contaminant adsorption, reducing their availability  for  plant  uptake  (Lehmann  et  al.,  2011). Compost adds organic matter and nutrients, boosting microbial  activity  and  soil  fertility,  which  promotes plant growth and pollutant removal (Hagemann et al., 2017;  López  et  al.,  2023)..  Mycorrhizal  fungi  form symbiotic  relationships  with  plant  roots,  improving nutrient  uptake  and  potentially  aiding  in  the  degradation of organic pollutants (Chen et al., 2022). Integrating  these  amendments  can  significantly  enhance the efficiency of pollutant removal by plants.

Phaseolus  vulgaris  L ,  the  common  bean,  shows promise for phytoremediation due to its adaptability and ability to accumulate heavy metals and degrade organic  pollutants  (Jha  et  al.,  2017;  Priya  et  al., 2023). Its symbiotic relationship with nitrogen-fixing bacteria further enhances its potential for remediating nutrient-poor contaminated soils. Incorporating  biochar,  compost,  and  mycorrhizae  can significantly  improve  the  remediation  efficiency  of Phaseolus  vulgaris  L .  These  amendments  enhance soil fertility and structure, fostering optimal growth conditions and mitigating the toxic effects of Hg and CPF contamination through adsorption and microbial  activity  (Acosta-Luque  et  al.,  2022;  BecerraAgudelo  et  al.,  2022;  Leudo  et  al.,  2020;  Serrano et  al.,  2023a,  2023b).  Mycorrhizal  fungi  augment the  plant's  root  system,  facilitating  nutrient  and water uptake, while also enhancing the degradation of  organic  pollutants  in  the  rhizosphere  (Azaizeh et al., 2011; Leudo et al., 2020; Nanjundappa et al., 2019). This integrated approach leverages the combined  benefits  of  plant-microbe  interactions  and soil  amendments,  resulting  in  a  holistic  and  efficient  strategy  for  remediating  soils  contaminated with Hg and CPF. By leveraging the inherent capabilities of Phaseolus vulgaris L. in conjunction with these  amendments,  this  integrated  approach  offers a sustainable and environmentally friendly solution to  the  pressing  challenges  posed  by  soil  pollution,

thereby contributing to the restoration and preservation of ecosystem health and functionality. The aim of  this  study  was  to  evaluate  the  phytoremediation potential of Phaseolus vulgaris L. (common bean), enhanced by various combinations of biochar, mycorrhizal, and compost amendments, as a sustainable alternative for the remediation of soils contaminated with Hg and CPF. For this purpose, some physicochemical  properties  have  been  measured,  in  addition to CPF and Hg, to determine if the amendments favor  the  bioavailability  of  the  contaminants  to  be absorbed by common bean. Similarly, morphological  changes  and  physiological  impacts  induced  by the presence of these contaminants in common bean plants  have  been  measured.  Finally,  the  efficacy of  the  three  amendments  and  their  combination  in the remediation of CPF and Hg present in soils and the effects that these could have on a plant such as common bean was compared.  Finally,  it  addresses the  opportunity  to  implement  these  methods  particularly  in  mining  and  agricultural  areas  across Colombia that are afflicted by similar levels of contamination  to  foster  environmental  sustainability and promote restauration of soil and environmental resilience.

## Materials and methods

## Materials

The  study  was  divided  in  four  parts:  (i)  Preparation of the soil, (ii) Seeding and sample collection, (iii) Physicochemical analysis and morphological evaluation of the plant, and (iv) Evaluating the effectiveness  of  phytoremediation.  The  seeds  were Phaseolus  vulgaris L.  certified  to  have  a  purity of  98%  and  a  germination  rate  of  80%  (Semicol,  Bogotá,  Colombia).  After  being  cleaned  in  a 10% KOH solution and deionized water, the seeds were planted  in  a  seedbed  and  allowed  to  grow  to an average height of 5 cm. The soil, sourced from the  area  in  the  municipality  of  Segovia,  Colombia,  underwent  characterization  as  detailed  below. ## Methods

The  study  has  been  developed  in  two  stages,  a  first one of CPF contamination and soil stabilization from a  mining  process,  in  addition  to  the  germination  of common bean seeds. The second stage involved transplanting  the  seedlings  and  monitoring  for  45  days with  periodic  observations  every  15  days.  After  the initial  preparation  phase,  which  included  soil  sieving and sampling without contamination, the soil and contaminants  were  prepared,  and  biochar  was  activated. Subsequently, in the experimental setup phase, measurements of seedlings, planting, and initial analysis of uncontaminated soil as a reference were carried out.

Day  0  of  the  experiment  was  the  time  when  the common bean was transplanted into the rhizoboxes. From this moment on, a follow-up every 15 days of soil evaluation was conducted. On day 15, a comprehensive analysis of the soil was performed, focusing on parameters such as pH, conductivity, and the availability of Hg and CPF. On days 30 and 45, additional soil  analyses  were  conducted,  expanding  the  assessment  to  include  physicochemical  parameters,  and reinforcing  the  observation  of  the  availability  of  Hg and CPF. Additionally, on day 45, a detailed analysis of the plant was included, examining aspects such as Hg availability, weight, length of the aerial and root parts, as well as the presence of seeds.

These sequential stages allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of the treatments applied to  the  soil  and  their  influence  on  plant  development over time. The methods used are explained along the section for further comprehension.

The municipality is part of a mining district of more than 9000 hectares dedicated to the extraction and production of gold. These high-grade mines have been in continuous operation for more than 150 years and, during that time, have produced more than 5 million ounces of gold (Fig. 1).

## Physicochemical properties analysis

After  transplanting  the  beans  into  the  rhizoboxes on  days  15,  30,  and  45,  soil  samples  were  taken, approximately 10 g weighed in a beaker using a precision  balance,  for  the  analysis  of  pH  and  electrical conductivity (EC). Subsequently, 20 ml of deionized water were added to the soil in the beaker and mixed for  5  min.  The  sample  was  then  allowed  to  rest  for 10 min. After the allotted time, the pH and conductivity  of  each  sample  were  measured  and  registered using the pH-meter and conductivity meter (ASTM, 2019;  ISO,  1994).  Moisture  was  determined  by  the ASTM-D2030 method, in which the sample was dried for 24 h at 105 ºC. The water holding capacity (WHC)

Hg and CPF contaminant analysis

Chlorpyrifos extraction and GC-MS analysis Using the  solid-liquid  extraction  of  non-volatile  and  semi-

volatile  organic  chemicals  from  solid  matrices,  such as soils, the EPA 3550C method was applied to extract chlorpyrifos  from  soil  samples.  This  was  accomplished by using the ultrasound-assisted method suggested by Correa Zuluaga et al. (2018), which comprised the subsequent steps: 5 g of sample and 15 ml of  a  50:50  dichloromethane:acetone  mixture  were manually agitated in a centrifuge tube for ten seconds, then the mixture was vortexed for 30 s; the mixture was then sonicated for 20 min; and finally, the extract was separated from the sample by centrifugation for five minutes at 4000 rpm. These three steps are performed twice.

For  analyte  detection,  an  Agilent  Technologies 6890N  gas  chromatograph  coupled  to  an  Agilent Technologies 5975 VL  MSD  single  quadruple mass  detector  and  automatic  injector  and  an  Agilent  Technologies 7683B injector and an HP5-MS chromatographic column (30 m, 250 μm, 0.5 μm) were used. The injection mode used has been splitless; 1 μL of the extract was injected, and the injection  port  was  at  a  temperature  of  250  °C.  During the analysis, helium was used as a carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL   min -1 .  The initial temperature of the chromatographic oven was 100 °C and  was  increased  at  a  rate  of  8  °C/min  up  to 230 °C and maintained for 10 min. Then a post run was performed at 300 °C for 5 min to eliminate any residual substance.

Heavy metals For the analysis of Hg, two types of analysis were performed: a bioavailability analysis was  performed  on  the  soil,  and  a  total  Hg  analysis  was  performed  on  the  plant  after  harvesting. 3  g  of  homogenized  soil  were  accurately  weighed into  a  120  mL  amber  flask.  Subsequently,  60  mL of  0.01  M    CaCl 2 solution  were  added  to  the  flask (López &amp; Saldarriaga, 2023). The amber flask was then securely capped and agitated on a shaker for 60  min.  Following  agitation,  the  soil  suspension was filtered through a 0.45 µm filter paper to remove soil particles. The filtrate was then transferred into another 120 mL amber flask and adjusted to a pH below 2. The sample was clearly labeled and submitted  to  the  laboratory  assistant  for  metal  analysis  using  ICP-OES  (ICP-OES  Thermo  Scientific ICAP6500 DUO kit Thermo Scientific equipment, Waltham, USA).

## Phaseolus vulgaris L. seedling monitoring

After  45  days  of  monitoring  the  bioavailability  of Hg and CPF in addition to monitoring the  physicochemical  properties  described  above,  the  common bean seedlings were harvested. These seedlings were carefully  harvested  from  each  rhizobox  in  order  not to  damage  their  structure,  and  length  measurements were taken of the stem, leaves, and roots. Then they were weighed and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h in order to proceed with the analysis of contaminants, both Hg and CPF.

## Statistical analysis

Statistical  analysis  was  carried  out  using  JMP®  Pro version 13.1.0 software. Data are presented as means with their corresponding standard error (SE). A oneway  analysis  of  variance  (ANOVA)  was  performed, and P values  &lt;  0.05  were  considered  significant.  To compare the means of the variables, a post hoc test was  applied.  In  addition,  multivariate  analysis  was used  to  identify  relationships  between  the  different variables.

## Physicochemical parameters results

The  impact  of the modifications' application on the  pH  and  EC  parameters  is  shown  in  Table  2.  It is  shown  how  pH  was  affected  by  the  addition  of amendments to  the  soil,  because  the  control  always showed  an  average  pH  of  5.5,  while  in  the  treatments with amendments it increased to more than 5.8 (Table 2). The addition of biochar increases nutrient availability,  which  causes  significant  pH  shifts  (Luo et  al.,  2011;  Wang  et  al.,  2015).  According  to  certain  research,  the  material  used  in  the  production  of biochar affects its ability to raise soil pH in the short and medium term. In a similar vein, the addition of mycorrhiza promotes an elevation in pH, but one that is  marginally  less  than  that  which  was  noted  in  the absence  of  biochar.  It  is  observed  that  although  the application of only mycorrhiza increases the pH, it is not  similar  to  the  amendments  that  present  biochar. Finally, it is noted that while adding just mycorrhiza raises pH, the effects are not comparable to those of biochar-containing  amendments.  In  the  case  of  EC, an  initial  increase  was  observed  with  respect  to  the control (CK), but over time, this decreased in all the treatments applied. In the literature, it has been recommended that the EC be below 1 mS·m -1 because this prevents phytotoxicity in crops and facilitates less management in the application of fertilizers (Butnan et al., 2015). On the other hand, it is evident that the application of this type of amendment can favor the increase of organic matter, which is strongly related to  EC  since  organic  matter  is  responsible  for  the accumulation of nutrients and their retention, which are key factors in reducing EC (Butnan et al., 2015; Chintala  et  al.,  2014;  Singh  et  al.,  2022).  Similarly, it  has  been  reported  that  EC  can  also  decrease  due to  the  absorption  of  nutrients  by  plants.  The  results shown in this study have also been reported in other works,  in  which  the  application  of  biochar  favors the  decrease  in  EC  after  several  days  of  application (Chintala et al., 2014; Singh et al., 2022).

## Mercury results

The fluctuation in Hg concentration over time in the various soil treatments is depicted in Fig. 2. On day 15,  it  is  clear  that  the  treatments  with  mycorrhiza (M)  and  control  (CK)  had  the  highest  Hg  bioavailability concentrations, exceeding 15 mg   kg -1 , but the treatments  with  biochar  (B)  and  biochar-mycorrhiza (B + M) had lower bioavailability concentrations. With a concentration of over 5 mg   kg -1 , or an approximate  80%  reduction,  biochar  stands  out  among  the methods used for the remediation of Hg in the soil. Another  treatment  that  presents  good  results  is  the combination  of  biochar  with  mycorrhizae  (B + M), which shows a reduction in the concentration of Hg in  the  soil,  although  it  is  a  slightly  greater  than  that achieved with biochar alone. In the case of the application of only mycorrhiza (M), it is observed that the decrease  in  concentration  is  very  low,  reaching  an almost 40% reduction with respect to the initial concentration  (58  mg    kg -1 ).  Finally,  the  control  (CK) shows  a  low  degradation  of  Hg,  which  is  expected with  this  type  of  metal  that  has  low  or  poor  degradation.  Some  authors  have  studied  the  effect  on  the symbiosis  between Lolium  perenne and  soil  mycorrhizae  at  very  low  concentrations  of  Hg,  reporting that  this  symbiosis  improves  the  absorption  of  Hg from the soil, and that is evident again in this work with common beans (Cruz et al., 2021; Leudo et al., 2020).

Figure  2  shows  that,  on  day  30  of  the  treatment, the bioavailability of Hg was only slightly reduced as a result of all soil treatments. From day 0 to day 15, the Hg levels decreased most significantly. The mycorrhizae (M) treatment showed a much higher reduction  by  day  45,  bringing  the  Hg  levels  down  to  far below 10 mg   kg -1 . This is a decrease from the starting concentration of almost 70%. In comparison, the Hg concentration in the control group (CK) decreased as well, but it did so much less successfully than it did in response to the soil treatments (Fig. 4).

Mycorrhizae  improve nutrient  uptake  and  help  stabilize  the  soil,  fostering circumstances  that  aid  in  the  breakdown  of  organic pollutants  and  the  immobilization  of  heavy  metals. The  results  consistently  show  that  the  combination of biochar and mycorrhizae is the most effective treatment for reducing contaminant concentrations in the  soil,  evidencing  a  remarkable  synergistic  effect that  enhances  the  degradation  process.  The  B + M treatments  achieved  reductions  of  more  than  70% (80  mg    kg -1 ),  in  some  cases  reaching  up  to  90% (120 mg   kg -1 ). This performance highlights not only the  ability  of  biochar  to  adsorb  contaminants  and improve  soil  properties  but  also  the  crucial  role  of mycorrhizae  in  promoting  plant  growth  and  associated microbial activity, resulting in a more favorable environment for bioremediation.

The effectiveness of this combination underscores the importance of using natural amendments in bioremediation  strategies,  offering  a  sustainable  and  less costly alternative to traditional methods. As the use of

biochar  and  mycorrhizae  continues  to  be  researched and refined, this approach is likely to become a key solution  for  mitigating  hydrocarbon  soil  contamination, thus contributing to the restoration of degraded ecosystems and overall environmental health.

From  Fig.  7,  it  can  be  seen  how  the  combined B + M amendment provided a more balanced nutrient supply and better soil conditions, leading to healthier and more robust plant growth. For its part, the bicohar probably improved soil structure and water retention, which  benefited  the  plants  in  their  growth. Therefore, the combination of biochar  and  mycorrhiza  provided  better  conditions  for plant growth, probably due to a synergistic effect that improved both nutrient availability and soil health.

The use of biochar and mycorrhiza as a combined soil amendment can have significant long-term effects on  soil  health  and  phytoremediation  efficiency.  This amendment  significantly  improves  soil  structure  by increasing soil porosity, which promotes water retention  and  aeration,  resulting  in  more  optimal  root development and more robust overall plant health. In addition,  both  biochar  and  mycorrhiza  contribute  to increasing the soil's ability to retain nutrients, thereby reducing reliance on chemical fertilizers and encouraging more sustainable agricultural practices (Saleem et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2019). It may also stimulate microbial  activity,  essential  for  nutrient  cycling  and decomposition  of  organic  matter,  leading  to  a  more fertile and healthier soil in the long term. Biochar can neutralize soil pH, making it more suitable for plant growth, while mycorrhizae favor plant root structure, decreasing the amount of metals that can be absorbed by  the  plant  (Fig.  5).  In  terms  of  phytoremediation, biochar  adsorbs  contaminants  such  as  heavy  metals  and  pesticides,  decreasing  their  bioavailability and toxicity, which enhances the ability of plants to perform this process (Mansoor et al., 2021). Healthy plants, with more developed root systems, can absorb and degrade contaminants more effectively. The combination  of  biochar  and  mycorrhizae  can  promote  robust  plant  growth,  improving  the  results  of phytoremediation. However, it is critical to consider some challenges,  such  as  the  risk  of  nutrient  runoff and water contamination from excessive manure use, which makes it crucial to apply these amendments in adequate  amounts  (Goss  et  al.,  2013;  Khoshnevisan et  al.,  2021;  Mansoor  et  al.,  2021).  In  addition,  the quality  of  biochar  can  vary  depending  on  its  source material  and  production  process,  so  it  is  essential to  use  high-quality  biochar  to  achieve  the  desired benefits.

Further research on the effects of Hg and CPF on both  soil  and  common  bean  ( Phaseolus  vulgaris )  is essential. Preliminary findings indicate that the application  of  certain  amendments  may  result  in  metal accumulation  in  the  bean  stem,  raising  concerns about the possible transfer of contaminants to seeds. It  is  crucial  to  determine  whether  seeds  can  absorb significant amounts of metals, as this could have serious implications for human health and food safety. In addition, it is important to review the existing scientific literature to identify data on mercury concentrations  in  common  bean  seeds.  Comparing  these  data with the results of recent studies will not only provide a  clearer  perspective  on  the  risk  associated  with  the consumption of contaminated beans but will also help to establish guidelines for contaminated soil management and sustainable agricultural production.

Additional research should focus on evaluating the bioaccumulation of metals in different bean varieties and identifying factors that may influence this uptake, such as the type of amendment used, soil conditions, and agricultural practices. Toxicity studies that assess the effects of exposure to these metals on bean growth and  development  as  well  as  on  consumer  health would also be beneficial. Addressing these issues may contribute  to  a  more  complete  understanding  of  the risks associated with Hg and CPF contamination and facilitate  more  effective  strategies  to  mitigate  their impact on agriculture and public health.

The effects  of  CPF  and  Hg  on  soil  and  common beans are being studied, and this research has important implications for environmental management and remediation  techniques.  To  effectively  mitigate  and remediate the effects of these toxins on crops and the environment, one must have a thorough understanding  of  these  effects.  Best  agricultural  practices  can be  informed  by  research  on  metal  bioaccumulation in plants. This can assist farmers in avoiding polluted soils and in choosing amendments that will decrease

the  uptake  of  heavy  metals.  This  promotes  the  sustainability  of  agricultural  ecosystems  while  simultaneously safeguarding consumer health.

Furthermore,  contrasting  the  mercury  content  of common  bean  seeds  with  data  already  available  in the scientific literature can offer important context for risk  evaluation  and  control.  By  incorporating  these discoveries  into  environmental  management  regulations, programs for pollution control and monitoring can be more easily implemented, leading to safer and more  conscientious  farming  operations.  In  the  end, this  multidisciplinary  strategy  promotes  increased public  awareness  of  the  significance  of  soil  health and food security in addition to addressing contamination,  which  helps  to  promote  more  sustainable development.

## Conclusions

The  study  offers  insightful  information  about  the remediation  potential  of  biochar  and  mycorrhizae (B + M) for soils contaminated with Hg and CPF. The results consistently show that the B + M combination exhibits  a  synergistic  impact  that  greatly  accelerates the  degradation  process,  making  it  the  most  effective  treatment  for  lowering  pollutant  concentrations. Biochar  and  mycorrhizae  treatments  demonstrated impressive decrease levels that often surpassed 70% (80  mg    kg -1 )  and,  in  several  instances,  exceeded 90% (120 mg   kg -1 ). The crucial role these modifications  play  in  bioremediation  activities  is  highlighted by  their  effectiveness.  Although  there  was  a  slight decrease  in  CPF  concentrations  in  the  control  samples  due  to  natural  degradation,  the  levels  were  still the  highest  of  all  treatments,  indicating  that  natural degradation alone is unable to achieve significant pollutant reduction in a reasonable amount of time.

The  study  emphasizes  the  important  function that biochar plays in lowering the bioavailability of mercury in soil. High percentages of Hg concentration  reduction  are  demonstrated  by  biochar  treatments,  demonstrating  the  efficacy  of  biochar  as  a remediation  agent.  Even  though  mycorrhizae  and biochar  together  produced  somewhat  superior  outcomes,  biochar  by  itself  was  incredibly  successful in eliminating metallic contaminants. Because they promote bioremediation processes and facilitate cation transfer, mycorrhizae are important components

of the soil rhizosphere. Mycorrhizae by themselves had less of an impact on CPF or Hg concentrations, but when combined with biochar, they had a noticeable synergistic effect. In addition to stabilizing soil structure and boosting nutrient uptake, mycorrhizae also  promote  the  breakdown  of  organic  pollutants and the immobilization of heavy metals. The combination of this dual purpose and the porous nature of biochar, which allows it to absorb toxins, makes it the perfect material for bioremediation.

The results further demonstrate that the addition of organic matter, such as biochar and mycorrhizae, is  crucial  for  successful  bioremediation.  Organic matter  improves  soil  health  by  enhancing  water retention,  increasing  nutrient  availability,  and  supporting  beneficial  soil  microorganisms.  The  study confirms that the combination of biochar and mycorrhizae creates a potent synergy, reducing the bioavailability of harmful metals like Hg while simultaneously promoting healthier plant growth through improved soil  conditions.  Additionally,  incorporating  compost  alongside  biochar  and  mycorrhizae significantly  boosts  contaminant  degradation  and lowers  bioavailability.  This  combination  aids  in CPF decomposition and reduces Hg bioavailability, making these contaminants less accessible to plants. Biochar's  capacity  to  adsorb  pollutants,  working in  synergy  with  compost,  enhances  soil  retention, while  mycorrhizae  boost  nutrient  uptake  and  contribute  to  soil  stability.  This  synergistic  effect  not only improves soil and plant health but also shields crops from the toxic impact of contaminants.

The results of this study demonstrate the viability of using mycorrhizae and biochar together as a reliable and effective bioremediation method for contaminated  soils.  The  combination  of  these  supplements works  in  concert  to  greatly  improve  the  breakdown of organic pollutants and the immobilization of heavy metals,  making  it  a  viable  option  for  reducing  soil pollution.  The  accumulating  evidence  in  favor  of using organic amendments to enhance soil health and lessen  environmental  contamination  is  strengthened by these findings. In agricultural settings, in particular,  it  is  advised  that  environmental  managers  and agricultural  practitioners  use  biochar  and  mycorrhizae in their remediation techniques for contaminated sites. In the end, this method can protect the environment and public health by improving soil quality and encouraging sustainable agriculture practices.
