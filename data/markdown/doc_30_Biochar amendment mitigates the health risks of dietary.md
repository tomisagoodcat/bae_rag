The accumulation of methylmercury (MeHg) in rice is an important MeHg exposure pathway in humans in several mercury (Hg)-contaminated areas. In this study, the effects of low-dose biochar (BC) amendment (0.3%, w/w) on MeHg mobility/phytoavailability in different Hg-contaminated paddy soils, MeHg accumulation in rice plants and the health risks associated with MeHg-laden rice consumption were investigated. Soils amended with different doses of bamboo-derived BC (0.3, 0.5, and 1%, w/w) were incubated under anoxic conditions in microcosm experiments. In addition, pot experiments were conducted involving rice cultivation with a low BC application rate (0.3%, w/w). We observed that (1) the fraction of extractable MeHg in soils decreased with BC addition in both the microcosm and pot experiments; (2) MeHg concentrations in the rice grains (brown rice) signi /uniFB01 cantly decreased by 56 e 88% in response to BC amendment, which may be attributed mainly to decreases in MeHg mobility/phytoavailability in the soil; and (3) the hazard quotient (HQ) values for adults and children and fetal intelligence quotient (IQ) decrements associated with MeHg-laden rice consumption were signi /uniFB01 cantly alleviated under BC amendment. Taken together, our /uniFB01 ndings suggest that a low dose of BC (0.3%, w/w) could have great potential for mitigating the health risks of dietary MeHg exposure from the consumption of rice grown in mercury (Hg)-contaminated areas.

## 1. Introduction

Mercury (Hg) is a global contaminant and can be methylated by certain microbial methylators to form methylmercury (MeHg) under anaerobic conditions in wetlands, lakes, rivers, and seawater (Beckers and Rinklebe, 2017; Frohne et al., 2012). Owing to its high

MeHg exposure can cause developmental impairment in fetuses, which can lead to intellectual disability and lower intelligence quotient (IQ) values (Axelrad et al., 2007); moreover, MeHg exposure may result in an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events in adults (Roman et al., 2011). Like wetlands, irrigated lowland/uniFB02 ooded rice paddies serve as active Hg methylation sites that facilitate inorganic mercury (IHg) conversion to MeHg (Liu et al., 2019b). In particular, MeHg in paddy soils can be easily taken up by rice plants and transferred to the grains (Zhang et al., 2010), ultimately posing a health risk to rice consumers. Owing to the shortage of arable land, Hg-contaminated soils are still being

used for rice cultivation, which can in turn lead to dietary exposure to MeHg among consumers of rice grown in Hg-contaminated regions (Feng et al., 2008; Li et al., 2015). Furthermore, a recent study has shown that rice may be a substantial global dietary source of human MeHg exposure because of international trade (Liu et al., 2019c). Therefore, remediation technologies, especially costeffective and green in situ remediation technologies, for decreasing MeHg accumulation in rice grown in Hg-contaminated areas are needed.

MeHg accumulation in rice is commonly mitigated by different in situ methods, such as growing rice aerobically (Wang et al., 2014) and adding chemical agents (e.g., sulfur or selenate/selenite) (Li et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2019). These techniques were developed to decrease the net MeHg production in soils. Biochar (BC) is recognized as an ideal material for in situ remediation of Hgcontaminated soils and sediments because of its multiple functions in the reduction in IHg and MeHg mobility and their bioavailability in soils and sediments (Beckers et al., 2019; GomezEyles et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2019a; O ' Connor et al., 2018) and in sustainable agriculture (Xiao et al., 2018). Several pioneering studies have indicated that BC and nanoactivated carbon can effectively reduce MeHg and the total Hg (THg) accumulation in rice (Shu et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020; Xing et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019), and BC amendment has occasionally been shown increase soil MeHg concentrations (Shu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019). For example, grain MeHg concentrations decreased by 49 e 92% following amendment with rice straw BC (1 e 4%, w/w) (Shu et al., 2016), by 73% following amendment with sewage sludge BC (5%, w/w) (Zhang et al., 2019), and by 47 e 53% following amendment with rice hull BC (24 e 72 t/ha, which is approximately equal to 1 e 4%, w/w) (Xing et al., 2020). However, these studies commonly used high-dose BC amendments rather than low-dose BC amendments, which could hinder widespread use of BC for remediating Hg-contaminated farming soils. The effects of low doses of BC on MeHg concentrations in the grains of rice plants grown in different Hg-contaminated paddy soils remains unclear, and the health risks to local people following BC amendment of paddy soils must be assessed.

The speci /uniFB01 c goals of this study were therefore to (1) investigate the impacts of BC amendment on MeHg mobility/phytoavailability in different Hg-contaminated paddy soils and MeHg accumulation in rice plants and (2) further assess the health risks associated with rice consumption following BC amendment on the basis the doseresponse relationship between MeHg intake and human health impacts. Speci /uniFB01 cally, using microcosm batch incubation, we /uniFB01 rst evaluated the effects of BC dose on net MeHg production and mobility in soils under anoxic conditions. Second, we investigated MeHg accumulation in rice plants grown in paddy soils after lowdose (0.3%, w/w) BC amendments in experiments, together with microcosm incubation, and the possible mechanisms involved were explored. Finally, based on existing models, we evaluated the in/uniFB02 uence of BC amendments on the hazard quotient (HQ) and IQ effects associated with rice consumption.

## 2.2. Microcosm batch experiments

Microcosm experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of BC dose on net MeHg production and MeHg mobility/ phytoavailability in soils under anoxic conditions, which predominate in paddy soil during rice cultivation. Brie /uniFB02 y, 6 g of soil was added to 50-mL centrifuge tubes (Corning, USA), after which the three soils were amended with different doses of BC (0.3, 0.5, and 1%, w/w). The control treatments without biochar addition were also conducted. Thirty milliliters of deionized water and sodium acetate (5 mM /uniFB01 nal concentration), an electron donor for microbial methylators (Christensen et al., 2018), was added to each tube, after which the tube was then sealed. All treatments (in triplicate) were incubated in a climate chamber at 28 /C14 C in the dark for 14 days. The sealed tubes were shaken manually twice per day during the incubation period. The overlying water and solid soil particles were sampled after incubation. For sampling, the tubes were put into a glovebag (AtmosBag, Aldrich, USA) /uniFB01 lled with nitrogen gas, the pH and redox potential (Eh) of the overlying water were measured via a Hach portable multimeter (HQ40D, Hach, USA), and the Eh was normalized to the standard hydrogen electrode. The tubes were then centrifuged (2000 /C2 g, 20 min) to separate the overlying water and soil particles. The overlying water samples were /uniFB01 ltered (0.45 m m) in the glovebag and preserved with 0.4% (v/v) HCl at /C0 20 /C14 C until further analysis of the dissolved MeHg. The soil particles were subsequently used to determine the MeHg concentrations and MeHg phytoavailability, which were calculated on a dry weight (dw) basis per unit mass of soil particles from the control treatments and BC-amended treatments. Moreover, at the end of incubation period, the population sizes of sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) and iron-reducing bacteria (IRB), which are considered the principal Hg methylators in paddy soils (Beckers and Rinklebe., 2017), were quanti /uniFB01 ed in the high-dose BC-amended treatments (1%, w/w) according to the most probable number (MPN) method (Vester and Ingvorsen, 1998).

## 2.3. Pot experiments

To further test the effects of BC on reducing MeHg mobility/

phytoavailability, we established pot experiments. Three Hgcontaminated paddy soils with or without BC amendment (i.e., BC-amended or control treatments, respectively) were used to investigate the effects of low application rates of BC on MeHg accumulation in rice in pot experiments. The soils (1.6 kg of airdried soil) were thoroughly homogenized with bamboo-derived BC (0.3%, w/w) and then placed into plastic pots (16 cm high, 12 cm in diameter). In total, 6 treatments with three replicates were investigated, which included BC-amended and control treatments for each soil. Two thirty-day-old healthy rice seedlings (Yongyou 538, a hybrid rice) were transplanted into the soil in each pot. The rice seedlings were grown under /uniFB02 ooded conditions (tap water 2 e 4 cm above the soil surface) in a rain shelter greenhouse for 112 days (06/22 e 10/12) in 2018. A more detailed description of the rice cultivation is presented in the Supplementary Data.

Soils were sampled on days 76 (heading stage) and 112 (at harvest) after rice transplantation. Two fresh soil core samples (1 e 6 cm) were collected from each pot, after which they were immediately placed into polypropylene centrifuge tubes and vacuum packed. All samples were stored in a cooler for approximately 2 h, transferred to the laboratory within 10 min and then preserved at /C0 20 /C14 C. The soil samples were lyophilized (Labconco, USA) and then stored in a vacuum-packed plastic bag for the determination of MeHg concentrations and MeHg phytoavailability.

At harvest, rice tissues, including the grain, straw (stems and leaves) and roots, were collected, after which they were washed with tap water and deionized water. The rice grains and straw were oven-dried (45 /C14 C, 72 h), and the aboveground biomass was calculated on the basis of all of the dry tissues from each pot. To obtain unpolished brown rice grains, their husks were removed via a mortar and pestle. Subsamples of brown rice and straw were ground into a /uniFB01 ne powder, and the brown rice sample was passed through a sieve (0.15 mm). The roots were subjected to iron plaque removal and then ground into a /uniFB01 ne powder by an IKA basic analytical mill (IKA A11, Germany) with liquid nitrogen. All the samples were stored at /C0 20 /C14 C prior to further analysis of MeHg.

## 2.4. Analytical methods

The THg in the soils and BC was determined by a DMA-80 direct Hg analyzer (Milestone, Italy) according to US EPA method 7473. Multiple blank boats were analyzed to determine the detection limit (DL), which was 0.04 m g/kg (Table S1). The MeHg concentrations in the soils and plants were determined by digestion and cold vapor atomic /uniFB02 uorescence spectrometric analysis (CVAFS, Brooks Rand, USA) according to US EPA method 1630. The samples (0.3 g of soil and 0.02 e 0.03 g of rice tissues) were digested in 2 mL of 25% KOH-methanol (w/w) on an end-over-end shaker (180 rpm, 60 /C14 C) for 4 h. The digests were diluted with deionized water, mixed thoroughly and then centrifuged (2900 /C2 g, 20 min). The supernatant was /uniFB01 ltered through a 0.45m m /uniFB01 lter with a polyethersulfone membrane (Anpel, China) for the determination of MeHg. Blanks were tested to determine the DL, which was 0.001 ng/L (Table S1). Certi /uniFB01 ed reference materials were digested and measured for quality control, with total recoveries ranging from 86 to 97% (relative standard deviation (RSD) &lt; 11%) for THg and MeHg. Detailed information is given in Table S1.

MeHg phytoavailability in the soils was assessed according to the ammonium thiosulfate ((NH4)2S2O3) extraction method, which has recently been validated for predicting MeHg accumulation in rice (Zhu et al., 2015). The fraction of extractable MeHg in soils, presented as the percentage of the total, was calculated as follows: fraction of (NH4)2S2O3 extracted from soils/total MeHg in soils /C2 100%. Ammonium thiosulfate extraction solution (2 g/L) was prepared by the use of deoxygenated water (1.5 L N2/min for

30 min). Approximately 0.8 g of freeze-dried soil and 2 mL of extraction solution were mixed together in a 15-mL centrifuge tube (Corning, USA) in a glovebag /uniFB01 lled with nitrogen gas. The tubes were capped tightly, after which they were shaken for 12 h in the dark (190 rpm, 25 /C14 C). Then centrifuged at 2900 /C2 g for 20 min, after which the supernatant was removed. One milliliter of the /uniFB01 ltered extract was then digested and used to determine the MeHg concentration according to the methods described above.

The concentrations of sulfate in the soil samples or BC were determined via ion chromatography (Dionex ICS1100, Thermo Fisher Scienti /uniFB01 c, USA) at a ratio of 1:10 (particles:water) after 4 h. In addition, the total carbon and sulfur contents were determined (Vario MICRO Cube, Elementar, Germany).

## 4. Discussion

## 4.1. BC amendment decreased MeHg accumulation in rice grains

The soils presented different characteristics of Hg contamination, as expected (Table 1). The values of THg and MeHg concentrations in the soils were within the range of values reported for rice paddy soils (Feng et al., 2008; Horvat et al., 2003; Rothenberg et al., 2014), and the MeHg/THg ratio (which typically serves as a proxy of net production potential of MeHg) was in the following order: soil Y &gt; soil X &gt; soil W. After 14 days of soil incubation, the MeHg/THg ratio increased and presented the same order as that of the air-dried soils (Fig. 1A). These results suggest that the soils contaminated with wastewater discharge that contained Hg could result in more severe health risks than those in Hg mining areas.

In the present study, BC amendment decreased the net MeHg production in all soils, with different reduction rates observed in the microcosm incubation experiments (Fig. 1A), which is consistent with /uniFB01 ndings in the literature (Bussan et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019). However, inconsistent changes in soil MeHg were observed in the pot experiments compared with the incubation experiments. For soils Y and X, soil MeHg concentrations decreased during rice cultivation (Fig. 2A and B); however, no reduction or a slight increase in soil MeHg was detected in soil W (Fig. 2A and B). Soil net MeHg production depends mainly on the concentration and bioavailability of IHg and the activity of microbial methylators (Hu et al., 2013; O ' Connor et al., 2019). The functional groups (e.g., sulfur-containing compounds) in BC have been reported to complex with IHg (Liu et al., 2019b), thereby reducing the mobility and bioavailability of IHg for microbial methylators (Liu et al., 2019b; O ' Connor et al., 2018), which could be mainly responsible for the reduced MeHg concentrations in the soils in the present study. The increased soil MeHg in soil W in the pot experiments could be attributed to the enhanced iron-reducing bacterium activity following BC amendment, which may be closely related to organic matter decomposition during rice cultivation (Marvin-DiPasquale et al., 2014). Compared with concentrations of total soil MeHg, the fraction of extractable MeHg concentrations or the percent of the total signi /uniFB01 cantly decreased in BC-amended soils X and Y in both the microcosm (Fig. 1D) and pot experiments (Fig. 2C and D). Furthermore, reductions in grain MeHg concentrations were observed following BC amendment, even at an application rate as low as 0.3% (w/w) in the pot experiments (Fig. 3A). The reduction rate of grain MeHg (by 56 e 88%, Fig. 3A) in our results is close or comparable to previously reported results involving BC addition at rates of 1 e 4%, for example, by 49 e 92% with a BC addition rate of 1 e 4% (Shu et al.,

2016), 73% with a BC addition rate of 5% (Zhang et al., 2019) and 47 e 53% with a BC addition rate of approximately 1 e 4% (Xing et al., 2020). The grain MeHg concentrations (Fig. 3A), MeHg uptake by the aboveground parts of rice plants (Fig. 4A) and MeHg BAFs (Fig. 4B) decreased in all three BC-amended soils, indicating that the translocation of MeHg from the soil to the rice plants was suppressed by BC amendment. The decreases in the fraction of extractable soil MeHg (percentage of the total) in the microcosm incubation (Fig. 1D) and pot experiments (Fig. 2C and D) con /uniFB01 rmed this suppression mechanism. Indeed, positive relationships were observed between the extractable MeHg concentrations in soils (i.e., on days 76 and 112) and both the MeHg concentrations in the grain and amount of MeHg uptake in rice plants (Fig. S3). Furthermore, the fraction of extractable MeHg (percentage of total) tended to decrease with increasing BC dose (Fig. 1D), which was consistent with the results of a previous study (Wang et al., 2019), and this fraction was negatively related to MeHg log K d values (r 2 ¼ 0.87, p &lt; 0.05 for the data of all three soils, Fig. S4). These results suggest that the dissolved MeHg diffuses in BC-amended soils and decreases MeHg phytoavailability, which could play a signi /uniFB01 cant role in the suppression of MeHg phytoavailability in soils amended with BC. The mechanism underlying this suppression is most likely the direct complexation of MeHg by organosulfur groups in BC (Wang et al., 2019) and/or the indirect binding of MeHg to organic sulfur species in soils because of the induction by BC amendment (Xing et al., 2020). However, much is unknown concerning their relative importance for reducing MeHg phytoavailability in soils.

4.2. BC amendment mitigated human exposure to MeHg associated with rice consumption

Previous studies have shown that MeHg concentrations are elevated in the grains of rice plants grown in Hg-contaminated areas in China and other countries (Rothenberg et al., 2014), with MeHg concentrations of 1.9 e 27.6 m g/kg in the Wanshan Hg mining area (Feng et al., 2008), 8.2 e 80 m g/kg in the Xunyang Hg mining area (Qiu et al., 2012) and 1.8 e 67 m g/kg in Qingzhen valley, where the water is contaminated with Hg originating from a chemical plant (Horvat et al., 2003). On the basis of these reported concentrations and the reference values of IR and BW (Equation (2)) in this study, we calculated HQ values to compare the results from the /uniFB01 eld with those from our pot study. The /uniFB01 eld results showed that the HQ values for adults and children were 0.1 e 5.7 and 0.1 e 4.0, respectively, in the Wanshan and Xunyang Hg mining areas and 0.1 e 4.8 and 0.09 e 3.4, respectively, in the Qingzhen Hg-polluted areas caused by wastewater discharge. HQ values &gt; 1 indicate potential health hazards associated with MeHg exposure from dietary rice consumption, which is consistent with other health risk assessments that use different values of IR and BW in /uniFB01 eld study (Feng et al., 2008). Compared with the HQ values from /uniFB01 eld investigations in Hg-contaminated areas, our results from the control treatments of the three soils in the pot experiments presented comparable or greater HQ values (Fig. 5A and B), which strongly indicates the potential health risks in residents.

Notably, a low application rate of BC signi /uniFB01 cantly decreased the daily intake of MeHg via rice consumption by reducing MeHg accumulation in the rice grains (Fig. 3A), thereby mitigating the health risks, as re /uniFB02 ected by the decreased HQ values (Fig. 5A and B) and fetal IQ decrements (Fig. 5C). The decreases in MeHg concentrations in the rice grains could also reduce the risks of adverse cardiovascular events in adults on the basis of the model of fatal heart attacks related to MeHg intake (Rice et al., 2010). Indeed, the MeHg concentration in rice grains harvested from BC-amended soils X and Y still exceeds the threshold concentration of Hg in rice grains (20 m g/kg) permitted by the national standards of China (GB2762-2017), demonstrating that additional investigations are needed to meet the national standard for food safety of rice. Given the fraction of extractable MeHg decreased consistently with increasing BC dose (Fig. 1D), a high application rate of biochar would further reduce the MeHg concentration in rice grains. In addition, the remediation methods combining both biochar and selenium (Wang et al., 2019) or using engineered BC (e.g., sulfurmodified biochar, O ' Connor et al., 2018) could be an alternative approach for mitigating MeHg accumulation in rice gains. Generally, BC amendment decreased the rice grain MeHg concentrations and subsequently reduced the daily MeHg intake from rice, which mayeventually reduce the health risks of dietary MeHg exposure to local inhabitants. Thus, our results suggest that low-dose BC amendments may have great potential for reducing MeHg health risks in Hg-contaminated areas, even despite the associated uncertainties and limitations.

## 5. Conclusions

Taken together, our results show that the addition of low-dose bamboo-derived BC (0.3%, w/w) to different Hg-contaminated paddy soils could be a cost-effective method for reducing MeHg accumulation in rice grains. The reduced accumulation can be mainly attributed to decreases in MeHg mobility/phytoavailability linked to the interactions between MeHg, BC and the soil. Consequently, BC amendment may lead to a reduction in both the average daily intake of MeHg via rice consumption and a range of health risks. In the present study, only bamboo-derived BC was selected to investigate the mitigation effects of BC on three Hg-contaminated paddy soils, and additional long-term studies that consider different biochars derived from other materials and other soils are needed. Moreover, additional studies that incorporate several methods, including the reduction in MeHg phytoavailability in soils via engineered BC, selection of low-MeHg-accumulating crop cultivars and management of water resources, are warranted for a more effective mitigation strategy to reduce the health risks of dietary MeHg exposure from rice consumption.
