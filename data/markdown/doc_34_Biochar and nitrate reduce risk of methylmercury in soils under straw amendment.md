## a b s t r a c t

There is growing evidence that incorporating crop straw into soils, which is being widely encouraged in many parts of the world, could increase net methylmercury (MeHg) production in soils and MeHg accumulation in crops. We explored the possibility of mitigating the risk of increased MeHg levels under straw amendment by transforming straw into biochar (BC). Greenhouse and batch experiments were conducted, in which soil MeHg concentrations, MeHg phytoavailability and accumulation in rice, dynamics of sulfate, nitrate and abundances of sulfate reducing bacteria (SRB) were compared in ' Control ' (Hg contaminated soil), ' Straw ' (soil with 1% rice straw), ' Straw + BC ' (soil with 1% straw and 1% biochar), and ' Straw + BC + N ' (soil with 1% straw, 1% biochar and 0.12% nitrate). Our results indicate that straw amendment increased MeHg concentrations in soils (28 -136% higher) and rice plants (26% higher in grains, ' Straw ' versus ' Control ' ), while co-application of biochar with straw reduced grain MeHg levels (60% lower, ' Straw + BC ' versus ' Straw ' ). However, biochar amendment enhanced soil MeHg levels (5 -75% higher, ' Straw + BC ' versus ' Control ' ). Interestingly, nitrate addition helped reduce soil MeHg concentrations (11 -41% lower, ' Straw + BC + N ' versus ' Straw + BC ' ) by facilitating nitrate reduction while inhibiting SRB activities. ## 1. Introduction

Recently, there is increasing evidence that straw return, a common wayoforganic matter put into farm soils, could facilitate transformation of inorganic mercury (IHg) to the more toxic and bioaccumulative methylmercury (MeHg) species (H.K. Zhu et al., 2015; Windham-Myers et al., 2014a; Strickman and Mitchell, 2017; Y.R. Liu et al., 2016). For instance, amendment of crop straw into soils was reported to increase soil MeHg levels by 19 -33% (Zhu et al., 2016), 29 -78% (Shu et al., 2016a), 25 -91% (Y.R. Liu et al., 2016), and 20 -669% (Windham-Myers et al., 2014a). Consequently, elevated MeHg levels have been found in rice under straw amendment (Zhu et al., 2016). Considering that rice consumption has recently been demonstrated to be an important pathway of dietary MeHg uptake (Zhang et al., 2010a, 2010b), the enhanced MeHg accumulation in rice under straw amendment could intensify concerns about human exposure to MeHg (Zhang et al., 2010b; Meng et al., 2010; Windham-Myers et al., 2014a, 2014b). Straw amendment is being encouraged globally to reduce air pollution (compared with straw burning) and increase soil fertility (Mandal et al., 2004; Tirol-Padre et al., 2005; Ladha et al., 2004); thus, mitigating the risk posed by higher MeHg concentrations in straw amended farming soils is of great importance.

In the last decade, there has been increasing interest in using biochar to immobilize metals and remediate metal-contaminated soils (Shu et al., 2016b; Jiang et al., 2012; Khan et al., 2013; Bian et al., 2014). This is partly because of the high surface areas and the abundance of metal binding sites in biochar (Cao et al., 2009; Gomez-Eyles et al., 2013; P. Liu et al., 2016). For Hg, a few recent studies indicate that straw-derived biochar could immobilize IHg and MeHg in soils and sediments (Shu et al., 2016b; Liu et al., 2017) and biochar amendment has been shown to reduce MeHg accumulation in plants (e.g., rice and mustard, Shu et al., 2016a, 2016b). For instance, Shu et al. (2016b) recently reported that straw biochar amendment could reduce MeHg levels in rice grains by 49 -92%. Furthermore, a XANES (X -ray absorption near edge structure) study provides novel insight into the possible mechanisms of Hg-biochar binding: Hg was bound strongly with S in high-S content biochar, but bound to O and Cl in low-S biochar (P. Liu et al., 2016). Therefore, the use of straw biochar may be a promising approach to reduce straw amendment-induced MeHg bioaccumulation in plants.

While biochar was shown to effectively immobilize MeHg in soils (Shu et al., 2016a, 2016b), it should be noted that straw biochar increased net production of MeHg in soils at the same time (33 -306% higher), probably through increasing supply of electron acceptors (i.e., sulfate) for microbial methylators. This together with the high concentrations of electron donors produced following straw decomposition/transformation in soils (e.g., acetate), could possibly result in elevated microbial MeHg production in soils amended with both straw and straw biochar. To reduce the side effects of biochar amendment on soil MeHg levels, there is a need to decrease the activities of SRB (Zeng et al., 2016). In this regard, nitrate could be a potential candidate to be amended together with biochar. Nitrate addition, which could facilitate nitrate reduction while simultaneously inhibiting sulfate reduction, has been widely documented to inhibit SRB activities (Greene et al., 2003; Zhang et al., 2015). Therefore, we hypothesize that co-application of nitrate with biochar may help reduce soil MeHg levels and further decrease MeHg accumulation in rice under straw amendment.

This study therefore aims to address two important questions about controlling the potential risk posed by MeHg in straw amended soils: (1) Can MeHg accumulation be reduced in rice under straw amendment, using biochar and/or nitrate addition? If so, (2) what are the underlying mechanisms? To achieve these goals, greenhouse experiments (i.e., pot experiments) together with batch experiments were carried out: (1) Soil MeHg levels and MeHg accumulation in rice following straw, straw + biochar, and straw + biochar + nitrate amendment were compared in pot experiments; and (2) the variation in MeHg mobility and availability to rice plants (i.e., phytoavailability, quanti /uniFB01 ed as percentage of MeHg extractable by ammonium thiosulfate), sulfate and nitrate reduction, and SRB activities were monitored through time in batch experiments. Results from this study might help control the risk posed by increased MeHg accumulation under straw amendment andimproveourunderstandingof Hgbiogeochemistry in contaminated soils.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Soil, rice straw and biochar

Surface soil (0 -20 cm) was collected from a Hg-contaminated paddy /uniFB01 eld located in Xunyang County, Shaanxi Province, China, and is referred to as XY soil. Xunyang is one of the biggest Hg mining areas in China (Qiu et al., 2012). The XY soil was air-dried, ground and sieved through 2-mm mesh, and used in both pot and batch experiments. The total Hg (THg) and MeHg concentrations in the soil were 33,200 ±400 and 6.0 ± 0.1 μ g/kg, respectively. Low-Hg rice straw (THg: 30.9 ±0.2 μ g/kg, MeHg: 2.7 ± 0.1 μ g/kg, to minimize straw amendmentinduced Hg input) was collected from Yixing City, Jiangsu Province, China, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, oven-dried, ground and sieved through 0.28-mm mesh and used for soil amendment and biochar production. Straw biochar was produced from rice straw by pyrolysis at 600 °C for 2 h under oxygen -limited conditions (Shu et al., 2016b), ground into powder and sieved through 0.28-mm mesh (similar to above). Concentrations of THg and MeHg in the biochar were 14.1 ±0.1 μ g/kg and 0.3 ± 0.01 μ g/kg, respectively. The other characteristics of the soil and biochar are listed in Table S1 (Supplementary information). Chemicals and containers used in this study are described in Supplementary information, text.

## 2.2. Pot experiments

Pot experiments, in which rice was planted in XY soil amended with or without rice straw, straw biochar and nitrate, were conducted to explore the possibility of reducing MeHg accumulation in rice under straw amendment using biochar and/or nitrate addition. A total of four treatments were carried out in triplicate, i.e., (1) ' Control ' , i.e., XY soil; (2) ' Straw ' , i.e., XY soil amended with 1% of rice straw (10 g straw/kg soil); (3) ' Straw + BC ' , i.e., XY soil amended with 1% of straw and 1% of straw biochar; (4) ' Straw + BC + N ' , i.e., XY soil amended with 1% of straw, 1% of biochar and 0.12% of nitrate (1.215 g NaNO3/kg soil). Since we mainly focused on investigating ways to mitigate risk of MeHg under straw amendment, effects of adding biochar alone on Hg dynamics in soils were not investigated, but it was reported in our previous study (Shu et al., 2016b). On day 0, 2 kg of XY soil was amended with straw, biochar or nitrate, added with basic fertilizer

(128 mg N/kg soil in CO(NH2)2, 82 mg P/kg and 103 mg K/kg soil in KH2PO4), mixed thoroughly, and saturated with deionized water. All pots were placed in a greenhouse (located in Nanjing, Jiangsu Province) at ambient temperature (15 -38 °C). After the soils were incubated for 24 days, rice seedlings ( Oryza sativa Wufengyou2168, pre-cultured in low-Hg soil for 30 days) were transplanted into the soils (2 seedlings/ pot). The soils were /uniFB02 ooded with deionized water (2 -3 cm) during the rice cultivation period. The number of replicates, i.e., 2 seedlings/ pot and 3 replicate pots/treatment, could result in relative low relative standard deviation in either soil or tissue MeHg levels, according to our previous experiences in conducting pot experiments (e.g., Wang et al., 2016a, 2016b; Zhu et al., 2016; Shu et al., 2016b).

To investigate the potential effects of straw amendment on MeHg dynamics in soils, MeHg concentrations in soils and rice tissues were quanti /uniFB01 ed. Soil samples were collected several times during the rice cultivation period, i.e., on day 24 (the day of seedling transplant), day 80 (the beginning of heading stage) and day 145 (the day of rice harvest). Speci /uniFB01 cally, rhizosphere soil (2 -10 cm) was sampled, and immediately separated into sub-samples in a glove-box /uniFB01 lled with nitrogen gas. Subsamples were then used for determining soil MeHg concentrations (details described below) and concentrations of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) (Zeller et al., 2001). Rice tissues (i.e., root, straw and grain) were harvested on day 145, rinsed thoroughly with tap water and deionized water, oven-dried, weighed, ground into powder, stored at 4 °C and analyzed for MeHg within 21 days. For root, iron plaque on root was removed using a previously published method (Huang et al., 2013).

## 2.3. Batch experiments

Batch experiments, in which XY soils were incubated in batch reactors, were carried out to further elucidate how biochar and nitrate addition could affect bioaccumulation of MeHg in straw amended soils. Conducting batch experiments would enable exploring the earlier (day 0 to 15) and probably more dynamic phases of MeHg productions in straw-amended paddy soils. More importantly, this is achieved without intensively sampling soil from pots during the early stage of rice cultivation, which could possibly alter soil conditions and thus MeHg production, as well as growth of seedlings. The batch experiments, i.e., incubation of Hg-contaminated paddy soils in batch reactors under generally reduced conditions, were carefully designed to simulate rhizosphere environment in soil-rice systems (Wang et al., 2016b; Zhu et al., 2016). Especially, one of the key effects of rice cultivation on soil biogeochemistry and thus MeHg production is root exudation of small-molecular-weight organic matter as labile carbon (MarvinDiPasquale et al., 2014). This is simulated in the batch experiments by adding lactate, serving as electron donors for microbial methylators of Hg (e.g., SRB).

The availability of MeHg to the rice plants and dynamics of sulfate, nitrate, and chloride, and microbial methylators were quanti /uniFB01 ed and compared among ' Control ' , ' Straw ' , ' Straw + BC ' , and ' Straw + BC +N ' treatments. Brie /uniFB02 y, 10 g (dry weight) of XY soil was amended with or without straw, straw biochar or nitrate (as described above) and placed into a 50 mL polypropylene centrifuge tube. Thirty mL of deionized water and lactate (5 mM, as electron donor for microbial methylators, Fulda et al., 2013) were added to each tube, sealed and then incubated in the dark in a climate chamber at 28 °C. The tubes were shaken manually twice per day. Samples were collected four times during the experiments, i.e., on day 0 (4 h after straw, biochar or nitrate amendment), day 5, day 10 and day 15. During sampling, tubes were /uniFB01 rst centrifuged (4000 r/min, 20 min) to separate the overlying water and soil particles and then put into a glove-box /uniFB01 lled with nitrogen gas. Eh in the overlying water was immediately measured using multi-parameter water quality sensors (HQ30d, HACH, USA). After that, overlying water samples were /uniFB01 ltered (0.45μ m) and stored in the dark at 4 °C until further analysis for sulfate, nitrate and chloride using ion chromatography (Dionex ICS-1100, Thermo Fisher Scienti /uniFB01 c, USA). The soil particles were quanti /uniFB01 ed for MeHg mobility and availability to the rice plants (operationally de /uniFB01 ned as the extraction rate of MeHg from the soil by ammonium thiosulfate, D.W. Zhu et al., 2015, more details are described in Supplementary information, text) and analyzed for total Hg and MeHg concentrations. In addition, abundances of SRB in soils on day 15 were quanti /uniFB01 ed based on a method described by the previous publication (Vester and Ingvorsen, 1998).

## 2.4. MeHg analysis and QA/QC

The protocol for MeHg analysis of soils, rice plant tissues and aqueous samples (e.g., extractant solutions) has been described in previous publications (D.W. Zhu et al., 2015; Shu et al., 2016a, 2016b). Brie /uniFB02 y, samples were extracted in 25% KOH-Methanol (w/w) and incubated at 60 °C for 4 h. Then, the digests were diluted with deionized water and analyzed for MeHg using an automatic MeHg analyzer (CVAFS, Brooks Rand model III, Brooks Rand Laboratories, USA) according to Method 1630 (USEPA, 2001). The method detection limit (MDL) is 0.002 μ g/kg for MeHg. Quality control for MeHg measurements included method blanks, triplicates analyses, matrix spikes and the parallel analysis of certi /uniFB01 ed reference materials (CRM), i.e., soil (ERM-CC580) and plant tissue (GBW10010). The recoveries of MeHg were between 85 and 110% in the CRMs and 70 -110% in the MeHg-spiked ammonium thiosulfate. Relative standard deviations of MeHg concentrations in soils and rice tissues were 0.7 -12.3% (averagely 7.1%) and 3.1 -16.4% (averagely 9.0%), respectively.

## 4. Discussion

4.1. Straw biochar mitigates risk posed by MeHg associated with straw amendment

It has been reported in a few pioneering studies that amendment of crop straw into soils could enhance net MeHg production in soils (Windham-Myers et al., 2014a, 2014b; Marvin-DiPasquale et al., 2014; Alpers et al., 2014; Y.R. Liu et al., 2016; H.K. Zhu et al., 2015) and thus MeHg bioaccumulation (Zhu et al., 2016). Consistent with those previous studies, the results of our pot experiments also demonstrate significantly enhanced MeHg levels in soils on day 24 (Fig. S1A) and in rice plants (Fig. S1B). It is speculated that amended straw could affect Hg dynamics in different ways: (1) Straw-derived organic matter, e.g., acetate, may serve as electron donor to microbial methylators (e.g., sulfate reducing bacteria) and facilitate microbial MeHg production (Windham-Myers et al., 2014b). Similarly, we also observed significantly higher DOC levels in soils at the beginning and end of rice cultivation in straw amended soils ( p = 0.001; p = 0.004, Fig. S4).

The changes in rice MeHg concentrations under biochar amendment could be mainly attributed to the changes in (1) plant biomass, (2) the availability of MeHg to the rice plants and (3) soil MeHg concentrations. under biochar amendment ( ' Straw + BC ' versus ' Straw ' , Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, SRB abundances were evidently higher in ' Straw + BC ' than in ' Straw ' (Fig. S7), which could be mainly attributed to the increased supply of both electron donors due to straw amendment (e.g., increased DOC levels, Fig. S4) and electron acceptors due to biochar amendment (Fig. 3B). Considering that SRB are mainly responsible for the microbial MeHgproduction in the studied soil (Wang et al., 2016a), we may infer that biochar-induced increases in SRB abundances could be responsible for the increased MeHg production in soils under biochar amendment. In addition to sulfate, biochar amendment also increased chloride levels in soils by 169% (Fig. 3D, ' Straw + BC ' versus ' Straw ' ), possibly because biochar could promote the leaching of chloride from soils (Yue et al., 2015). Consequently, the increased chloride levels may increase mobility and availability of inorganic Hg in soils (Mason et al., 1995), and subsequently facilitate microbial transformation of inorganic Hg to MeHg. To our knowledge, biochar-induced increases in chloride levels and their potential impacts on MeHg production have not been reported before and warrant further investigation.

4.2. Nitrate addition reduced net MeHg production in soils and further reduced MeHg bioaccumulation

While biochar amendment with straw could reduce MeHg accumulation in rice, the elevated soil MeHg levels may increase the risk of MeHg uptake by organisms in soil-rice systems (e.g., deposit-feeders), and could pose a risk of MeHg accumulation in crops in a longer term. To address this concern, we added nitrate with biochar and straw, hypothesizing that nitrate addition would inhibit the activities of SRB by facilitating nitrate reduction in soils. We did /uniFB01 ndthat the addition of nitrate in the presence of straw and biochar signi /uniFB01 cantly reduced soil MeHg concentrations ( ' Straw + BC + N ' versus ' Straw ' or ' Straw +BC ' , Fig. 1A). The nitrate-induced reduction in MeHg production in soils should be attributed to the reduced SRB activities in the presence of nitrate, considering that (1) sulfate levels decreased slower in ' Straw + BC + N ' than ' Straw + BC ' (Fig. 3B), and (2) abundances of SRB were lower in ' Straw + BC + N ' than ' Straw + BC ' (Fig. S7). Meanwhile, quick reduction of nitrate was noted in ' Straw + BC + N ' , i.e., from 280 mg/L to below detection limit within 5 days in bath experiments (Fig. 3C), suggesting enhanced activities of nitrate reducing bacteria. The changes in the activities of sulfate and nitrate reducing bacteria could be attributed to their competition for electron donors, e.g., lactate in the batch experiments. In fact, competition between sulfate and nitrate reducing bacteria has been widely documented (Voordouw et al., 2009; Hubert, 2010; Gieg et al., 2011; Callbeck et al., 2013).

Nitrate addition had only minor effects on the availability of MeHg in soils ( ' Straw + BC + N ' versus ' Straw + BC ' Fig. 2). This is not surprising in view of the weak complexation between nitrate and Hg (Canty et al., 1978); thus nitrate is not expected to affect the binding between biochar and MeHg.

It should be noted also, that nitrate addition resulted in signi /uniFB01 cantly lower biomass of whole plant (Fig. S3, ' Straw + BC + N ' versus ' Straw +BC ' ) and grain (1.6 ± 0.5 g/pot in ' Straw + BC + N ' versus 3.8 ± 0.7 g/pot in ' Straw + BC ' ). This is because high levels of nitrate could suppress storage root formation and thus plant growth, which would ultimately reduce biomass yield (Wilson and Lowe, 1973; Taranet et al., 2017). Consequently, the reduced MeHg production in soils led to the further reduction in MeHg concentrations in rice plants following nitrate addition (Fig. 1A, ' Straw + BC + N ' versus ' Straw + BC ' ). Meanwhile, the reduction in plant MeHg levels was partly offset by the decreased plant biomass.

## 5. Conclusions and implications

Our results indicate that addition of straw biochar with straw could reduce MeHg accumulation in rice plants under straw amendment. The possible reasons for the decreased accumulation of MeHg, are that biochar reduces MeHg phytoavailability in soils and ' biodilutes ' MeHg levels in plants by increasing plant biomass. In addition, nitrate addition could help reduce biochar-induced increases in soil MeHg levels, probably by inhibiting the activities of SRB. The co-application of straw biochar and nitrate into Hg-contaminated soils could reduce both soil MeHglevels and MeHg accumulation in rice plants under straw amendment. Therefore, this may be a promising method to remediate Hgcontaminated soils, especially considering the massive use of straw and biochar for soil amendment (Tan et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2017) and nitrate as a fertilizer (Zhu et al., 2002). However, the reduced plant biomass due to high levels of nitrate addition emphasizes the necessity to further investigate the possibility of using lower concentrations of nitrate (for inhibiting SRB-mediated MeHg production). These data, together with results of other recent studies (e.g., Cheah et al., 2014; H.K. Zhu et al., 2015; Shu et al., 2016b) may suggest that straw management, e.g., straw burning, straw return and application of straw biochar, could be a key factor controlling Hg biogeochemistry and cycling in soils and should be carefully considered, especially in Hg-contaminated areas.
