## A B S T R A C T

Edited by Dr. G. Liu

Keywords: Mercury (II) Modified biochar Sulfur/zinc co-doping

Stabilization mechanism

In this study, a novel sulfur/zinc co-doped biochar (SZ-BC) stabilizer was successfully developed for the remediation of mercury-contaminated soil. Results from SEM, TEM, FTIR and XRD revealed that biochar (BC) was successfully modified by sulfur and zinc. In the batch adsorption experiments, the sulfur-zinc co-pyrolysis biochar  displayed  excellent  Hg(II)  adsorption  performance,  with  the  maximum  adsorption  capacity  of  SZ-BC (261.074 mg/g) being approximately 16.5 times that of BC (15.855 mg/g). Laboratory-scale static incubation, column leaching, and plant pot experiments were conducted using biochar-based materials. At an additional dosage of 5 % mass ratio, the SZ-BC exhibits the most effective stabilization of mercury in soil, leading to a significant reduction in leaching loss compared to the control group (CK) by 51.30 %. Following 4 weeks of incubation and 2 weeks of leaching with SZ-BC, the residual mercury in the soil increased by 27.84 %. As a result, potential ecological risk index of mercury decreased by 92 % compared to the CK group. In the pot experiment, SZ-BC significantly enhanced the growth of Chinese cabbage, with biomass and root dry weight reaching 3.20 and  2.80  times  that  of  the  CK  group,  respectively.  Additionally,  the  Translocation  Factor  (TF)  and  Bioconcentration Factor (BF) were reduced by 44.86 % and 74.43 %, respectively, in the SZ-BC group compared to the CK group. Moreover, SZ-BC can effectively improve enzyme activities and increase microbial communities in mercury-contaminated soils. The mechanisms of adsorption and stabilization were elucidated through electrostatic adsorption, ion exchange, surface complexation, and precipitation. These findings provide a potentially effective material for stabilizing soils contaminated with mercury.

## 1. Introduction

Mercury is a naturally occurring toxic heavy metal element that is essentially non-biodegradable. It primarily exists as highly reactive Hg (II) in the environment and can pose significant risks to both humans and aquatic creatures, even in small quantities (Chizitere Emenike et al., 2023).  Human  industrial  production  activities,  such  as  smelting  in mercury  mines,  burning  fossil  fuels  in  industrial  areas,  discharging mercury-containing waste, irrigating with sewage, using mercury-containing pesticides, engaging in long-term human activities in commercial areas, and improperly disposing of domestic waste, are the  primary  sources  of  soil  mercury  pollution  (Zhang  et  al.,  2024a). According to a study, the average concentration of mercury in Chinese farmland was 10.8 mg/kg between 2005 and 2017 (Huang et al., 2019). Mercury is a bioaccumulative element that can pose a significant risk to microorganisms, plants, and animals at different trophic levels. It can be transported  and  accumulated  through  the  food  chain,  eventually entering  the  human  body  through  biomagnification  and  potentially threatening human health (Ali et al., 2019). Therefore, it is crucial to investigate  effective  techniques  for  eliminating  mercury  from  the environment.

In recent decades, various physical, chemical, and bioremediation techniques have been employed to remediate soils contaminated with mercury  (Huang  et  al.,  2022).  Among  these  methods,  stabilization techniques are preferred because of their ease of operation, low cost, and high efficiency in removing contaminants (Huang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024b). Specifically, stabilizers are added to the soil to chemically react with mercury through processes such as adsorption, precipitation, ion  exchange,  co-precipitation,  complexation,  and  redox  reactions, reducing  its  toxicity,  mobility,  and  bioavailability  (Liu  et  al.,  2023).

Currently,  hydroxyapatite  (Yang  et  al.,  2022),  ground  granulated blastfurnace  slag  (GGBS)  (Ng  et  al.,  2021),  ferrihydrite  (Yang  et  al., 2023) and other stabilizers have been widely used in the remediation of heavy  metal  contaminated  soils.  Nevertheless,  Liu  et  al.  (2024) demonstrated  that  ferrihydrite  is  susceptible  to  transformations  that result in secondary release of adsorbed contaminants. This process is influenced by the presence of organic matter in the soil, including humic acid,  fulvic  acid,  and  lignin.  Ng  et  al.  (2021)  demonstrated  that  the incorporation of GGBS into the soil resulted in an increase in pH, with a bias towards the remediation of acidic soil types. Moreover, the utilization of GGBS was observed to diminish the active constituents of the herbs. Consequently, it is of the utmost importance to select an appropriate stabilizer.

Biochar is a stable solid material that is rich in carbon and highly aromatized.  It  is  produced  by  thermally  cracking  biomass  feedstock under fully or partially anoxic conditions (Lehmann et al., 2011). Biochar possesses various  physicochemical  properties  that  render  it appropriate for a diverse range of energy and environmental applications. It can serve as an adsorbent, catalyst precursor, anaerobic digestion,  soil  amendment,  and  electrochemical  energy  storage  material. (Zhang et al., 2019). The type of biomass feedstock and pyrolysis conditions are the main factors that affect the physicochemical properties of biochar. Lignin-rich biomasses produce macroporous biochar (pore size &gt; 50 nm), whereas cellulose-rich biomasses mainly produce microporous biochar (pore size &lt; 2 nm). The high porosity of biochar and the abundance  of  functional  groups  on  its  surface  make  it  effective  in absorbing heavy metal pollutants from the environment. Particularly, heavy metal cations including copper, silver, cadmium, lead, and mercury exhibit enhanced adsorption and binding properties when interacting with biochar. In a study conducted by Xu et al. (2016), two types of biochar were prepared from bagasse (BB) and hickory chips (HCB) and their adsorption capabilities for mercury in water were compared with activated carbon (AC). The results showed that both biochar types were not only more effective than AC in removing mercury (BB &gt; HCB &gt; AC), but also more cost-effective to produce compared to AC, which requires higher  temperatures and activation treatments.  However, the adsorption performance of traditional single biochar is insufficient for meeting current pollution control requirements, as it has low adsorption  quantity  for  heavy  metals.  Recent  research  has  therefore focused on developing biochar-modified materials. Studies have demonstrated that loading sulfur-containing functional groups into the biochar material can greatly enhance its adsorption capacity for Hg(II). According  to  Pearson ' s  hard-soft  acid-base  (HSAB)  theory  (Pearson, 1963), functional groups containing sulfur (S) have been found to form strong  complexes  with  metals  that  are  classified  as  soft  acids.  For example, mercury is considered a soft acid and can form a strong complex, Hg-S, with functional groups that contain sulfur. In many studies, singlet sulfur or sulfur-containing compounds (such as mercaptan, sulfuric acid, SO2, CS2, and H2S) have commonly been used for modifying biochar (O ' Connor et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2017; Anstey et al., 2016). However, most sulfur modification reagents are economically expensive and  environmentally  toxic,  whereas  sublimated  sulfur  is  non-toxic, inexpensive, and can be used to modify biochar through a simple pyrolysis  process  (Jeon  et  al.,  2020).  In  addition,  among  the  various modifiers, ZnCl2, a Lewis acid, can function as a wetting and swelling agent for cellulosic biomass feedstocks. Due to its corrosive and soluble properties in cellulose, it is well-suited as a catalytic dehydration and pore forming agent, thereby facilitating the creation of biochar micropores (Acharya et al., 2009; Li et al., 2018; Xia et al., 2016).

In this study, a novel composite of sulfur/zinc co-doped corn stover biochar (SZ-BC) was synthesized and utilized as an effective stabilizer. Batch adsorption experiments, static incubation, and dynamic leaching were carried out to evaluate the SZ-BC adsorption and stability properties.  Furthermore,  pot  experiments,  enzyme  activity  measurements and microbial community analyzes were used to assess the ecological risks  of  mercury  in  soil.  The  study  aimed:  1)  to  synthesize  a  novel modified biochar stabilizer, 2) to evaluate its effectiveness in stabilizing mercury-contaminated soils and assessing associated ecological risks, and 3) to analyze the mechanisms underlying the stabilization process.

## 2. Materials and experiments

## 2.1. Materials

The corn stover was purchased from Lianyungang, Jiangsu Province. Mercuric  chloride,  sublimated  sulfur  and  zinc  chloride  for  modified biochar were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd. The chemicals and reagents used for adsorption experiments, including nitric acid, hydrochloric acid, potassium hydroxide, and sodium hydroxide, were purchased from Shanghai Xilong Scientific Co. Ltd. Potassium borohydride and potassium dichromate were procured  from  Shanghai  Comio  Chemical  Reagents  Co.  Ltd.  Ultra-pure water (Hitech, China, 18.25 M Ω /cm) was used throughout all experimental procedures.

## 2.2. Synthesis of stabilizers

SZ-BC  was  prepared  by  impregnating  corn  stover  powder  with  a ZnCl2  solution, followed by co-pyrolysis with sublimation sulfur. The mass ratio used was 1:1:1 for corn stover powder, ZnCl2, and sublimation sulfur. Firstly, the corn stover powder was mixed with ZnCl2 solution, impregnated and shaken for 24 h, then extracted and dried at 60 ◦ C for 24 h to obtain the pyrolysis precursor. Secondly, the pyrolysis precursor was then mixed with sublimation sulfur powder in a centrifuge tube, evenly distributed in a crucible, and subjected to pyrolysis in a tubular furnace. Under a pure N2 atmosphere (99.99 % purity, gas flow was 80 mL/min), the composite material was maintained at 450 ◦ C for 1  h.  Finally,  to  remove  excess  ZnCl2 from  the  material  surface,  the composite material was flushed with 1 M hydrochloric acid. Afterwards, it was washed with ultrapure water until a constant pH was reached. The purified biochar material was then dried, ground, sieved and bagged to obtain SZ-BC. SBC was produced without impregnation with a ZnCl ₂ solution, while ZBC was produced without sublimated sulfur doping. The other processes were identical to those described above.

## 2.3. Adsorption experiments

The adsorption experiments were conducted at room temperature (25 ◦ C) on a mechanical shaker rotating at 180 rpm (Lyu et al., 2022). The pH value of mercury solution was adjusted to 6.00 ± 0.05 with 0.1 M nitric acid and 0.1 M sodium hydroxide. To investigate the kinetics of mercury adsorption by biochar-based materials, 100 mg of BC, SBC,  ZBC,  and  SZ-BC  materials  were  individually  introduced  into  a 200 mL solution containing 10 mg/L of HgCl2. Samples were collected at various time intervals. With regard to the mercury adsorption isotherm experiments, 100 mg of biochar-based materials was added to 200 mL of HgCl ₂ solution at varying concentrations (1, 2, 4, 8, 10, 20, 30, and 40 mg/L), respectively. All the solution samples were extracted with a 5  mL  syringe,  and  filtered  through  a  0.22 µ m filter  membrane.  The concentration of mercury solution was determined by Atomic Fluorescence  Spectrometry  (AFS)  (AF-2200,  Beifen-Ruili,  China).  All  above experiments were carried out three times under identical conditions to calculate the standard errors.

## 2.4. Soil static culture experiment

Surface soil (0 -20 cm) was collected from a wasteland in Nanchang, Jiangxi  Province  (N28 ◦ 43 ′ ,  E115 ◦ 49 ′ ).  Table  S1  shows  the  basic  soil properties. The collected soil were dried in the air, finely ground, and sifted through a 10-mesh screen before being stored in bags. To prepare mercury-contaminated  soil,  HgCl2  solution  was  mixed  with  the  soil, stirred well, and the supernatant was discarded after 24 h of settling. Following air-drying, the soil sample had a mercury concentration of 6.75 mg/kg. In each experimental batch, the prepared BC, SBC, ZBC, and SZ-BC were evenly mixed with 80.0 g of soil at dosages of 1 %, 2 %, and 5 % mass ratio, respectively. A control group was also established. Three  replicate  groups  were  established  for  all  groups.  The  culture dishes were placed in an incubator at 25 ± 1 ◦ C, with deionized water added based on weight to maintain soil moisture at 50 ± 5 %, replenished every 2 days. The soil samples underwent a four-week incubation period, with weekly sampling to assess leaching toxicity. Untreated soil was labeled as CK, while soil treated with 1 %, 2 %, and 5 % SZ-BC were labeled as SZ1, SZ2, and SZ3, respectively. Similar labeling was applied to the BC, SBC, and ZBC groups.

## 2.6. Pot experiment

The SEM images of BC and SZ-BC were shown in Fig. 2. BC exhibits a stem-like honeycomb pore structure with distinct surface pore outlines (Fig.  2a).  However,  the  structure  appears  to  be  loosely  packed  and predominantly macroporous, no micropores are observed. Compared to BC, SBC contains more fine particles on the pore surface. This may be due to the successful loading of elemental sulfur on the surface of biochar.  (Fig.  2b).  Furthermore,  the  pyrolysis  and  evaporation  of  zinc chloride  can  create  pore  structures  of  varying  sizes  and  fine  crack structures in the material, resulting in a significant increase in the surface porosity of ZBC (Fig. 2c). By analyzing the SEM image of SZ-BC (Fig. 2d), a greater number of micropores on the surface of the sulfur/ zinc co-modified biochar was observed. Additionally, the surface of the material exhibited a rougher texture and was loaded with numerous particles of varying shapes, indicating the successful loading of sulfur and zinc substances. Significantly, all three biochar materials, SBC, ZBC and SZ-BC, had particulate matter attached to their surfaces.

All materials exhibit isothermal adsorption lines of type IV with H4-type hysteresis  loops,  which  are  typically  found  on  adsorbents  with  a mixture of micropores and mesopores and in solids containing narrow cleavage pores. Fig. 3a presents the pore structure and specific surface area parameters of different biochar materials. The specific surface areas of SBC, ZBC and SZ-BC are 48.71, 577.85 and 102.15 m 2 /g, respectively, which are 2.90, 33.95, and 6.00 times higher than that of BC. Similarly, the micropore specific surface areas of SBC, ZBC, and SZ-BC are 31.79, 359.71, and 59.38 m 2 /g, respectively, which are 3.70, 41.53, and 6.90 times  higher  than  that  of  BC,  indicating  that  the  co-modification  of sulfur  and  zinc  significantly  increased  the  number  of  micropores  in biochar. Combined with the SEM characterization results, the successful loading of sulfur and zinc substances onto the biochar surface increased its specific surface area. The average pore size and total pore volume of BC were 4.8621 nm and 0.0207 cm 3 /g, respectively, which is consistent with the SEM results showing predominantly mesoporous and macroporous pore structures (Qambrani et al., 2017). Meanwhile, the total pore volume of SZ-BC increased to 2.81 times that of BC, and the average pore size of SZ-BC decreased to 0.47 times that of BC, further indicating that  sulfur  and  zinc  enhanced  the  pore  development  of  the  biochar material and formed more micropores and mesopores during the pyrolysis process.

## 3.2. Adsorption kinetics

The fitted kinetic curves of mercury adsorption by BC, SBC, ZBC, and SZ-BC are presented in Fig. 3c. The results indicate that the mercury adsorption by four materials increased rapidly within the first 60 min and  reached  equilibrium  after  240 min.  This  phenomenon  can  be explained by the greater number of adsorption sites that are available on the  biochar-based  materials  during  the  initial  stages  of  adsorption. Consequently, mercury can be readily adsorbed on the surface of the material and quickly bound to its active sites. During the progression of adsorption,  mercury  gradually  occupied  the  pores  and  functional adsorption sites on the material. This resulted in an increase in electrostatic  repulsion  between  the  ions,  leading  to  the  attainment  of adsorption equilibrium and saturation of adsorption capacity. Table S2 lists the fitted parameters of the adsorption kinetic models for the four biochar materials. The adsorption process of mercury by biochar materials is more consistent with the pseudo-second-order kinetic model. Thus, the adsorption of mercury in water by biochar-based materials is primarily  a  chemisorption-dominated  process,  rather  than  a  single physisorption process. Meanwhile, the theoretical equilibrium adsorption  capacity  of  SZ-BC  for  mercury  obtained  by  fitting  the  pseudosecond-order kinetic model was 19.267 mg/g, which was close to the actual equilibrium adsorption capacity obtained from the experiments and significantly greater than the theoretical adsorption capacity of BC (12.466 mg/g), indicating that the modified biochar (SZ-BC) had a more excellent removal effect of mercury in water.

## 3.7.2. Effect of stabilizers on soil enzyme activities

Soil enzyme activities can be influenced by heavy metal contamination, leading to a reduction in their activity. Among the soil enzymes, urease, acid phosphatase, and dehydrogenase are particularly sensitive to heavy metals (Song et al., 2022). Urease plays a crucial role in hydrolyzing urea in the soil, reflecting the soil ' s nitrogen supply. Catalase functions to break down hydrogen peroxide in the soil, serving as a key indicator of soil metabolism and organic matter accumulation. Dehydrogenase has a similar function, while phosphatase aids in breaking down organophosphorus compounds, acting as an indicator of phosphorus biotransformation in the soil. Following SZ-BC treatment, the activity of these enzymes in soil nearly doubled compared to the CK group (Table S5), suggesting that SZ-BC effectively reduced the toxic effects of mercury on enzymes and bioavailability, improved soil quality and promoted plant growth.

## 3.7.3. Effects of stabilizers on microbial community composition

Figure S4 displays the composition of the microbial community in the soil  of  various  treatment  groups.  At  the  T1  group,  the  untreated mercury-contaminated soil was dominated by four microbial communities:  Bacteroidetes,  Firmicutes,  Proteobacteria,  and  Actinobacteria. Previous  studies  have  shown  that  Bacteroidetes,  Proteobacteria,  and Actinobacteria  are  the  dominant  bacterial  phyla  in  heavy  metalcontaminated  soils.  These  bacteria  are  able  to  resist  the  toxicity  of metals  through  their  adsorption  and  complexation  capabilities  (Liu et al., 2018). In addition, soil microbes can adapt to varying forms and concentrations of heavy metals in the soil by altering the abundance and structure of their microbial communities (Hao et al., 2022). Therefore, following  SZ-BC  treatment  (T2),  the  relative  abundance  of  the  four dominant  phyla  decreased,  while  the  relative  abundance  of  Thaumarchaeota,  Acidobacteria,  and  Chloroflexi  increased. Characterization of mercury-contaminated soil before and after SZBC treatment using XPS provided initial insights into the stabilization mechanism of SZ-BC on mercury in soil (Fig. 6a-d). The addition of SZBC to the soil resulted in changes to the material form and composition, leading to the emergence of new characteristic peaks. In the C1s spectrum (Fig.  6b),  the  CK  group  displayed  three  characteristic  peaks  at 284.8 eV, 286.3 eV, and 289.0 eV, corresponding to C-C, C-O, and OC --O, respectively. The intensity of these peaks in the SZ-BC group was significantly weaker than that of the CK group. Additionally, a peak of newly generated metal carbonate (531.7 eV) was observed in the O1s spectrum  (Fig.  6c).  This  suggests  that  the  introduction  of  SZ-BC

facilitated the binding of mercury with minerals in the soil, promoting the formation of precipitates such as HgCO3  and thereby reducing the mobility of mercury in the soil. Moreover, the addition of SZ-BC resulted in  an  increase  of  oxygen-containing  functional  groups  (silica,  metal carbonate, metal oxide, -OH) in the soil (Fig. 6c), indicating that the stabilizer could complex soil mercury through oxygen-containing functional  groups.  Meanwhile,  the  abundance  of  alkaline  substances  like metal oxides and carbonates present in biochar-based materials could promote the precipitation of soil mercury. Apart from that, the formation of Hg-S precipitation in the soil after SZ-BC treatment (Fig. 6d). In summary, the mechanisms by which SZ-BC stabilizes mercury in soil are illustrated in Fig. 7. Firstly, the negative charge on the surface of biochar in  soil  solutions  attracts  positively  charged  Hg(II)  ions  through  electrostatic  forces.  Secondly,  acidic  functional  groups  like  hydroxyl  and carboxyl groups on the biochar surface facilitate ion exchange with Hg (II)  ions  for  immobilization.  Thirdly,  oxygen-containing  functional groups  on  the  biochar  surface  form  complexes  with  Hg(II).  Finally, sulfur-containing functional groups in the stabilizer react with watersoluble and exchangeable mercury in the soil to form Hg-S precipitates.

## 4. Conclusions

In summary, a modified biochar co-doped with sulfur and zinc was synthesized, which has a higher adsorption capacity and better stabilization effects compared to the pristine biochar. The maximum adsorption capacity of SZ-BC is 16.5 times greater than that of BC according to adsorption isotherm analysis. Static culture experiments revealed that SZ-BC  effectively  reduces  the  leaching  risk  of  mercury  in  mercurycontaminated  soil,  resulting  in  a  74.17 %  lower  leaching  concentration of mercury in soil compared to the CK group. Dynamic leaching and speciation analyses indicated that SZ-BC increases the concentration of mercury  in  the  residual  state  in  soil  and  shows  strong  resistance  to

leaching caused by acid rain. The results of plant potting experiments, enzyme activity tests, and microbial community composition analysis demonstrate that SZ-BC can effectively reduce ecological risks, promote plant  growth,  increase  enzyme  activity,  and  enhance  microbial  diversity. Following SZ-BC treatment, the potential ecological risk index of mercury was notably decreased by 92 % compared to the CK group. These findings suggest the feasibility of SZ-BC as a stabilizing repair material.
