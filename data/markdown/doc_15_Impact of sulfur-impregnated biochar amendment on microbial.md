S-impregnation of biochar through elemental S streaming is known to increase its sorption performance against Hg and methyl mercury (MeHg). However, the effects of %S-loading on biochar ' s mechanism and sorption capacities for MeHg, and its consequent impact when used as an amendment material for Hg-contaminated sediments, are poorly understood, and thus, were investigated in this work. Our results showed that a minimum sulfur loading of 1% was the most effective in reducing MeHg levels in sediments. At higher %S-loading (3 -20%), the reduction in surface area, pore blockage due to unreacted sulfur particles, and presence of poorly bound sulfur  species  resulted  in  lowered  effectiveness  for  MeHg  control.  Increasing  S-functionalization  during impregnation shifted the sorption process of MeHg from Hg-O to Hg-S in S-impregnated biochar (BCS). Our 60day slurry experiment showed a significant reduction in pore water THg (40 -70%) and MeHg (30 -55%), as well as sediment MeHg (50 -60%) in biochar-amended sediments. The reduction in the bioavailable Hg resulted in lowered Hg methylation, as supported by the suppression of both the Fe- and SO4 2-reduction activities in the amended sediments. The microbial community structure in BCS-amended sediments showed a shift towards sulfur-consuming, iron-reducing, thiosulfate-oxidizing, and sulfate-reducing bacterial populations. ## 1. Environmental Implication

Sediment contamination by mercury (Hg) and the transformation of Hg into methyl mercury (MeHg) have been highlighted as health concerns because of the increased toxicity and bio-accumulative potential of the latter. This study highlights the application of S-impregnated biochar (BCS) as a promising strategy for remediation of Hg-contaminated sediments. ## 2. Introduction

Sediment contamination by mercury (Hg) and the transformation of Hg into methyl mercury (MeHg) are growing health concerns, owing to the increased toxicity and bio-accumulative potential of the latter (Liu et  al.,  2018b;  O ' Connor et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021). Major contributors to Hg contamination of aquatic environments include anthropogenic activities, such as gold mining, ore refining, and chlor-alkali industrial processes (Randall and Chattopadhyay, 2013). Sediments act as repositories for Hg contaminants and provide favorable conditions for the transformation of inorganic Hg to MeHg (Zhu et al., 2018). These contaminated sediments eventually become significant sources of Hg and MeHg in aquatic environments, through various re-emission processes, including re-suspension, diffusion, and accumulation in aquatic organisms (Zhang et al., 2021). Therefore, it is important to develop management  tools  and  strategies  to  overcome  water  and  sediment contamination  (Wang  et  al.,  2020).  One  of  the  materials  that  has received considerable attention in sediment remediation because of its effectiveness in mercury adsorption and carbon sequestration potential is biochar (BC) (Beckers et al., 2019; Shaheen et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018).

Depending on the feedstock and production conditions, BCs can have varying physical and chemical properties, such as surface area, hydrophobicity, microporosity, and functional groups. These properties affect the  sorption  mechanism  and  contaminant-removal  capacities  of  BC (Rajapaksha et al., 2016; Sizmur et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). In addition, chemical modification of BC significantly increases its sorption performance against heavy metals. FeCl3 activation of peanut hull BC increased its adsorption capacity for Cr from 900 to 77, 542 mg/kg (Han et al., 2016), while MnO2 and birnessite modification of pinewood BC improved its removal performance for As and Pb (Wang et  al.,  2015).  Sulfur-iron  modification  of  BC  resulted  to  increased immobilization of Cd (by 6%), when applied as soil amendment (Wu et al., 2019). However, acid and alkaline modification of BC can improve heavy metal sorption by increasing the surface area of BC (Ahmed et al., 2016; Panahi et al., 2020).

Sulfur  is  one  of  the  most  studied  modifiers  of  Hg.  S-modification significantly improves the Hg removal performance of BC, by increasing its complexation with S-functional groups, such as thiophene, C --S, CSOx-C, and sulfoxide (Huang et al., 2019a; Park et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2009) and by increasing HgS precipitation (Liu et al., 2018c; O ' Connor et  al.,  2018;  Park  et  al.,  2019;  Zhao  et  al.,  2020).  Although  several studies have been conducted on S-modified BC for Hg management, the effect of %S-modification and its consequent impact on MeHg removal performance of BC are not yet fully understood. In addition, there is a need to further explore the specific mechanisms of MeHg sorption in water and its impact on Hg methylation in sediments, when applied as an  amendment  material.  S-modification  can  be  achieved  through various  methods,  including  H2S  plasma  modification  (Zhang  et  al.,

In this study, sulfur-impregnated pine-needle BC (BCS) was prepared by means of BC post-treatment via elemental S streaming. Elemental S streaming was employed because of its simple methodology and the low amount of waste generated in the process. The current study employed a semi-isolated system, as an improvement over our previous work (Jeon et al., 2020), to reduce the mass loss of S during sorbent preparation. Our previous work showed that the sulfur modification of pine-needle BC was effective in increasing the sorption capacity of BC to inorganic Hg in water. However, the reduction efficiency and removal mechanism of MeHg from water have not been investigated. The overall aim of the current study was to evaluate the effect of %S-loading on the physicochemical properties and sorption mechanism of BC against MeHg, and to test its potential as an amendment material for MeHg management in contaminated sediment. To better understand the underlying biogeochemical processes that mitigate Hg risks in sediments, the sediment microbial community structure was also studied using a metagenomics approach. The S-loading ratios are critical to this study because the increase in S-functionalization with increasing %S-loading is expected to improve  the  sorption  performance  of  BCS.  However,  pore  structure transformation may occur during sorbent synthesis, potentially affecting the sorption behavior of the sorbent.

## 3. Materials and methods

## 3.1. Chemicals and reagents

Sulfur, ascorbic acid, and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were purchased from Acros Organics (USA). Hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, and nitric acid were supplied by Fisher Chemicals (Canada). Sodium  hydroxide  was  purchased  from  Thermo  Fisher  Scientific  (UK), while N-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine-N ' -(2-ethanesulfonic acid) (HEPES) buffer solution was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Potassium chloride was purchased from Merck (Germany). The estuarine sediment  reference  material  (ERM-CC580)  was  purchased  from  the Institute for Reference Materials and Measurements (Belgium). MeHg and total Hg standard solutions were supplied by Brooks Rand (USA).

## 3.2. BC synthesis, S-impregnation, and characterization

BC was prepared from pine-needles collected from pines in North Gyeongsan  Province,  South  Korea,  through  pyrolysis  at  500 ◦ C  in  a Lindberg furnace. The needles were first washed with deionized (DI) water before oven-drying at 80 ◦ C overnight. Dried pine-needles were packed in a gas-tight steel reactor, with a length of 30 cm and a diameter of 5 cm, and purged with high-purity N2 gas, at a flow rate of 100 mL/ min, for 2 h (Jeon et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2017). The furnace temperature was then increased at a ramping rate of 7.58 ◦ C and maintained at the peak temperature for 6 h, under continuous N2  gas flow. The produced BC, labeled as BCS0, was then cooled and ground to a 160 µ m mesh size and subjected to S-impregnation.

S-impregnation  was  performed  at  various  loading  ratios  of:  1% (BCS1); 3% (BCS3); 5% (BCS5); 10% (BCS10); and 20% (BCS20). Ten grams of BC-sulfur mix was loaded into a stainless-steel reactor with a length of 30 cm and a diameter of 1.27 cm (Fig. S1 in the supporting material).  After  the  samples  were  loaded,  the  ends  of  the  tube  were capped  with  glass  wool,  before  securing  both  ends  with  lock-fitting

To ensure that the samples were dried prior to pyrolysis, the system was heated to 100 ◦ C at a ramping rate of 20 ◦ C/min and maintained at this temperature for 1 h. After drying, the valves were closed at both ends and the samples were pyrolyzed at 500 ◦ C for 5 h (ramping rate = 6.7 ◦ C/ min).

All  produced  adsorbents  (BCS)  were  stored  in  brown  bottles  and packed in double-sealed zipper bags to avoid contamination of the sorbents prior to the exposure experiments. These BCS were then characterized  using  an  elemental  analyzer  (Elementar  Analysensysteme GmbH) for elemental composition, using sulfanilic acid and benzoic acid standards.  Functional  group  analysis  of  the  samples  was  performed using  Fourier  transform-infrared  spectroscopy  (FT-IR)  (Vertex  80  v, Bruker) by means of the KBr method (Li et al., 2020). The specific surface area of BCS was measured using an adsorption analyzer (Micromeritics 3Flex version 5.00) and calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller  isotherm.  The  pore  volume  and  pore  size were determined by means of the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda method, using the N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm data at 77 K. Thermal stability of the synthesized BCS was studied using simultaneous differential scanning  calorimetry  and  thermogravimetric  analysis  (SDT  Q600  V20.9 Build  20).  Surface  imaging  and  elemental  mapping  were  conducted using  a  high-resolution  field  emission  scanning  electron  microscope (JSM 7800 F PRIME with Dual EDS) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray analyzer. The possible adsorption mechanisms were studied using X-ray  photoelectron  spectroscopy  (XPS;  AXIS  Ultra-DLD,  Kratos  Inc.) with monochromatic Al K α X-rays (h υ = 1586.6 eV), at the Korea Basic Science Institute. Confocal Raman spectroscopy was performed using an XperRam 200 ™ (NanoBase) instrument at a 532 nm laser wavelength. The operation of some characterization methods can be found in the literature  (Chen  et  al.,  2022;  Rao  et  al.,  2022;  Zhang  et  al.,  2022a, 2022b).

## 3.4. Microcosm experiment with BC amendment

The microcosm experiment was designed to evaluate the effect of BCS  amendment  on  Hg-contaminated  sediment  maintained  under anoxic  conditions.  Hg-contaminated  sediments  collected  from  the Hyeongsan River estuary in South Korea were used because of their high levels  of  Hg  and  MeHg  (Bailon  et  al.,  2018).  Inside  the  anaerobic chamber, wet sediment equivalent to 10 g of dry sediment was added to 40 mL acid-cleaned serum bottles. The adsorbent (5%, based on the dry weight of the sediment) was then added to all serum bottles, except to those of the control group. High-purity DI water (25 mL) was then added to the mixture, before capping it with a silicon septum and sealing it with an aluminum crimper. The slurries were mixed well and left inside an anaerobic chamber for 60 d. Four replicate reactors were prepared for each treatment and the control group. The bottles were shaken regularly to thoroughly mix the contents and prevent the formation of a localized anaerobic layer in the sediment. The reactors were kept inside a dark box  during  the  entire  experiment,  to  prevent  photo-oxidation  of  the sediment.

After incubation, the bottles were allowed to settle, and then opened inside the anaerobic chamber. Overlying water (henceforth referred to as ' pore  water ' )  was  immediately  filtered  through  a  0.45 µ m  polysulfone filter, and the filtrate was parsed and preserved for total THg, MeHg, Fe 2 + , S 2, and other anions. An unfiltered aliquot was used for pH and  conductivity  measurements.  The  solids  remaining  in  the  bottles were collected, frozen, and freeze-dried for THg and MeHg analysis.

## 3.5. Pore water analysis

For THg analysis, water samples were first digested and stabilized with 2% BrCl in concentrated HCl for 24 h. THg was then determined using cold vapor atomic fluorescence spectroscopy (CVAFS, Model III, Brooks  Rand),  following  the  EPA  method  1631  (Ting  et  al.,  2018; USEPA, 2002). MeHg analysis was conducted using pre-treatment and analysis  procedures  based  on  the  EPA  1630  method  (USEPA,  1998). Briefly, 4 mL water samples were diluted to 25 mL before adding 0.2 mL of 50% H2SO4 (trace metal grade) and 0.1 mL of 20% KCl, distilled, and then analyzed using CVAFS.

The filtered water samples were directly analyzed for Fe 2 + , S 2, and other  anions.  Fe 2 + was  analyzed  using  the  phenanthroline  method (Eaton et al., 1995). Briefly, 2 mL of the water samples were mixed with 0.8 mL  0.1%  phenanthroline  reagent  and  0.4 mL  ammonium  acetate buffer solution. The iron concentrations were then compared to the results  of  calibration  using  a  standard  iron  solution.  The  sulfide  (S 2-) concentration in water was determined using an Orion ™ silver/sulfide ion-selective electrode (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sulfide anti-oxidant buffer (SAOB) was prepared to a final solution containing 2 M NaOH, 0.1 M ascorbic acid, and 0.1 M EDTA (Brouwer and Murphy, 1994). Three milliliters of pore water was mixed with 100 µ L of SAOB prior to the ORP measurement using the sulfide ion-selective electrode. NO3 -and SO4 2in the pore water were measured using a 4 mm × 250 mm Dionex AS25 column fitted to a Dionex ICS-1100 ion chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The analysis was conducted at an isocratic

## 4. Results and discussion

## 3.6. Sediment analysis

The  THg  and  MeHg  concentrations  of  sediment  samples  from different reactors were determined after a 60-d incubation period. To determine THg concentrations, 20 mg of freeze-dried sediment samples was  analyzed  using  a  direct  mercury  analyzer  (DMA-80,  Milestone, Germany). MeHg analysis was performed using the pre-treatment and analysis procedures described in EPA 1630. In brief, sample extraction for MeHg analysis from freeze-dried sediment samples was conducted by dissolving the sediment in a KCl/H2SO4  mixture and distilling it until 90 -95% recovery  was  achieved.  The  distillates  were  analyzed  using CVAFS. Detailed analytical methods for estimating MeHg are provided in the Supplementary Information (Box S1). The recoveries for both THg and MeHg were compared with the standard reference material ERMCC580 and were within 5% of the certified values.

## 3.7. Analysis of the bacterial community structure

The impacts of BCS amendments on the bacterial communities of anoxic  sediments  were  analyzed  using  the  16 S  rRNA  metagenomics sequencing approach. The sediment DNA was extracted from the control,  BCS0, BCS1, BCS3, BCS5, BCS10, and BCS20 slurry microcosms using a FastDNA SPIN kit (MP Biomedicals, France). DNA quality was assessed  using  1.5%  agarose  gel  electrophoresis  and  a  MaestroNano spectrophotometer (model name: MN-913; MaestroGen). Amplification of  the  16 S  rRNA  V3 -V4 region was performed using a Herculase II Fusion DNA Polymerase Nextera XT Index Kit (Illumina, CA, USA). All amplified  PCR  products  were  sequenced  to  obtain  paired-end  reads using the Illumina MiSeq platform at the Macrogen, Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). The resulting sequenced data were processed and analyzed according to the NGS Analysis Manual (QIIME manual) and EzBioCloud ' s 16S-based MTP pipeline (https://www.ezbiocloud.net/contents/16smt p) (Caporaso et al., 2010). The raw 16 S rRNA metagenome sequence data  were  deposited  in  the  NCBI  GenBank  database,  under  the  BioProject accession number PRJNA689206.

## 3.8. Statistical analysis

## 4.1. Material characterization

## 4.1.1. Elemental analysis and pore structure characterization of biochar

To investigate the effects of post pyrolysis modification on the biochar,  elemental  analysis  and  pore  structure  characterization  using Brunauer-Emmet-Teller (BET) and Barret-Joyner-Holanda (BJH) methods were done on pristine and modified biochar. The measured properties are presented in Table 1. Heating biochar-sulfur mixture at 500 ◦ C, which is above the boiling point of elemental S, allowed for successful S impregnation to the biochar matrix. The modification process successfully increased the S content of BC with minimal mass loss of S observed in all modified biochar. The %S in the modified sorbents was close  to  the  target  %S-loading  of  1 -20%,  with  BCS20  showing  the highest %S-loss due to possible formation of sulfur dioxide or release of excess gas-phase S2-S8, when complete monolayer surface coverage was achieved during impregnation. Reductions in H/C, O/C, and (O + N/C) ratios  were  observed,  suggesting  the  formation  of  more  aromatic structures, a decrease in surface polar functional groups, and an increase in surface hydrophobicity (Chen et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2017).

The surface area (SA) of BCS0 was 248 m 2 /g, with 97.5% attributed to the micropores. At 1% S-loading, the SA increased to 297.8 m 2 /g, possibly due to an effect of the post-treatment used to aid in S impregnation. An additional heating step under oxygen-deficient conditions can cause the removal of additional volatile matter (O ' Connor et al., 2018).  At  much  higher  %S-loading,  the  SA  decreased  exponentially, with BCS5, BCS10, and BCS20 having SA values of 3.558, 2.954, and 2.548 m 2 /g, respectively. The reduction in the SA thereafter could be the result of the collapse or blockage of micropores, as evident in the reduction of the micropore contribution to the SA (Ting et al., 2018). The pore diameter of the pine-needle BC increased from 2.18 nm to about 16 nm in 10% and 20% S-loaded BC. During pyrolysis, the pore structure may collapse or coalesce, leading to either smaller or larger pores. Nonetheless, the pore diameters of the sorbents prepared in this study were within the 2 -50 nm mesopore range, which is considered favorable for metal adsorption (Jeon et al., 2020).

Statistical  analyses  were  conducted  using  SigmaPlot  ver.  12.5,  to evaluate the effect of BCS amendment on THg and MeHg levels in the water  and  sediment  from  reactor  systems.  Specifically,  a  one-way analysis of variance using a Holm -Sidak multiple comparison test at the 95% confidence interval was performed to identify statistically significant  differences  between  the  BCS  treatments  for  the  different datasets.

Scanning  electron  microscopy  (SEM)  was  used  to  obtain  highresolution  images  of  the  surface  of  the  biochar.  The  SEM  images (Figs. S2 and S3) of pristine BC (BCS0) showed an uneven surface, with the presence of folded and distorted sheets in the form of cracks and crevices. The number of these pores may increase or decrease owing to structural reordering, coalescence of neighboring pores, collapse of microporous structures, and blockage due to occlusion of volatiles and other materials in the micropore structure. ## 4.1.3. Infrared spectroscopy of biochar

It is important to study the different functional groups present on the surface of BC, because the sorption behavior of the material depends on the type of functional groups present (Shaheen et al., 2019; Sizmur et al., 2016). Several studies have shown that S-impregnation is effective in enhancing the removal performance of BC for Hg pollutants, because of increased S-functionalization (Tan et al., 2016; Ting et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2020). It has also been documented that the BC particles with higher S content present higher tendency that the Hg will be bound to S (Jeon et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2016). The FT-IR spectra (Fig. 1) show decreased intensities for the -OH stretch (3400 -3450 cm -1 ) and C --O (1650 cm -1 )  during S functionalization. Furthermore, a closer examination of the FT-IR spectra (Fig. S4) showed increased intensities for the sulfone -C --S, sulfate -S --O stretch (1080 -1150 cm -1 ), and ester S-OR peaks (680 -750 cm -1 ), confirming increased S functionalization during the modification process (Segneanu et al., 2012). The slight increase in the  peaks  at  896,  1164,  and  1319 cm -1 after  S-impregnation  can  be attributed to the S-O stretching of sulfonate, symmetric stretching vibration of SO2 on sulfonic acid, and anti-symmetric stretching vibration of SO2 on sulfone, respectively (Zhang et al., 2019). A more prominent C-S stretching peak was also observed at 1401 cm -1 , after modification. These observations confirm the successful integration of the S modifier into the sorbent.

## 4.1.4. Raman spectroscopy of biochar

Alterations  in  the  chemical  structural  features  of  biochar  were further investigated using Raman Spectroscopy. In the Raman spectrum (Fig.  1),  the  characteristic  peaks  around  1580  and  1330 cm -1 corresponded to the G (graphitic) and D (disorder-induced) bands, respectively. The G mode is related to the E2 g optical mode and involves the in- plane  bond-stretching  motion  of  the  C  sp 2 pairs  present  in  graphitic carbon (Ferrari, 2007; Reich and Thomsen, 2004). In contrast, the D peak is related to the breathing mode of A1 g symmetry in phonons near the K zone boundary, which is forbidden in perfect graphite. Such modes appear  to  be  active  only  in  the  presence  of  a  disorder  (Ferrari  and Robertson, 2000). The relatively narrow characteristic of the G band compared to that of the D band indicates the crystalline component of carbons (Jawhari et al., 1995). The curve fitting of the Raman spectra (Fig. S5) for all samples showed two second-order weak bands between the D and G bands, labeled V and GL. The V band (1380 -1450 cm -1 ) represents amorphous carbon structures, methylene, methyl, and carbon structures  that  link  aromatic  structures  (Igalavithana  et  al.,  2020;  Li et al., 2006), whereas the GL  band (1450 -1490 cm -1 )  represents aromatics with 3 -5 rings and amorphous carbon structures (Dissanayake et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2016). In addition, the S band (1150 -1230 cm -1 ) on the left of the D band represents sp 2 and sp 3 carbons related to the C-H  on  aromatic  rings,  aromatic,  or  aliphatic  ethers,  and  C-C  on hydroaromatic rings (Li et al., 2006).

To quantify the defect degree of the carbon materials, the intensity ratio  of  ID/IG  are  measured for all  adsorbents  (Liu et  al.,  2018a),  as shown in Fig. 1 and Table S2. Based on the Voigt fitting results, the ID/IG ratio was found to increase with increasing %S-loading, confirming an increase in the degree of defects in the carbon structure. Since there was no distinct peak between 0 and 500 cm -1 , corresponding to pure S in all modified BC, it can be inferred that most of the S(0) was successfully integrated in BCS1-BCS20 (Xia et al., 2020) and the increase in defects was due to adulteration  of  S-functional  groups  on  the  surface  of  BC (Mendonça et al., 2017; Tang et al., 2015), which was also confirmed by the results of the FT-IR analysis.

## 4.1.5. Thermal stability and S-leaching behavior of biochar

The thermal stability  of  the  prepared  BC  was  studied  using  thermogravimetric analysis (Fig. S6). No significant weight loss due to H2O was observed at 25 -100 ◦ C. Minimal S loss at 200 -400 ◦ C was observed for BCS with low %S-loading (1 -5%). Pore blockage due to unreacted sulfur particles occurred for BCS10 and BCS20 showing significant mass loss of S (1 -4%) in the TGA spectra. This was similar to the literature, where sulfur sublimation in the S-microporous material in the temperature range of 200 -400 ◦ C was suppressed due to the S8 ring undergoing molecular  transformation,  to  form  long-range  ordered  and  large domain-sized 1D sulfur chains in pores with diameters of 1 nm. S atoms are  covalently  linked,  stabilizing  the  sulfur  species  inside  the  pores (Fujimori et al., 2013; Takahashi et al., 2015).

## 4.2. Isotherm and kinetic studies of MeHg on BCS

## 4.2.1. Isotherm experiments

Batch sorption experiment was conducted to determine the MeHg sorption capacities of the different sorbents. To determine the effect of S (0) functionalization on MeHg uptake, all sorbents were exposed to solutions containing different initial concentrations of MeHg (100 μ g/L to 100 mg/L) for 24 h. The results revealed that MeHg was undetected in all sorbents prior to the exposure test, indicating that the initial MeHg concentration in the sorbent did not affect the sorption process during the experiment. The adsorption parameters are summarized in Table 2 and the isotherm-fitted data are shown in Fig. S9. The highest MeHg uptake was observed in BCS1, followed by BCS3. The 1/n values obtained for all sorbents were within the range of 0.5 -0.6, which showed that the MeHg adsorption isotherm had a high curvature for all sorbents. The  values  of  n  were  then  between  1  and  10,  indicating  favorable adsorption (Akpomie et al., 2015).

Highest  KF values  were  observed  for  BCS  with  low  %S-loading (1 -3%). BCS1 (5.91) showed more than a two-fold increase in sorption capacity compared to that of BCS0 (2.43). BCS3 (4.27) and BCS5 (3.98) also  showed higher KF  values,  which is  due  to  the  increased  S-functionalization, as confirmed by the increased intensities of -C --S, -S = 0, C-S, and -SOR in the FT-IR spectra of BCS1, BCS3, and BCS5. Furthermore, the increase in the sorption capacity of modified BC confirmed the findings from previous literature that the thiophene group and S(0) were the dominant S species in BCS. These groups are known to form more stable bonds with Hg than sulfoxide groups (Jeon et al., 2020). At much higher  %S-loadings  of  10%  and  20%,  the  obtained  KF values  were relatively lower than those of the control group, which could be due to the significant reduction in SA, as described in the previous section.

The maximum MeHg loading value (Qe) of the 50 mg/L solution was obtained from batch adsorption studies. Relative to BCS0, higher Qe values were recorded for BCS1 (27.07 mg/g), BCS3 (18.79 mg/g), BCS5 (26.43 mg/g),  and  BCS10  (20.37 mg/g).  These  loading  values  were approximately  1.5 -2  times  higher  than  those  of  BCS0  (11.24 mg/g). BCS20, on the other hand, showed a significantly reduced Qe  value of 3.75 mg/g. ## 4.2.2. Kinetics experiment

To  further  understand  the  adsorption  behavior  of  MeHg  on  BCS, kinetics experiment was performed (Fig. 2 and Table S3). The pseudosecond-order kinetics model best described the adsorption kinetics of 100 mg/L MeHg at pH 7 and 20 ◦ C for all sorbents. This observation confirmed that the primary mechanism for the adsorption process was chemisorption. Our previous study reported that Hg(II) primarily binds to  the  O-functional  groups  in  BC.  The  binding  shifts  to  S-containing functional groups in S-modified BC, owing to the strong complexation of Hg with S species (Jeon et al., 2020; Ting et al., 2018). In addition, CH3Hg + binds to the -SH group in modified BC, forming an S-HgCH3 complex (Huang et al., 2019b). The MeHg adsorption for BCS0 reached the maximum value of 0.07 mg/g-h at 24 h. This rate of sorption was significantly increased by 1.5 -5 × for all the sulfur-modified BCs. The magnitude of the rate constants followed the trend BCS1 (0.36) &gt; BCS5 (0.22) &gt; BCS3 (0.29) &gt; BCS10 (0.10) &gt; BCS20 (0.10). Similarly, the Cs value after 24 h was observed to be the highest for BCS1 (3.95 mg/g), followed  by  BCS3  (3.73 mg/g)  and  BCS5  (3.49 mg/g).  These  values were 5 -20% higher than those of BCS0 (3.32 mg/g). Lower rate constants were obtained for BCS10 (2.08 mg/g) and BCS20 (1.66 mg/g), indicating that despite increased S-functionalization, the reduced surface  area  for  sorption  led  to  a  significant  reduction  in  the  sorption capacity.

## 4.5. Effect of BCS amendment on bacterial community structure

As  sorbent  amendment  may  cause  shifts  in  microorganism  abundance in amended sediment, 16 s rRNA gene sequencing was used for microbial  community  structure  analysis.  The α -diversity  indices  presented  in  Table  S5  show  that  the  bacterial  communities  of  various sediment microcosms were affected by BCS amendment. BCS5 showed the highest values of diversity indices (Ace, Chao1, and Shannon) and taxonomic distribution compared to the control and other treated microcosms.  The  steepest  curve  was  observed  for  BCS5  and  BCS10, whereas  the  lowest  steep  curve  was  reported  for  BCS0  and  BCS20 (Fig. S11). This implies that an appropriate amount of sulfur impregnation  in  BC  can  improve  the  OTUs  value  and  diversity  indices.  In addition, the Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) analysis and UPGMA cluster tree results from β -diversity analysis ascertained the differences in  bacterial  diversity  resulting  from  various  BCS  amendments  in  the sediment (Figs. S12 and S13). The presence of elemental S in amended materials has been reported to influence bacterial abundance and diversity (Zhao et al., 2015). It has been documented  that BC, sulfurized-BC,  and  various  other  carbon  amendments  can  drastically alter microbial diversity in heavy metal-contaminated habitats (Beckers et al., 2019; Christensen et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019).

Increases and decreases in the bacterial composition and structure, as a function of different types of BC treatments, have been observed in several studies (Beckers et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018). In the present study,  the  effects  of  various  BCSs  on  the  bacterial  composition  of Hg-contaminated  sediment  were  described  in  terms  of  the  relative abundance of the dominant phyla and families (Fig. 5). In all samples, the phyla Proteobacteria , Firmicutes , Bacteroidetes , and Chloroflexi were the most abundant. Bacterial populations belonging to these phyla have been previously reported in Hg-contaminated habitats (Xu et al., 2019). Among  these  phyla, Proteobacteria was  the  most  predominant  in  all sediment samples, accounting for the highest (40.31 %) in BCS3 and lowest (31.62 %) in BCS20 treatments. Compared to the control (13.73 %),  the  relative  abundance  of Firmicutes was  higher  in  all  the  BCS amendments (14.03 -25.94 %), suggesting that BC and BCS supported the enrichment of the Firmicutes phylum. In contrast, the Bacteroidetes population  declined  in  all  BCS-amended  samples  (8.37 -11.26  %) compared to that in the control (11.81 %), implying that BC and BCS negatively  affected  the Bacteroidetes population.  At  the  family  level, Clostridiaceae was predominantly reported in all samples, accounting for the highest proportion (15.29 %) in BCS20. The relative abundance of the family Desulfobulbaceae was comparatively higher in all amended samples (5.50 -8.66 %) than that in the control (4.65 %). However, the bacterial  population  belonging  to  the  families Flavobacteriaceae and Desulfobacteraceae decreased in all treated samples, as compared to that in the control. BC and other amendments in soil and sediments directly affect  biogeochemical  species,  which  in  turn  influence  the  bacterial community structure (Wang et al., 2018).

Further exploration of the effects of BCS amendment on bacterial communities  was  performed  by  analyzing  the  relative  abundance  of genera  (Table  S6).  Most  of  the  bacterial  genera  observed  in  various treated  samples  are  well  known  for  sulfur-consuming,  iron-reducing, thiosulfate-oxidizing, and sulfate-reducing. Sulfate-reducing and ironreducing  bacteria  are  regarded  as  principal  Hg-methylators  (Beckers et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2019). Genus-level analysis further revealed that most of the Hg-methylators, as listed in the ORNL dataset (Christensen et al., 2018; Parks et al., 2013) (https://www.esd.ornl.gov/programs/r sfa/data.shtml), were reduced in various BCS-amended microcosms, as compared to that in the control (Table S7). However, further microbial and molecular analyses by quantifying the methylating gene ( hgc AB) are essential  to  confirm  the  role  of  BCS  amendments  in  lowering  the Hg-methylating populations.

## 5. Conclusions

BC was thermally functionalized with sulfur and evaluated for MeHg removal from contaminated sediment using a series of batch experiments. BC modification has been limited to varying S-loading percentages (1 -20%), while the effect of temperature and other post-treatment conditions has not been investigated. There have been several studies on BCS against inorganic Hg; however, its sorption mechanism for MeHg and  its  impact  on  Hg  methylation  in  sediment  when  used  as  an amendment have rarely been studied. Our results showed that MeHg adsorption on BCS was best described by the Freundlich isotherm, with KF values in the range of 0.87 -5.91 mg-L/g and a pseudo-second order adsorption kinetic model with a rate of 0.10 -0.36 mg/g-h. Adulteration of  the  S-functional  groups  during  modification resulted in  increasing shifts in Hg sorption from Hg-O to Hg-S. When used as an amendment, BCS reduced pore water THg and MeHg levels by 40 -70% and 30 -55%, respectively. Furthermore, a 50 -60% reduction in sediment MeHg was observed in all amended sediments, with superior performance observed for BC with the lowest sulfur loading (1%). Our results highlight the potential  of  BCS  for  Hg  contaminant  management  in  sediments.  A possible  scheme  for  MeHg  reduction  when  BCS  is  applied  as  an amendment to sediment relies on its effect on both Hg bioavailability and changes in the microbial community in the sediment. The shift in the dominant type of bacteria in the sediment somehow resulted in a reduced occurrence of redox-driven reduction associated with organic matter decomposition. When this is coupled with significant immobilization of Hg in the sorbent, Hg methylation in amended sediments is expected to be reduced. The results of this study show that the sorption performance of BCS increased after S-modification. However, among the modified BCs, the lowest %S-loading was the most effective for MeHg mitigation.  Determining the effect  of  a  much  lower  %S-loading  (less than 1%) and varying the synthesis conditions must be considered in future studies.
