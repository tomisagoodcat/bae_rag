The stability of mercury (Hg) contamination in soil environments can change over time. This has implications for agricultural sites under long-term management after in situ treatment involving soil amendments. In this study, rice husk biochar (RHB) and sulfur modi /uniFB01 ed rice husk biochar (SRHB) were synthesized and applied (dosage ¼ 5% dry wt.) to a Hg polluted agricultural soil collected from Guizhou province, Southern China (soil total Hg content ¼ 28.3 mg/kg; C ¼ 2%; and, S ¼ 0.1%). The long-term stabilization effectiveness of the soil treatments was evaluated by a combined approach involving: (i) accelerated aging for 104 simulated years; (ii) soil extraction as a proxy for plant uptake; and, (iii) sequential extraction to identify Hg fractions. The SRHB amendment raised the soil ' s total S content by approximately an order of magnitude (to 0.9%), which remained at a generally constant level throughout the simulation. The initial pH levels for the untreated and treated soils were alkaline and remained between 7.0 and 7.5 for the /uniFB01 rst 50 years of simulated aging, before decreasing as the simulation time increased further. The pH of the SRHB treated soils did not drop below that of untreated soils during the simulation. Soil extraction tests with 0.1 M HCl solution indicated that RHB and SRHB treatments could effectively immobilize the Hg in soil for at least 50 and 75 simulated years, respectively. At simulated year 50, the amount of Hg extracted from RHB and SRHB treated soils was &lt; 200 ng/L and &lt; 100 ng/L, respectively. Thus, showing SRHB to be a particularly promising remedial option. The soil Hg was mostly associated with the stable sequential extraction fractions (F3-5). By the end of the simulation, the F5 fraction for SRHB and RHB treated soils reduced by 44.6%, and 42.0%, respectively, whereas the F4 fraction increased by &gt; 400% in both cases. In summary, SRHB may provide long-lasting Hg stabilization at contaminated sites. Therefore, further research toward the development of this stabilization technology is warranted.

An estimated 8 million people are at risk from the different forms of Hg in the environment (Pure Earth and Green Cross, 2016; Wang et al., 2012), leading to the international community to introduce the Minamata convention on mercury in January 2013; which was adopted in October 2013 and came into force in August 2017. Remedial treatments can render contaminated sites /uniFB01 t for use (Hou et al., 2020). There are, however, signi /uniFB01 cant challenges to achieve successful remediation of Hg contaminated sites (Beckers et al., 2019a; Blom, 2012). Moreover, traditional remediation approaches are associated with secondary environmental emissions and socio-economic impacts (Hou, 2019; Hou et al., 2017; O ' Connor et al., 2020). Emerging green technologies in this /uniFB01 eld include enhanced thermal treatments (Lin et al., 2014), bioremediation (Mahbub et al., 2016; Rocha-Santos and Duarte, 2015), phytomanagement (Jia et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019b), biosorption (Dash and Das, 2015; Yin et al., 2016) and stabilization-based approaches (Gil-Díaz et al., 2017). Among these, stabilization is considered highly promising (Shen et al., 2019b; Shen et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020).

A number of stabilization technologies have been developed to immobilize Hg in soil to minimize environmental risk (Beckers et al., 2019b; Wang et al., 2019a; Wang et al., 2020c). This technology relies on the fact that stabilized contaminants display lower bioavailability and, therefore, the environmental risk is mitigated (Bolan et al., 2014; Frohne and Rinklebe, 2013; Houben et al., 2013; Jing et al., 2008). In general, stabilization approaches to soil remediation are viewed as sustainable because of relatively low cost and minimal associated environmental impacts (Blom, 2012; Hou, 2019; Wang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). Moreover, stabilization agents that are produced from waste crop residue (Zhao et al., 2018a) exploit a resource that may otherwise be disposed by open burning, thus helping to mitigate a source of air pollution and greenhouse gas emission (Brewer et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2020b).

Pyrolysis of waste agricultural crop residues (i.e., burning with limited oxygen) (Downie et al., 2009; Gil-Lozano et al., 2014) produces biochar, which offers a nature-based stabilization material (Dong et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2019a; Ye et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2018b). Moreover, biochar addition to agricultural soils can also improve crop yields (Sahin et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2020). However, while biochar can feasibly be used to stabilize a wide range of heavy metals in soil (O ' Connor et al., 2018b), its effectiveness can be somewhat inconsistent for Hg contaminated soils (Beckers et al., 2019a; Beckers et al., 2019b; Gomez-Eyles et al., 2013). For example, while pinecone biochar (pyrolized at 200 and 500 /C14 C) was found to render little effect on Hg mobilization levels in one study (Beckers et al., 2019a), a notable /uniFB01 eld trial conducted in the Wanshan Hg mining area, Southern China, showed that soil amendment with rice husk biochar (RHB) (also known as rice hull biochar) was associated with lower Hg levels in the roots, stalks and leaves of rice plants at the /uniFB01 lling and maturing stages (Xing et al., 2019).

Recently, researchers have increasingly attempted to modify biochar to fashion novel remediation materials that have superior properties for the stabilization of recalcitrant contaminants (O ' Connor et al., 2018; Ok et al., 2015; Rajapaksha et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019c; Yong et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2020a). Various modi /uniFB01 cation approaches have been proposed, including chemical and physical alterations to biochar matrix structures and impregnation with reactive components (Cho et al., 2019; Premarathna et al., 2019; Sahin et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2019). For instance, the Hg adsorption capacity of RHB can be enhanced by mixing RHB with elemental S and heating the mixture to a temperature above the boiling point of elemental S (444.6 /C14 C), which produces S-modi /uniFB01 ed RHB (SRHB). The Langmuir maximum sorption capacity (Qmax) of SRHB for Hg has been shown to be ~80% higher than that of RHB (O ' Connor et al., 2018). The increased amount of sorption is attributed to the strong binding af /uniFB01 nity between S and Hg, which forms the highly stable compound HgS

## (cinnabar) (O ' Connor et al., 2018; O ' Connor et al., 2018b; Xing et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2009).

SRHB is considered a particularly promising option for treating Hg polluted surface soils (O ' Connor et al., 2018). However, much uncertainty remains over its long-term performance owing to a lack of experimental data (Hou et al., 2016). A key research gap regarding this technology relates to temporal performance under weathering conditions (O ' Connor et al., 2018). Speci /uniFB01 c concerns relate to SRHB long-term effectiveness when exposed to acidic rainwater. Moreover, soil bacteria may covert the sulfur component of SRHB to sulphuric acid, also lowering the soil pH.

In this study, RHB and SRHB were synthesized as soil remediation materials. Their long-term stabilization performance was tested in a Hg contaminated soil by a combination of (i) accelerated aging, (ii) Hg extraction tests that mimic plant uptake, and (iii) sequential extraction to identify different fractions. Temporal variations in soil properties were also evaluated to support the analysis.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Biochar preparation and modi /uniFB01 cation

Biochar preparation followed the procedures previously detailed in O ' Connor et al., 2018. In brief, biochar was produced by the pyrolysis of waste rice husk under an N2 atmosphere (0.2 L/ min), with a highest treatment temperature (HTT) of 550 /C14 C maintained for 2 h. After cooling, the rice husk biochar (RHB) was collected, crushed, sieved ( &lt; 0.85 mm), placed in a sealed bag, and stored in a desiccator before use. The S content of the RHB was minimal ( &lt; 0.1% wt.). Modi /uniFB01 cation of RHB to form sulfur modi /uniFB01 ed rice husk biochar (SRHB) involved mixing RHB with elemental S in a 1:1 ratio (dry wt.), which was manually homogenized before being heated to 550 /C14 C under an N2 atmosphere (0.2 L/min) for 2 h. The S content of the SRHB was 14% wt. Data regarding SRHB characterization, S speciation, and sorption mechanisms can be found in O ' Connor et al., 2018.

## 2.2. Soil treatments and incubation method

Surface soil was collected from an agricultural site (rice paddy) in the vicinity of historic Hg mining in Guizhou province, Southern China. The composite sample was manually homogenized, airdried, ground by pestle and mortar, and sieved ( &lt; 0.85 mm). The initial average total Hg concentration in the soil was 28.3 mg/kg (see Section 3.3 for the fractional distribution of Hg). Properties of the processed soil are as follows: pH ¼ 7.1; electrical conductivity (EC) ¼ 62.2 m S/cm; sulfur content ¼ 0.1% (dry wt.); carbon content ¼ 2% (dry wt.).

RHB and SRHB were separately added to air dried soil samples (dosage ¼ 5% dry wt.). After homogenization, the water content of the RHB and SRHB treated soils and an untreated control were increased to 40% (wt.) with deionized water. The samples were then incubated for 14 d in containers covered with plastic /uniFB01 lmwith pinhole air vents to prevent anaerobic conditions occurring. The moisture content was maintained during incubation.

## 2.3. Accelerated aging process

Accelerated aging tests were conducted according to procedures described by Shen et al. (2018a, 2018b). Brie /uniFB02 y, continuous wet-dry cycles with CO2 saturated water (pH 5.6) were used to simulate the acid effect of typical natural rainwater. According to the Shen et al. (2018a) procedure, it can be calculated that 1 g of dry soil (density ¼ 1.3 g/cm 3 ) receives 1.538 mL of pH 5.6 rainwater per

year, assuming the average annual precipitation is 2 m (Shen et al., 2018a). In this way, a 1:10 mixture (g/mL) of soil and CO2 saturated water stirred at 75 rpm for 8 h, simulates 6.5 years of accumulated H þ from rainwater. After each wet cycle, the mixtures were stood until the sediment settled and the supernatant removed, before drying in an oven at 40 /C14 C until constant weight was attained. The wet-dry cycle was repeated 16 times to simulate 104 years of aging in 6.5-year increments.

## 2.4. Soil extraction and sequential leaching method

Soil extraction tests were used to determine the relative fractions of Hg that were potentially bioavailable for plant uptake. A 0.1 M HCl solution was prepared to extract Hg, which was reported by Jing et al. (2008) to be a suitable proxy for assessing Hg plant uptake from soil (Jing et al., 2008). Brie /uniFB02 y, 1.5 g of soil was mixed with 7.5 mL of 0.1 M HCl and stirred for 30 min before separation by centrifugation and /uniFB01 ltration.

The method for sequential leaching performed in this study was a modi /uniFB01 cation of the method previously described by (Bloom et al., 2003). The modi /uniFB01 cation was necessary because the second step of the Bloom et al. (2003) method entails the use of CH3COOH (0.1 M) and HCl (0.01 M) to determine human stomach acid soluble Hg. In this study, we were concerned with Hg bioavailability to plants; therefore, we applied NH4Ac (1 M) as an alternative second extraction step. Prior to sequential extraction, soil samples were ground and sieved ( &lt; 0.1 mm). Leaching was carried out with an initial sample mass of 0.4 g in borosilicate vials with PTFE caps. The detailed procedures and corresponding extraction reagents are as follows:

Fraction 1 (attributed to water soluble Hg): Deionized water (40 mL) was added to the solid sample, followed by 18 ± 4 h of shaking at 250 r.p.m. After extraction, the samples were centrifuged (3000 rpm) for 20 min, and the supernatant /uniFB01 ltered (0.4 m m), followed by oxidation with BrCl (0.2 M,1.25 mL). Fraction 2 (attributed to plant available Hg): NH4Ac (1 M, 40 mL) was added to the residue of the previous extraction, which was shaken for 18 h ± 4 h at 250 r.p.m. The solution was then processed as per fraction 1. Fraction 3 (attributed to organo-complexed Hg): KOH (1 M, 40 mL) was added to the residue of fraction 2, which was shaken for 18 h ± 4 h at 250 r.p.m. The solution was then processed as per fraction 1 except 10.0 mL of BrCl was added owing to the high neutralizing capacity of the KOH. Fraction 4 (attributed to elemental Hg or strongly complexed Hg): HNO3 (12 M, 40 mL) was added to the residue of fraction 3, which was shaken for 18 h ± 4 h at 250 r.p.m. The solution was then processed as per fraction 1 except /uniFB01 ltration was not performed because the cellulose nitrate membrane was easily dissolved by the HNO3. Fraction 5 (attributed to HgS): Residue from fraction 4 was digested with aqua regia at room temperature.

Following each step (except the last) the vials were re /uniFB01 lled with the same extractant solution, shaken vigorously to suspend the sediment, and then immediately re-centrifuged and /uniFB01 ltered. The rinse solution was added to the original extract.

## 2.5. Analysis and quality control

An elemental analyser (Thermo FlashSmart, USA) was used to determine the elemental composition of treated soils (i.e., C and S content). The pH of soil was determined at a solid to liquid ratio of 1:2.5 g/mL (Xu et al., 2014). Prior to Hg analysis, soil samples were subjected to microwave assisted digestion (Mars, USA) in aqua regia solution (4:1 HCl:HNO3), /uniFB01 ltered (0.45 m m), diluted, and preserved with HCl (12 M, 0.25 mL) (US EPA, 2001). All Hg concentrations were determined in aqueous solution using modi /uniFB01 ed US EPA

method 1631 (US EPA, 2002) involving measurement by cold-vapor atomic /uniFB02 uorescence spectroscopy (CV AFS) (Tekran 2600).

Soil samples were preserved at 1 e 4 /C14 C by refrigeration prior to processing. Glassware was immerged in 1% HNO3 followed by rinsing with deionized water to prevent lab-introduced contamination. Instrument calibration was undertaken with known reference solutions before analysis (r 2 &gt; 0.999). A continuing calibration solution (Hg ¼ 25 ng/L) was analysed every 10 samples to verify instrument stability.

## 2.6. Statistical analysis

Experimental data presented herein pertain to the mean of three samples. Where error bars are shown they represent the standard deviation of the mean. Data subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed at the 95% con /uniFB01 dence level. Statistical analyses were performed using Origin (version 9.0) and IBM SPSS (version 13.0).

## 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1. Temporal effect of treatments on soil properties

## 3.1.1. Soil pH

Biochar poses an abundance of alkaline minerals (Ahmad et al., 2017; You et al., 2014), which can be consumed or leached away over time by rainwater acid. To assess the effect of external acid addition, pH values were measured throughout the accelerated aging programme (Fig. 1). The initial pH levels of the untreated and treated soils were alkaline and remained between 7.0 and 7.5 during the simulation for the /uniFB01 rst 50 years, before decreasing as the simulated aging time increased from 50 to 75 years. This drop was the steepest for the untreated soil, revealing additional acid buffering of soils amended with biochar. It should be noted that Hg stability relates to soil pH (Coufalík et al., 2012; Miretzky et al., 2005) and that soil guideline values may vary with soil pH. In China, for example, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment (MEE) state that total Hg levels in paddy soils should be &lt; 0.5 mg/kg if the pH is &lt; 6.5; &lt; 0.6 mg/kg if the pH is between 6.5 and 7.5; and, &lt; 1 mg/kg if the pH is &gt; 7.5 (MEE, 2018).

A concern over SRHB is whether soil bacteria will convert the sulfur content to sulphuric acid, thus causing soil pH levels to decrease. For instance, it is a common agricultural practice for

## 3.1.2. Soil S content

After treatment with SRHB, the average sulfur content of the soil was raised to ~0.9%, almost an order of magnitude higher than the ~0.1% of untreated soil (Fig. 2). Although the S content is considered insoluble, a concern over SRHB is whether the sulfur content would wash away during the wet-dry cycles (e.g., possibly as colloids or organo-complexes). However, variation in the measured soil S content over the entire simulated aging process was low (SD ¼ 0.07%), demonstrating that the loss of soil sulfur was limited.

## 3.2. Temporal stabilization effectiveness

## 3.2.1. Short-term effectiveness

The treatment ef /uniFB01 ciency of the amended soils (5% SRHB and 5% RHB treatments) as well as the untreated control soil, were evaluated on incubation days 1 and 14. As shown in Fig. 3, the amount of Hg extracted by the 0.1 M HCl solution was affected by the soil treatment type and incubation time. It was found that SRHB and RHB soil amendments both signi /uniFB01 cantly (P &lt; 0.05) lowered the Hg extraction level compared to the untreated control soil, signifying less Hg available for plant uptake after treatment.

The concentration of Hg in the extraction solution of the untreated control soil was 597 ng/L after 1 day of incubation. For RHB treated soil, the Hg concentration was 254 ng/L after 1 day, corresponding to a 57.4% treatment ef /uniFB01 ciency. Moreover, the effectiveness of the RHB treatment did not signi /uniFB01 cantly change between incubation day 1 and 14 (day 14 treatment ef /uniFB01 ciency ¼ 52.4%), showing that the treatment effect of the RHB in the amended soil matrix was achieved quickly.

For the SRHB treated soil, the Hg concentration in the extraction solution was 177 ng/L after 1 day of incubation, corresponding to a 70.2% treatment ef /uniFB01 ciency. Two plausible reasons for the observed initial improving trend in Hg extraction from treated soils from simulated year 1 e 50 are as follows: (i) the effectiveness of the biochar treatments increased with accelerated aging time, or (ii) an increasing amount of mobile Hg was lost to the environment which reduced the overall fraction of less stable Hg in the soil under accelerated aging conditions. This /uniFB01 nding is supported by the presence of a small amount of Hg in the water soluble and exchange fractions of the sequential extraction results (Section 3.3).

It was found that the RHB amendment was effective for at least 50 simulated years, while the SRHB amendment was effective for at least 75 simulated years. At simulated year 50, the amount of Hg extracted from RHB and SRHB treated soils was reduced to &lt; 200 ng/L and &lt; 100 ng/L, respectively. The amount of Hg extracted from RHB and SRHB treated soils remained lower than for the untreated control soil throughout the simulation experiment, demonstrating that the treatment systems provided enhanced Hg stability with reduced leaching levels. The results also show that SRHB could achieve longer lasting stabilization than RHB, with the SRHB treatment causing the Hg to remain stable for an ~50% longer period. Overall, the SRHB treatment not only showed good stabilization performance in the short term (Section 3.2.1), but also the potential to stabilize soil Hg for long simulated periods under the accelerated effect of rainwater acid.

The continual increase in the amount of Hg extracted between simulated year 50 and 75 indicates a weakening of the effectiveness of the treatments at this stage. The fact that this diminishing treatment effect commenced after simulated year 50 is likely associated with the simultaneous decrease in soil pH after this point (Section 3.1.1). It is well-known that the effectiveness of stabilization treatments can deteriorate over time (Shen et al., 2019b). In the case of biochar treatments, this effect is often related to the leaching of alkaline minerals (O ' Connor et al., 2018b), which hinders the long-term immobilization of heavy metal(loid)s. For example, Cui et al. (2016) reported on a /uniFB01 ve-year /uniFB01 eld trial involving biochar amendment at a Cu and Cd contaminated site, in which the treatment ef /uniFB01 ciency was reported to decrease with time. This diminishing effect was attributed to gradual leaching of biochar alkalinity.

## 3.3. Temporal change in Hg fractions

Stabilization technology relies on the formation of stable species within the amended soil environment, which relates to the proportion of a contaminant that is easily available for uptake by plants. Fig. 5 shows the fractional distribution of Hg in the treated soils at the beginning and at the end of the accelerated aging programme. Fraction 5 was dominant, which was attributed to the highly stable species mercuric sul /uniFB01 de (HgS). Fraction F3, which is attributed to organo-complexed Hg, was the second most signi /uniFB01 -cant fraction, whereas water soluble (F1), strongly complexed (F4) and exchangeable Hg (F2) fractions were much smaller.

In all treatments throughout the simulated aging, as well as the untreated soil, the soil Hg was dominantly associated with the most stable fractions (F3-5), with only low amounts in the mobile fractions (F1 and F2). This indicates that the stability of Hg in the soil was high. In general, Hg will tend to bind to soil constituents involving clay, organic carbon, and sulfur (Beckers and Rinklebe,

2017; O ' Connor et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020a; Wang et al., 2019a). Only a small proportion tends to partition to soil porewater. For example, Liao et al. (2009) revealed that Hg binding to three types of soil (clay, loam and sand) was rapid and strongly irreversible, with the freely available portion being typically low ( &lt; 1% dry wt.). Chen et al. (2016) assessed soils in the vicinity of historic gold mines and a reservoir located near Beijing, China. They reported that Hg existed mainly in a highly stable form, with the bioavailable Hg fraction accounting for only 0.3% (dry wt.).

The stability of Hg in soil is highly dependent on Hg speciation and pH. The untreated control soil in this study was shown to contain S (~0.1% dry wt.) and carbon (~2% dry wt.). The fact that most of the Hg in the soil can be attributed to HgS (F5) and organocomplexed Hg (F3) shows the effectiveness of S and organic carbon for stabilizing Hg. Bloom et al. (2003) also found mercuric sul /uniFB01 de to be the major form of Hg in naturally occurring sediments where S and organic carbon were present (Bloom et al., 2003). Notably, the application of SRHB as a soil amendment would substantially enhance both S and organic carbon levels, suggesting an effective remediation pathway (Dong et al., 2019; Jing et al., 2019).

By the end of the accelerated aging programme, the fraction of Hg in F5 signi /uniFB01 cantly decreased and F4 increased. The F5 fraction for SRHB and RHB treated soils was reduced by 44.6%, and 42.0%, respectively, whereas F4 was increased by more than a factor of four in both cases. One plausible explanation for F5 being lower at the end of the accelerated aging programme is that a degree oxidation occurred during the simulation (Shen et al., 2018b), which resulted in the removal of some HgS species. However, this hypothesis was not veri /uniFB01 ed experimentally. In any case, the Hg mostly remained strongly bound within the soil, representing a low environmental risk. Or better put: because the amount of Hg in F4 increased as F5 decreased, the Hg content was not lost to the environment. Although it should be noted that there was also a possible increase in Hg in the F1 and F2 fractions by the end of the accelerated aging programme, which is important in terms of the proportion of Hg available for uptake by plants. This also re /uniFB02 ects the diminishing treatment effect after simulated year 50.

## 4. Conclusions and future research directions

This study indicates that RHB and SRHB amendment of Hg contaminated soils could be effective for at least 50 and 75 simulated years, respectively. The initial pH of the study soil was alkaline and remained consistently between 7.0 and 7.5 for the /uniFB01 rst 50 years of simulated aging before it decreased. In the case of SRHB treated soil, the pH did not drop indicate sulphuric acid formation. In all treatments the soil Hg was mostly associated with the most stable fractions of sequential leaching (F3 e F5). The natural stability of the soil was relatively high prior to treatment, yet the bioavailability of Hg was still signi /uniFB01 cantly reduced after amendment with RHB and more so after SRHB was applied. In summary, SRHB was shown to offer long-lasting stabilization of inorganic Hg in soil under accelerated aging. The use of SRHB could easily be scaled-up for the treatment of large areas of Hg contaminated land. As a result, this technology may offer a possible means to safely use agricultural soils that contain elevated levels of inorganic Hg, e.g., crop farming after amendment with SRHB. Further research toward the development of this technology is therefore warranted. It would be prudent to test SRHB in soils under anoxic conditions. In particular, the complex anaerobic-aerobic cycling environments of rice paddy /uniFB01 elds should be examined, as SRHB application may induce various effects, such as pH change, dissolved organic matter (DOM) change, HgS activation and other issues at such sites.
