## A B S T R A C T

Soil mercury contamination poses health risks. However, effective immobilization techniques are lacking with challenges including low efficiency, limited long-term stability, susceptibility to multi-medium interference, and difficulty in controlling health risks. This study confirmed the feasibility of thiol-modified biochar, and elucidated the underlying mechanisms. Within 32 days of treatment, the leachable mercury decreased from 184.7 μ g/ L to below the hazardous waste threshold (100 μ g/L, HJ/T299 -2007, China). After 198 days of treatment, the soil achieved a safe ecological state with a mercury immobilization rate of 79.8 -98.2% and a 50% reduction in available methylmercury. ## 1. Introduction

Soil  serves  as  a  persistent  mercury  reservoir,  exhibiting  greater persistence  compared  to  lakes,  oceans,  and  other  ecosystems  [1 -3]. Chemical and microbial processes easily transform soil-bound inorganic mercury into organic  forms like  methylmercury,  which  has  a  higher propensity to bioaccumulate in the food chain [4,5]. Meanwhile, mercury in soil can also threaten human health through ' soil  pathway ' , including the exposure of oral ingestion, dermal absorption, inhalation, and  polluted  groundwater  consumption  [6 -8].  The  United  Nations Minamata Convention, effective since August 2017, has received ratification from more than 140 countries by April 2023, demonstrating global recognition of the need to reduce mercury emissions and usage. Given the global recognition of the seriousness of mercury pollution, the need for efficient remediation technologies is now more pressing than ever [9].

Conversely, the layered biochar displays greater graphitization, with 3-MPTS forming spherical polymers on its surface and within interlayers through π bonds, resulting in a specific surface area increase from 7.1 to 288.4 m 2 /g [19]. The layered structure promotes a uniform thiol group distribution, enhancing adsorption capacities  for  both  Hg 2 + and  CH3Hg + .  Thiol-modified  biochar  exhibits excellent  performance  in  remediating  mercury  pollution  in  water bodies, providing a potential approach for remediating mercury-contaminated  soil.  Nevertheless,  the  efficiency  and  mechanisms of Hg immobilization in soil through thiol-modified biochar, the impact on biochar structure, long-term stability of immobilization, and the corresponding health risks remain incomplete.

Immobilization is widely recognized as a simple, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly method for remediating heavy metalcontaminated soil, especially suitable for large-scale field applications [6,10].  Immobilization  effectively  reduces  heavy  metal  mobility  and bioavailability  through  various  mechanisms,  such  as  ion  exchange, adsorption, complexation, and precipitation [2, 11 -13]. In recent years, biochar (the anoxic carbonization product of organic solid waste) has garnered increasing attention due to its potential to improve soil quality, remediate  heavy  metal  pollution,  and  effectively  sequester  carbon [14 -16].  The  application  of  biochar  in  the  remediation  of  heavy metal-contaminated soil is notable for its alkalinity, large specific surface area, and abundant organic functional groups, which facilitate the immobilization of heavy metals in soil [17,18]. Nevertheless, its limited binding  capacity  and  insufficient  selectivity  for  mercury  hinder  its widespread  use  in  actual  mercury-contaminated  sites.  Improving  the adsorption capacity and selectivity of biochar for mercury is a pressing technical challenge in practical applications. Additionally, there is a lack of  consensus  on  how  to  comprehensively  assess  the  immobilization effectiveness  of  biochar.  We  believe  that  immobilization  efficiency, health risk reduction, and long-term stability should be regarded as key indicators for evaluation.

Our  previous  studies  have  demonstrated  thiol-modification  as  an effective  method  in  enhancing  biochar ' s  adsorption  capacity  and selectivity  for  mercury,  showing  promise  in  mercury  contamination remediation [15, 19, 20]. For instance, thiol-modification with 3-mercaptopropyltrimethoxysilane (3-MPTS) increased Hg 2 + adsorption capacity  by  0.2 -1.2  times  and  CH3Hg + adsorption capacity by 1.4 -7.4 times [15]. Although low-temperature carbonization (300 ◦ C) has achieved excellent thiolation modification, the stability of passivation materials  assumes  paramount  significance  in  remediation  applications. Thus,  we  proposed  a ' microwave-assisted  modification  method ' to produce highly stable thiol-modified biochars ( &gt; 500 ◦ C). Notably, this method led to a significant 11 -14 fold increase in Hg 2 + adsorption capacity and a remarkable 17 -19 fold increase in CH3Hg + adsorption capacity [19]. And the mercury adsorption capacity of our thiol-modified biochar far exceeds that of most reported modified biochars [21 -24].

Our previous study has revealed variations in thiol-modification efficacy and mercury removal among biochars with different structures. In  this  study,  two  types  of  thiol-modified  biochar,  one  with  a multilayer structure and the other porous, were utilized. Ther primary objective  of  this  study  is  to  confirm  the  suitability  of  thiol-modified biochar  for  mercury-contaminated  soil  remediation.  This  involves  a comprehensive  assessment  of  mercury  immobilization  performance, health risks, long-term efficacy, and mechanistic insights. The specific objectives of this study are as follows: (1) to investigate the effect of varying amounts and structures of thiol-modified biochar on immobilizing mercury in contaminated soil, (2) to elucidate the immobilization mechanism, (3) to evaluate the impacts of thiol-modified biochar on the physicochemical properties of soil, (4) to assess the long-term stability of immobilized mercury in soil over three years, and (5) to analyze the associated health risks in different land use scenarios.

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Thiol-modified biochar and Hg-contaminated soil

The two thiol-modified biochars used in this study are those we have recently reported [19]. The thiol-modified biochar derived from pine sawdust  with  a  porous  structure  was  designated  as  BPS,  while  the multi-layer thiol-modified biochar obtained from grapefruit peel was denoted  as  BGS.  For  detailed  characterization  data  of  BPS  and  BGS, please refer to our previously published work [19]. And the detailed procedure for the preparation and structural parameters of thiol-modified  biochars  are  provided  in  the  supplementary  materials ( Text S1, Table S1). The Hg-contaminated soil utilized in this investigation was previously described [2], and the mercury concentration in the contaminated soil was quantified as 195.0 mg/kg using the EPA 3050B method (Text S2). All chemicals used in this study were provided in the supplementary material ( Text S3 ).

## 2.2. Immobilization of mercury in soil by thiol-modified biochar

Batch tests were conducted using 400.0 g of mercury-contaminated soil  (dry  weight,  Hg  content  195.0  mg/kg)  in  polyethylene  bottles. Two different doses of thiol-modified biochar (0.2% and 0.4% by dry weight) were examined. The soil samples were supplemented with 100 mL of deionized water to maintain a moisture content of 25 ± 2%. The bottles were then sealed and incubated in the dark at a temperature of 25 ± 2 º C. The experimental setup included triplicate treatment tests and control tests (without the addition of biochar). The sampling was conducted at specific intervals: 2, 12, 32, 58, 92, 198, 365, 730, and 1095 days.

## 2.3. Soil characterization and analysis

After 198 days of incubation, the soil mineral composition was tested by  X-ray  powder  diffraction  (XRD)  (D/max-2500,  Rigaku,  Osaka, Japan), the surface chemical bonds of soil particles were detected by Xray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (PHI-5000, ULVAC-PHI, Chigasaki, Japan). The available N, P, K, and cation exchange capacity (CEC) of the soil were determined according to the relevant Environmental Protection  Standards  of  China:  NY/T  53 -1987,  HJ  632 -2011,  NY/T 87 -1988, and NY/T 1121.5 -2006, respectively. The total organic carbon was determined by a TOC analyzer (SSM-5000A, Shimadzu, Japan). The  available  and  total  methylmercury  in  soil  were  extracted  using (NH4)2S2O3  and CuSO4-HNO3-CH2Cl2  methods, respectively [27], and determined by Gas Chromatography Cold Vapor Atomic Fluorescence Spectrometry (GC-CVAFS) (Brooks Rand model III, Seattle, USA) (Text S5).  The  binding  species  of  mercury  in  soil,  including  exchangeable (EX), carbonate-bound (CB), Fe-Mn oxides-bound (OX), organic material-bound  (OM),  and  residual  (RS)  fractions,  were  determined using a revised Tessier method (Table S2) [28 -30]. The risk of mercury in soil was assessed using a risk assessment code (RAC) based on the revised Tessier method [31,32] (Text S6).

These considerations were informed by assessments conducted at actual mercurycontaminated  sites. This model was originally developed and endorsed by the U.S. EPA, has gained widespread acceptance in multiple countries [33].

## 3. Results and discussion

3.1. Immobilization kinetics of mercury-contaminated soil using thiolmodified biochar

The  effect  of  thiol-modified  biochar  amendments  on  mercury mobility  and  bioavailability  was  examined  by  extractions  with  H2O, H2SO4 + HNO3, CH3COOH, and CaCl2, respectively (Fig. 1). The initial concentrations  of  leachable  Hg  in  untreated  soil  were  158.5,  187.1, 184.7,  and  403.5 μ g/L,  respectively,  significantly  exceeding  the  hazardous waste identification standard threshold of  100 μ g/L  (GB 5085.3 -2007, China). Over the 32-day period of thiol-modified biochar addition,  the  Hg  immobilization  efficiency  exhibited  rapid  improvement, eventually reaching equilibrium at 92 days (Fig. 1). On day 32, leachable  Hg  concentrations  for  the  four  treatments  (i.e.,  0.2%  BPS, 0.4% BPS, 0.2% BGS, and 0.4% BGS) were determined using the H2SO4 + HNO3  method (HJ/T299 -2007, China) and found to be below the hazardous  waste  identification  standard  threshold  (100 μ g/L,  GB 5085.3 -2007, China) (Fig. S1).

## 3.2. Mercury species in soil after 198-day treatment

According to the Tessier method, the EX and CB fractions exhibit the highest bioavailability, while the OX and OM fractions are potentially bioavailable under acidic or oxidizing conditions. The RS fraction represents  the  most  stable  state,  where  heavy  metals  remain  within  the crystal structure [31,35]. After a 198-day incubation, the EX, CB, OX, OM, and RS fractions in the untreated soil accounted for 2.6%, 2.0%, 24.3%, 64.4%, and 6.7%, respectively (Fig. 2 a ). Conversely, the application of BPS and BGS led to a substantial transition in mercury speciation from highly available species (EX, CB, and OX) to less available species (OM and RS). For example, upon the inclusion of 0.4% BGS, the EX,  CB,  and  OX  fractions  witnessed  reductions  of  2.6%,  2.0%,  and 22.8%,  respectively,  while  the  OM  and  RS  fractions  experienced  increments of 23.4% and 4.1%, respectively (Fig. 2 a ). Importantly, in the case  of  both  BPS  and  BGS,  the  OM  fractions  demonstrated  the  most significant increase, which can be attributed to the significantly higher stability  constant  (log K 1)  between  mercury  and  thiol  groups  (22.1), compared to oxygen-containing groups (e.g., -OH: 10.6; GLYPH&lt;0&gt; COOH: 9.7) [19]. With the same dosage, the proportion of OM fraction in soil treated with BGS exceeded that treated with BPS (Fig. 2 a ). This difference can be attributed to the disparity in their structures. Specifically, BGS possesses  a  larger  specific  surface  area  (288.4 m 2 /g)  compared  to  BPS

Furthermore,  the  inhibitory  effect  of  thiol-modified  biochar  on inorganic mercury methylation in soil was observed. Following a 198day  incubation  period  with  the  addition  of  0.4%  thiol-modified  biochar, despite an increase in total methylmercury (rising from 3.4 μ g/kg in untreated soil to 5.6 μ g/kg and 5.8 μ g/kg for BPS and BGS, respectively), the available fraction decreased by half (reducing from 23.8 ng/ kg in untreated soil to 11.9 ng/kg and 11.5 ng/kg for BPS and BGS, respectively) (Fig. 2 b ). Moreover, the ecological risk of mercury after soil remediation was evaluated using the Risk Assessment Code (RAC) method (Text S6), which is based on the Tessier method. The RAC values depend on the exchangeable (EX) and carbonate-bound (CB) fractions, both of which were reduced by the addition of thiol-modified biochar. Consequently, untreated soils posed a low risk ( RAC = 4.65%, &gt; 1%), soils treated with thiol-modified biochar were considered safe ( RAC = 0.03% GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 0.45%, &lt; 1%) (Fig. 2 c ).

The increase in available N can be attributed to the introduction of ammonia during the thiol-modification process [14] and the material ' s ability to retain soil nitrogen.

## 3.4. Long-term stability of mercury immobilization in soil

immobilization was investigated by monitoring the extractable mercury concentrations in H2O, H2SO4 + HNO3, CH3COOH, and CaCl2 solutions over three years. As illustrated in Fig. 4, both BPS and BGS exhibited excellent long-term stability, as evidenced by the decreasing mercury leaching concentrations observed  over  the three-year  monitoring period.  For  instance,  with  an  application  of  0.2%  BPS,  the  mercury concentrations in the four leaching solutions decreased from 14.9 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 30.9 μ g/L (one year) to 2.5 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 5.5 μ g/L (two years) and 1.4 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 2.8 μ g/L (three years). Increasing the BPS dosage to 0.4% resulted in a more substantial reduction in mercury leaching concentration. This can be attributed to the introduction of a greater number of adsorption sites  [21].  BGS  demonstrates  superior  immobilization  and  long-term stability compared to BPS. For instance, the application of 0.4% BGS resulted  in  a  decrease  in  leachable  mercury  concentrations  from 1.3 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 6.4 μ g/L (one year) to 0.5 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1.2 μ g/L (two years) and 0.4 GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 0.9 μ g/L (three years).

After two years of treatment, the leachable mercury concentration in H2O met the fourth-level quality standard of 1.0 μ g/L for surface water (GB 3838 -2002, China) (Fig. 4 a ), and the other three leachable mercury concentrations  met  the  third-level  quality  standard  of  1.0 μ g/L  for groundwater (GB/T 14848 -2017, China) (Fig. 4 b-d ). The observations demonstrated that thiol-modified biochar can effectively facilitate the long-term immobilization of mercury in soil.

## 3.6. Mercury immobilization mechanisms

As  shown  in  Fig.  6,  complexation  and  mineralization  were  the immobilization mechanisms of mercury in soil. Thiol groups played a pivotal  role  in  both  competing  with  exchangeable  mercury  on  soil colloidal surfaces and sequestering carbonate-bound mercury and FeMn oxides-bound mercury. These interactions resulted  in  the  formation  of  organic  material-bound  mercury  (e.g.,  C3H4N2OS-Hg)  and insoluble minerals (e.g., HgS). The effectiveness of mercury immobilization in soil depended on the structure of thiol-modified biochar. For biochar with a porous structure, the surface is coated with thiol polymer, leading to pore blockage and the primary binding of mercury in soil pore water on the surface. Conversely, biochar with a multilayered structure exhibits  thiol  polymers  on  both  the  surface  and  interlayer,  enabling mercury to be bound not only on the surface but also within the interlayer, resulting in a higher immobilization rate. Thiol-modified biochar application effectively reduced  the  mobility of mercury  in  soil, mitigating both ecological and human health risks. The sustained stability of the immobilization effect over three years underscores its significant value in managing risks associated with mercury-contaminated sites.

## 4. Conclusions

Mercury contamination in soil poses a persistent threat to human health. There is a significant demand for passivation materials that are both  highly  efficient  and  durable.  This  study  demonstrates  thiolmodified  biochar  as  an  effective  material  for  remediating  mercurycontaminated soil. Various extractants, including H2O, H2SO4 + HNO3, CH3COOH, and CaCl2  solutions, were employed to simulate leaching processes induced by surface water or groundwater, acidic rain, landfill leachate,  and  bioavailable  mercury,  respectively.  Continuous  monitoring over a three-year period revealed the long-term stability of mercury immobilization for all treatments. The effective immobilization of soil mercury can be attributed to the precipitation and complexation, resulting in the transformation of mercury from a labile form (EX, CB, and  OX)  to  a  stable  form  (OM  and  RS).  These  findings  present  an effective strategy for immobilizing mercury-contaminated soil. Furthermore,  it  elucidates  the  technical  principles  of  thiol-modified biochar,  evaluates  its  long-term  stability,  and  assesses  risks  to  both soil ecology and human health. This confirms the feasibility of utilizing thiol-modified biochar for mercury immobilization in soil.

However,  thiol-modified  biochar  exhibits  potential  for  mercury methylation, rendering it more suitable for the remediation of mercurycontaminated sites rather than agricultural land. Consequently, ecological  toxicity  associated  with  thiol-modified  biochar  warrants continued attention, serving as one of the key determinants governing its applicability.
