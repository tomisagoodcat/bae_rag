## A B S T R A C T

Edited by Dr Fernando Barbosa

Keywords:

Natural sulphur-rich biochar Rhizosphere paddy soil Mercury pollution Microbial community

Co-occurrence network

Biochar and sulphur (S) are important factors regulating the level, speciation and transformation of mercury (Hg),  leading  to  alterations  in  the  assemblage  of  the  soil  microbial  community.  However,  variations  in  the taxonomic attributes of the rhizosphere soil bacterial community arising from the Hg speciation in paddy soil, amended with natural S-rich biochar (NSBC) derived from the pyrolysis of S-rich oilseed rape straw, remain unclear. Herein, a rice pot experiment was conducted. Hg-polluted paddy soils were amended with NSBC and low-S biochar (LSBC) to evaluate the role of Hg chemical form affected by NSBC in regulating the taxonomic attributes of rhizosphere soil, including microbial abundance, composition, and ecological clusters within the cooccurrence network of microbial communities. Results showed that microbial abundance was higher in soils with lower Hg levels, and mean increases of 149 observed operational taxonomic units (OTUs) and 238 predicted OTUs (Chao 1) were observed, with a 1 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 decrease in the total Hg (T-Hg) content. Among the 13 predictor variables, the T-Hg content was the strongest and most consistent predictor of the bacterial taxonomic attributes. This finding may be attributed to the fact that the drastic reduction in T-Hg and Hg bioavailability induced by NSBC results  in  the  decrease  of  Hg  stress  on  the  soil  microbiome.  Moreover,  NSBC  amendment  shifted  the ecological clusters toward the amelioration of Hg pollution.

## 1. Introduction

Mercury  (Hg)  pollution  in  paddy  fields  has  attracted  extensive attention because Hg-impacted paddy fields are an important pathway for  global  Hg  cycling  (Liu  et  al.,  2019).  Soil  microorganisms  play  a critical role in driving the biogeochemical cycling of elements and are of great  importance  to  the  formation  of  the  microenvironment  for  rice growth. However, elevated Hg pollution has toxic effects on soil microorganisms and can significantly alter the abundance and diversity of bacterial communities (Müller et al., 2001; Liu et al., 2018). The level of Hg pollution, distribution of Hg species, and duration of Hg pollution are the primary factors in predicting the influence of Hg pollution on the soil microbiome (Müller et al., 2001; Tazisong et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2014). Previous  studies  on  persistently  Hg-contaminated  paddy  soils  have shown that low levels of total Hg (T-Hg) and methylmercury (MeHg) can increase  the  bacterial  diversity  in  a  paddy  field,  and  this  finding  is apparently consistent with the intermediate disturbance hypothesis (Liu et al., 2014). Some studies have demonstrated that water-soluble Hg has a  considerable  effect  on  the  structure  of  the  bacterial  community  in forest soils injected with different doses of Hg (Frossard et al., 2017; Frey and Rieder, 2013). In addition, the influence of MeHg on the bacterial community in forest soil is highly correlated with soil physicochemical conditions (Frey and Rieder, 2013). Therefore, Hg in different chemical forms in the soil exert various effects on the diversity and structure of the microbial  community  due  to  the  differences  in  their  bioavailability (Frohne and Rinklebe, 2013; O ' Connor et al., 2019).

Ecological  clusters  or  modules  of  the  microbial  community  are defined  as  the  microbial  taxa  that  co-occur  within  the  ecological network of microbial communities identified during the analysis of the co-occurrence network (de Menezes et al., 2015). These networks are linked to the stability of the community, and ecological clusters have different environmental preferences and make different contributions to the function of the ecosystem (de Vries, 2018; Delgado-Baquerizo et al., 2018). Biochar has been extensively used to immobilise Hg in soil due to its special  physicochemical  traits  (Wang  et  al.,  2020a;  Gamboa-Herrera et al., 2021). This material can reduce Hg mobility in the soil by converting the mobile Hg species in soil into the immobile Hg-S chemical form (Liu et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020a). However, adding biochar has also been found to exert almost no influence on the mobilities of T-Hg and MeHg in floodplain soil (Beckers et al., 2019). Thus, no consensus has been reached on the influences of biochar application on the Hg species and bioavailability in soil.

Given that the ability of biochar in reducing the bioavailability of soil Hg has not been established, great efforts have been made to modify biochar to reduce the mobility and bioavailability of Hg (Feng et al., 2020a, 2020b; Huang et al., 2021; Jia et al., 2021). Considering the strong binding capacity of S to Hg, a few studies that have tested the efficacy of biochar modified by elemental S or S-containing compounds in removing Hg in aqueous solutions or immobilising Hg in soil apparently support the notion that S-modified biochar can strongly bind to Hg, and enhance the removal of Hg from solutions or else immobilise Hg in the soil (O ' Connor et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2019; Jeon et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020). The S modifiers (thiol compounds) are toxic to crops, and  the  extremely  high  S  content  in  nontoxic  S-modified  biochar (13.04%  S  w/w)  risks  causing  environmental  damage.  Thus,  the above-mentioned  S-modified  biochar  is  apparently  not  suitable  for remediating Hg-contaminated agricultural soils. Oilseed rape straw is a reasonable choice as biochar feedstock because of its higher S content and larger biomass compared with other crop residuals. Moreover, to further  increase  the  S  content  in  the  feedstock,  oilseed  rape  can  be suitably cultivated by fertilising with gypsum. The S-elevated oilseed rape straw can be pyrolysed in situ to produce natural S-rich biochar (NSBC) to reduce cost. Moreover, based on our latest finding, NSBC can effectively reduce the mobility of MeHg in the rhizosphere of paddy soil and thereby decrease the uptake of MeHg by rice (Hu et al., 2021). This finding indicates that the application of NSBC may influence the variation of Hg species in rhizosphere soils.

The soil rhizosphere is a microenvironment with unique physical, chemical, and biological properties formed during the growth of plants. The rhizosphere environment is not only a place for energy exchange, but  also  an  important  soil-microbiomes  interface  for  element  cycles (Blossfeld et al., 2011; van Dam and Bouwmeester, 2016; Kuzyakov and Razavi, 2019). Thus, investigating the influence of Hg on the assemblage of  the  microbial  community  in  rhizosphere  soil  is  highly  significant. However,  previous  studies  that  examined  the  influence  of  biochar amendment on the Hg speciation in farmland soil have mainly focused on bulk soil (O ' Connor et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020a; Yang et al., 2021), while rhizosphere soil has scarcely been explored.

In this study, we hypothesised that Hg species would have unique effects on the taxonomic attributes of bacterial communities -including bacterial  abundance,  composition,  and  co-occurrence  networks -in rhizosphere paddy soil amended with NSBC. To verify this hypothesis, we conducted a rice pot experiment for 157 days, during which NSBC and low-sulphur biochar (LSBC) were applied at low and high doses to treat Hg-polluted paddy soil. The influences of these biochars on the distribution  of  Hg  species  in  the  rhizosphere  soil  and  the  bacterial abundance, composition, and ecological clusters within the microbial communities at the mature stage of rice were comparatively analysed. Moreover, the correlations between T-Hg and organo-chelated Hg contents and the bacterial abundance in the rhizosphere soil were identified through structural equation modelling (SEM).

## 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Biochar preparation and properties of the produced biochar and Hgcontaminated paddy soil

Ordinary and S-rich oilseed rape straws were selected as the raw materials and pyrolysed in N2 atmosphere at 600 ◦ C to prepare LSBC and NSBC, respectively. The S-rich oilseed rape straw was collected from the Anqing Oilseed Rape Cultivation Base of the Chinese Academy of Sciences  in  Anhui  Province.  The  S  content  (0.75%)  was  elevated  by applying  gypsum,  and  the  ordinary  oilseed  rape  straws  (S  content: 0.15%) were purchased online. The S contents of the NSBC and LSBC were  5.1  g  kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 and  3.1  g  kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 (Table  S1),  respectively.  The  basic properties of the biochar are listed in Table S1.

Hg-polluted soil samples were collected from the Wanshan Mercury Mining District, Guizhou Province, China. The large gravels and plant roots were removed from the soil samples, which were then air dried, grinded with a plastic hammer, and sieved to &lt; 2 mm. The pH level of the Hg-polluted paddy soil was 6.54. The total organic C (TOC), total N (TN),  and  total  S  (TS)  contents  were  30.1,  1.2,  and  0.92  g  kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , respectively. The Hg content was 10.8 mg kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , and the MeHg content was 5.7 µ g kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 (Table S1).

## 2.2. Pot experiment and sample collection

The pot experiment was conducted in a greenhouse at 30 ± 2 ◦ C during daytime and at 25 ± 2 ◦ C at night, with air humidity at 40 -60%. Four  biochar  treatment  groups  were  set  in  the  experiment,  namely, biochar application doses of 0% (wt), 0.6% (wt) NSBC, 1.2% (wt) NSBC, and  1.2%  (wt)  LSBC,  which  were  denoted  as  CK,  0.6%  NSBC,  1.2% NSBC, and 1.2% LSBC, respectively. Each treatment was conducted in triplicate, with a total of 12 pots. In brief, the different biochars with the corresponding doses were added to 3 kg of Hg-polluted paddy soil and thoroughly blended in a plastic bag for later use. The customised cylindrical rhizosphere box ( Φ 14 cm × H 30 cm) made of 30 µ m nylon screen was placed at the centre of the plastic pot, and the previously blended soil was used to fill the pot uniformly. Next, deionised water was added until the soil moisture content reached approximately 60%, and  the  soil  sample  was  stabilised  for  5  days.  Then,  the  alreadycultivated rice seedlings were transplanted into the pot to start the pot experiment, which lasted for 157 days. Chemical fertilisers (100 mg N kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 soil  and  60  mg  P/K  kg GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 soil)  were  applied  based  on  the  requirements for rice growth. The deionised water was replenished every day to compensate for water evaporation.

Approximately 40 g of the rhizosphere soil samples in the customised rhizosphere box were collected in the rice seedling stage (0 days) and mature stage (157 days). Soil samples were divided into two portions. One portion was used to determine the physicochemical properties and Hg species, and the other portion was stored at GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 40 ◦ C for the bacterial genomic DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing.

## 2.3. Analysis of basic physicochemical properties

The  pH  levels  of  the  biochar  and  rhizosphere  soil  sample  were determined by a pH meter (Mettler Toledo, USA) at solid/liquid ratios of 1:10  and  1:2.5,  respectively.  The  TOC,  TN,  and  TS  contents  were determined using an elemental analyser (Elementar, Vario Curio, Germany).  The  dissolved  organic  matter  (DOM)  in  the  rhizosphere  soil samples was extracted following the method described in a previous work (Jones and Willett, 2006). In brief, one gram of lyophilised soil was weighed, and the DOM was extracted using deionised water at a soil/water ratio of 1:10 for 2 h, followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min. Then, the supernatant was filtered using a 0.45μ m poly tetra fluoro  ethylene  (PTFE)  filter.  Finally,  the  dissolved  organic  carbon (DOC)  content  in  the  supernatant  was  determined  using  an  organic carbon (OC) analyser (TOC-L, Shimadzu, Japan).

## 2.4. Determination of T-Hg and Hg species

To determine the T-Hg content in the biochar and rhizosphere soil samples, approximately 0.5 g of the sample was digested using 4 mL of aqua regia and 4 mL of deionised water at 95 ◦ C for 2 h and then diluted

to  50  mL  with  deionised  water.  To  determine  the  Hg  species  in  the rhizosphere  soil  sample,  the  soil  sample  was  pre-treated  using  a sequential  extraction  method  as  previously  described  (Bloom  et  al., 2003). In brief, approximately 1 g of the lyophilised soil was weighed, and  then  extracting reagents, including deionised water,  0.1  M CH3COOH + 0.01  M  HCl,  1  M  NaOH,  and  12  M  HNO3,  were  used sequentially at a solid/liquid ratio of 10:1 for extraction for 18 h (except for  the  first  step,  which  used  deionised  water  at  a  20:1  solid/liquid ratio). For each step of extraction, the corresponding extracting reagents were used to rinse the soil sample again, and the second solution was blended with the first extracted solution to determine the Hg concentration.  In  accordance  with  the  sequential  extraction  method,  the extracted Hg species included water-soluble Hg (Fraction 1, F1), human stomach acid-soluble Hg (Fraction 2, F2), organo-chelated Hg (O-Hg, Fraction 3, F3), and strong complexed/elemental Hg (Fraction 4, F4). Finally, the soil was lyophilised and digested using aqua regia, and the mercuric  sulphide  content  (Fraction  5,  F5)  was  determined.  The  Hg content  in  the  solution  was  determined  using  a  cold-vapor  atomic fluorescence spectrometer (PSA, PS Analytical, UK).

The MeHg content in the soil samples was determined using KOHmethanol extraction (Liang et al., 1996). In detail, approximately 0.5 g of air-dried rhizosphere soil was weighed and placed in a 30-mL Teflon bottle. Then, 2 mL of 25% KOH-methanol solution was added for the ultrasonic treatment for 3 h. After cooling, 6 mL of CH2Cl2 and 1.5 mL of HCl  were  added,  and  the  mixture  was  shaken  for  15  min  and  then centrifuged  for  5  min  to  separate  the  mixture.  The  substrate  CH2Cl2 solution was taken out and transferred to a new Teflon bottle. Then, 10 mL  of  deionised  water  was  added  into  the  bottle,  and  CH2Cl2  was volatised using a water bath at 50 ◦ C. The residual CH2Cl2 was removed through nitrogen blowing. The MeHg content in the obtained aqueous solution  was  determined  in  accordance  with  US  EPA  Method  1630 (USEPA, 2001).

The detection limits of the atomic fluorescence spectrometer (PAS, PS Analytical, UK) for Hg and MeHg were 1.0 ng L GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 and &lt; 0.1 μ g L GLYPH&lt;0&gt; 1 , respectively. The recoveries of T-Hg and MeHg in soil ranged from 92% to 112% and from 89% to 104%, respectively, and their corresponding matrix spike recoveries were 89 -102% and 78 -98%, respectively. The relative differences in the MeHg and T-Hg analytical results in the parallel samples were &lt; 8% and &lt; 11%, respectively. One blank sample was inserted into each batch of test samples (10 samples), and no interference was discovered based on the blank assays.

## 2.5. Genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction and high-throughput sequencing

The gDNAs were extracted from 0.2 to 0.7 g of fresh soil sample using FastDNA SPIN Kits (MP Biochemicals, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer ' s instructions. The total gDNA concentration and purity were determined  via  NanoDrop2000  (Thermo  Fisher  Scientific,  Waltham, MA,  USA).  Polymerase  chain  reaction  (PCR)  amplification  of  the extracted gDNA was implemented by selecting the primers specific to the  V3 -V4  hypervariable  regions  of  the  bacterial  16S  rRNA  genes, namely,  338F  (5 ′ -ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3 ′ )  and  806  R  (5 ′ -GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3 ′ ). The PCR amplification system was a 20 μ L mixture including 4 μ L of 5 × FastPfu buffer, 2 μ L of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 μ L of 5 μ M forward primer and reverse primers, 0.4 μ L of FastPfu polymer, 0.2 μ L of BSA, and 10 ng of the template DNA. The DNA amplification temperature program was set to 95 ◦ C for 3 min, then 95 ◦ C for 30 s, 55 ◦ C for 30 s, 72 ◦ C for 45 s, and 27 cycles, and finally 72 ◦ C for 10 min. PCR amplicons were separated with 2% agarose gel and then purified with AxyPrep DNA extraction reagent (Corning, USA). The purified PCR amplicons were quantified using a QuantiFluor fluorometer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and paired-end sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, USA).

## 2.6. Sequence analysis

The  raw  reads  generated  from  the  paired-end  sequences  were merged together using Fast Length Adjustment of Shot reads (FLASH). The chimeras of the acquired sequences were identified and removed using UCHIME algorithm (Edgar, 2010). The sequences were clustered via UPARSE method (http://www.drive5.com/uparse/) at a 97% similarity cut-off, and the taxonomy of representative operational taxonomic unit  (OTU)  sequences  was  assigned  using  the  Ribosomal  Database Project  (RDP)  classifier  (Caporaso  et  al.,  2010).  The  observed  mean number of OTUs and the predicted total number of OTUs (Chao 1) were calculated using QIIME2 pipeline.

## 2.7. Data analysis

The  statistical  significances  of  T-Hg  among  the  different  biochar treatment groups were determined using Tukey ' s multiple comparison tests  for  one-way  ANOVA.  Stepwise  regression  analysis  was  used  to evaluate the response relations of the observed and predicted OTUs in the rhizosphere soil with 13 predictors (environment variables) determined in this study. Univariate linear regression analysis was used to analyse the relationship between the relative abundance of the main OTUs and the T-Hg content in the rhizosphere soil. The above analyses were implemented via SPSS Statistics 20.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA).

The  co-occurrence  pattern  of  the  bacterial  taxa  was  explored  by analysing the network (Barber ´ an et al., 2012). The bacterial taxa with relative abundances greater than 0.02% were chosen from all samples to reduce the complexity of the network diagram. The paired Spearman correlation  matrix  between  bacterial  taxa  was  calculated.  If  the Spearman  correlation  coefficient  ( ρ )  between  two  OTUs  was  greater than 0.6 with a P value &lt; 0.01, the statistics of Spearman correlation between the two OTUs was considered robust. These robust correlations were used to form a correlation network, where each node represented one OTU and each edge indicated the strong and significant correlation between two OTUs. The above analysis was performed using the Igraph package in the R environment, and the network was visualised using the interaction platform Gephi (Bastian et al., 2009).

The direct and indirect influences of biochar, soil Hg pollution, and soil  physicochemical  parameters  on  the  bacterial  abundance  in  the rhizosphere soil were demonstrated using SEM. In accordance with the single-variable model results, a complete SEM model was established to verify the following hypotheses: biochar had a direct influence on the THg, O-Hg, soil organic content (SOC), TN, TS, and OTUs. T-Hg and O-Hg directly influenced the OTUs, and the OTUs were directly affected by SOC, TN, and TS. The assumed complete model was tested using a χ 2 test, comparative fitting index (CFI), and standardised root mean square error  of  approximation  (RMSEA).  The  variables  with  nonsignificant correlations were gradually excluded from the model. The difference between the hypothesis model path and the measured data was tested via a χ 2 test and a goodness-of-fit test. CFI values &gt; 0.95 indicated very favourable  goodness  of  fit.  RMSEA  values &lt; 0.05  manifested  very satisfactory goodness of fit.

## 3.2. Change in the bacterial abundance and its response to environmental predictive variables

Fifteen bacterial genomic DNAs in the rhizosphere soil samples were sequenced, and a total of 6387 OTUs were identified. The number of observed  OTUs  per  sample  within  the  bacterial  communities  ranged between 2332 and 3409, and the predicted number of OTUs (Chao1) was 3126 -4755.

## 3.3. Variation in the composition of bacterial communities and their relations with dominant environment variables

The 50 most abundant bacterial taxa were chosen to determine the variation in the microbial composition under biochar treatment. The top 50 OTUs at the phylum level consisted of Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi, Acidobacteria,  Bacteroidetes,  Cyanobacteria,  Actinobacteria,  Firmicutes, and Gemmatimonadetes (Fig. 4). In the mature stage of rice, the top  four  phyla  of  bacterial  communities  were  consistent  among  all treatment groups, but their relative abundances varied among the four treatment groups. The relative abundances of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Chloroflexi in the 1.2% NSBC group were 41%, 30%, and 14%, respectively. In the 1.2% LSBC group, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria  was  the  maximum  (48%),  followed  by  Acidobacteria (21%) and Chloroflexi (16%).

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between the relative abundances of the 50 most abundant OTUs and eight main environmental predictors (Fig. 4). The results showed that the  C:N ratio  was only  significantly negatively  correlated  with  Gemmatimonadetes, and TS had a significant positive correlation with Proteobacteria.  The  pH  level  and  TN  were  significantly  correlated  with several bacterial taxa, such as Holophagae, Bacteroidia, Anaerolineae and Gammaproteobacteria (Fig. 4). T-Hg exerted a significant correlation with the most of selected OTUs, which indicated that T-Hg was the primary environmental predictive variation for the bacterial community among  these  environmental  predictive  variables.  In  addition,  strong complexed/elemental Hg (F4) was recognised as being able to predict changes in the bacterial community compositions (Fig. 4).

## 3.4. Dominant bacterial taxa significantly influenced by T-Hg content

Given that T-Hg was the best predictor of the compositions of the bacterial  community  revealed  by  stepwise  multiple  regression,  the following bacterial taxa,  in  which  Hellinger-transformed relative  frequencies were significantly positively correlated with T-Hg content in the rhizosphere soil, were further analysed and identified in accordance with the correlation analysis results (Fig. 4). The bacterial communities included Arthrobacter (OTU240),  two Sphingomonas (OTU2708  and OTU4998), and Bacillus (OTU389) (Fig. 5a -d). Moreover, the relationships between the four OTUs and the T-Hg content in the rhizosphere soil were determined by combining the linear fitting method. The T-Hg content in the rhizosphere soil showed a significant negative correlation with  the  relative  abundances  of  four  dominant  OTUs  (OTU2934, OTU4953, OTU5936, and OTU3453), which were assigned to Gemmatimonadaceae,  Holophagae,  Holophagae,  and Geobacter ,  respectively (Fig. 5e -h).

## 4. Discussion

## 4.1. Natural S-rich biochar altered the bacterial abundance in rhizosphere paddy soil

The effect of NSBC application on the response of bacterial abundance in the rhizosphere paddy soil to Hg pollution was analysed. The SEM results showed that NSBC application directly contributed to the change in the bacterial diversity in the rhizosphere soil (Fig. 3). Similar to common biochar, NSBC could stimulate microbial growth in the soil, which might be realised mainly by changing the environmental conditions of soil. In general, biochar is rich in nutritive elements, such as P, K, Ca, Mg, B, Mo, and Zn, and dissolved organic C. Thus, it can provide the

substrate for microbial growth and metabolism (Dangi et al., 2020). In addition,  biochar  is  well  developed,  with  porosity  and  large  surface areas. Therefore, it could provide a suitable habitat for microbial growth in  soil  and  protect  these  microbes  from  being  predated  by  other microfauna (Quilliam et al., 2013).

The toxicity of Hg is largely dependent on its chemical speciation, which is closely correlated with soil physicochemical properties (Natasha et al., 2020). Different Hg species have diverse effects on soil microbial  diversity  and  activity  because  they  differ  in  their  potential bioaccessibility  and  bioavailability  (Frey  and  Rieder,  2013;  Campos et al., 2018). However, stepwise regression analysis and SEM suggest that  the  reduction  in  T-Hg  content  under  the  application  of  NSBC contributed to the alteration in the abundance of the microbial community (Table 1 and Fig. 3). This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that NSBC strengthened the reduction process of Hg(II) to Hg(0) mediated by DOM, and the T-Hg content was then reduced along with the volatilisation of Hg(0) into air (Gu et al., 2011). In addition, the decrease in T-Hg content in the rhizosphere soil as induced by NSBC was mainly  attributed  to  the  S-rich  characteristics  of  NSBC,  which  could strongly adsorb ionic Hg and then promote the microbial reduction of ionic Hg to volatile Hg because of its excellent electron transfer performance  (Yuan  et  al.,  2017;  Saquing  et  al.,  2016).  Our  results  also indicated that changes in the Hg chemical speciation had an effect on the microbial abundance in the rhizosphere soil, considering that F3, F4, and  F5  were  found  to  be  significantly  correlated  with  the  relative abundance of specific bacterial taxa in the rhizosphere soil (Fig. 4). For instance,  F3  was  significantly  positively  correlated  with  Deltaproteobacteria ( P &lt; 0.05), while F4 was significantly positively corelated with Actinobacteria,  Firmicutes,  and  Alphaproteobacteria  but  significantly negatively correlated with Holophagae, Gemmatimonadetes, and Gammaproteobacteria. The increase in the F5 component could significantly enhance the relative abundances of Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Alphaproteobacteria. Previous studies have also shown that Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria are all Hg-resistant bacterial phyla, the abundance of which would increase under the stress of soil Hg pollution (Liu et al., 2014; Frossard et al., 2017). These findings are consistent with the results in this study; that is, different Hg species increased the relative abundance of these bacterial phyla in the rhizosphere  soil.  Moreover,  microbial  enzymes  and  metabolism  have  specializes in response to different Hg species, which may cause different Hg components to have different effects on the microbial communities with various functions. Nevertheless, changes in the content of these Hg species are highly associated with the Hg source, i.e., T-Hg. Therefore, T-Hg dominantly drove the shifts in the abundance and composition of the bacterial community through the effect of NSBC application on the distribution of Hg species in the rhizosphere soil.

The changes in the environmental conditions in the rhizosphere soil under the application of NSBC may also influence the bacterial abundance, among which the degree of soil acidity or alkalinity (expressed as pH) was expected to be a master variable affecting the soil biological properties, including the assemblage of the microbial community (Jones et al., 2019). Stepwise regression analysis showed that compared with T-Hg, pH could indeed predict significant (Table 1) but smaller variations in bacterial abundance in the rhizosphere soil, although the pH effect was not identified by SEM (Fig. 3), which may have been ascribed to the minor change in the soil acidity under NSBC treatment. Soil C:N ratio is an important index reflecting the nutritional status of the soil. At an extremely high C:N ratio, the microbial decomposition of soil organic matters can be restricted due to the lack of N and can be boosted by properly lowering the C:N ratio (Brady and Weil, 2002). Therefore, the soil C:N ratio is an important environmental variable shaping the microbial community. Biochar can enhance the N fixation capacity of soil due to the N-containing substances it carries and its retaining effect on these substances (Hossain et al., 2011; Taghizadeh-Toosi et al., 2012). Meanwhile,  biochar  amendment  could  increase  the  SOC  content  by limiting the mineralisation of SOM and facilitating the humification of

SOM (Kasozi et al., 2010; Keith et al., 2011). However, in the present study, the C:N ratio was found to have little influence on the overall bacterial  abundance  in  the  rhizosphere  soil.  Meanwhile,  Hg  in  the rhizosphere soil could be further fixed by increasing the S content in the biochar (Fig. 1a), and the fixed Hg existed mainly in the form of nonbioavailable F4 (Fig. 1c), resulting in the weakening of the toxicity of Hg in the microbiome and an increase in bacterial abundance. However, the study also showed that elevated TS content in the soil caused by NSBC application had a directly negative effect on the number of OTUs in the rhizosphere soil (Fig. 3). Thus, the beneficial effect of elevated TS content on microbial abundance far outweighed the repressive effect of TS on the microbial abundance under the NSBC amendment. The S carried by NSBC had two-sided influences on the bacterial abundance in the rhizosphere soil, but its positive influence was remarkably greater than its negative influence. This result indicates that the application of NSCB is worth advocating for in order to optimise the microbial diversity and community in Hg-polluted paddy soil.

## 4.2. Natural S-rich biochar altered the compositions and ecological clusters of the bacterial community in rhizosphere soil

This work on the effect of NSBC application on the composition of the microbial  community  in  paddy  soil  supports  the  hypothesis  that  the microbial community  composition  and  structure  in  the  soil are extremely sensitive to environmental changes (Wang et al., 2020b). The structure of the microbial community can be affected even by minor changes in the physicochemical properties and toxic substances of soil (Singh  et  al.,  2011).  Therefore,  modifying  the  soil  physicochemical properties  through  the  ecological  niche  theory  and  decreasing  the bioavailability of toxic substances through the remediation of pollution caused by toxic  substances  are  consistently  critical  subjects  of  pedological  and  ecological  research.  The  results  demonstrated  that  the growth of some specific microorganisms was significantly stimulated by the reduction of T-Hg content in the rhizosphere paddy soil under the application of NSBC, among which Holophagae, Gemmatimonadaceae, and Geobacter were  the  taxa  that  primarily  responded  to  the  T-Hg reduction. However, the application of NSBC did not significantly increase or even reduce the abundance of many bacterial taxa and exerted significant inhibitory effects on Arthrobacter , Sphingomonas , and Bacillus . Existing studies have shown that the three inhibited microorganisms have  very strong environmental  suitability and  stress resistance (Vaishampayan et al., 2007; Zhao et al., 2017; Deng et al., 2021). In particular, Sphingomonas species  are  Hg-resistant  bacteria  containing mer A and mer B genes, in which the former reduces Hg(II) into Hg(0), while the latter facilitates the degradation of organic Hg (Mahbub et al., 2016). Therefore, Arthrobacter , Sphingomonas , and Bacillus became the dominant bacteria under Hg stress. However, these Hg-resistant bacteria may have been  outcompeted  by  other  bacteria  within  the  microbial community when NSBC application reduced the Hg stress, leading to the weakening of their ecological potentials. The two-sided effects of NSBC application on the soil microbiome may be attributed to the differential impacts of NSBC on microbes with varied Hg-resistance abilities and the S-rich characteristics of NSBC.

Holophage has been found to show a greater preference for rhizosphere soil than non-rhizosphere soil (da Rocha et al., 2010). Although the Acidobacteria that Holophage belongs to is a common bacterium in non-rhizosphere  oligotrophic  environments,  this  species  booms  in eutrophic environments (da Rocha et al., 2010). Studies have shown that Gemmatimonadaceae, an aerobe widely distributed in farmland soil, can reduce nitrite into nitric oxide with soil N fixation (Aanderud et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2021). Geobacter , which is considered to be the main Fe-reducing bacteria in the soil environment, can use organic matter, organic pollutants, acetate, and H2  as electron donors. In addition to coupling soil C, N, and S cycling, this species can change the toxicity of heavy metals by reducing them into less toxic chemical forms (Williams et al., 2011; Lovley et al., 2011). Therefore, the bacteria exhibiting a

positive  response to the application of NSBC play important roles in maintaining multiple ecosystem functions of nutrient cycling (Aanderud et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2021), ameliorating the rhizosphere microenvironment, and stimulating plant growth. Therefore, the results  of the present study show that applying NSBC to Hg-polluted paddy soil may, to some extent, help in relieving soil pollution, reducing the input of chemical fertilisers, promoting plant growth, and finally realising sustainable agricultural development.

T-Hg was also a significant predictor for the formation of ecological clusters within the co-occurrence network of rhizosphere soil bacteria (Fig. 6). NSBC application reduced the T-Hg content in the rhizosphere, leading  to  shifts  in  the  relative  abundance  of  the  different  modules within the co-occurrence network of the bacterial community (Fig. 1a and  Fig.  6b).  The  relative  abundances  of  some  modules  changed significantly with the variation in T-Hg content in the rhizosphere soil (Fig. 6c). For example, the relative abundance of module #2 was positively correlated with the T-Hg level. This module was mainly composed of Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria, which were confirmed to harbour Hg resistance genes (Barkay et al., 2003).

## 4.3. Environmental significance and perspectives

The Hg pollution of paddy soil triggered by human activities and natural  source  emission  in  recent  years  has  seriously  restricted  sustainable agricultural development and threatened global food safety and security (Liu et al., 2019). NSBC has a high pH, large surface areas, rich porosities, and abundant nutrient substances, just like common biochar. Most importantly, it is also rich in S with strong Hg-binding ability (Li et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2021). Therefore, whether NSBC could become a new organic amendment for improving soil conditions and ensuring soil and human health is worth exploring. Microorganisms are structural and functional  drivers  that  are  extensively  found  in  soil  ecosystems.  The microbes  in  different  ecological  clusters  of  microbial  communities jointly  form  a  unified  soil  ecosystem  through  their  interaction  and interdependence, and their stability is closely related to the stability of the  soil  quality  (Delgado-Baquerizo  et  al.,  2017;  dos  Santos  Teixeira et al., 2021). In this study, the interaction mechanisms of NSBC with rhizosphere soil, Hg pollution, and microorganisms were preliminarily explored.  Previous  studies  on  the  influences  of  biochar  input  on improving  soil  quality  and  food  security  have  mainly  focused  on single-macroscopic phenomena, while studies at the biochar -rhizosphere soil -Hg pollution -microorganism four-in-one system level have not been carried out, and their interrelationship needs to be further clarified. In the present study, the two-sided influences of S in the NSBC on the microbial taxonomic attributes were preliminarily evaluated at the four-in-one system level. These findings provide new insights for managing the soil quality related to soil microorganisms.

Given that the field environment is complex and quite different from the laboratory environment. Moreover, compared with laboratory conditions, Hg pollution is characterised by persistent pollution, extensive pollution sources, and diversified chemical speciation in the field, and its  bioavailability  deviates  considerably  from  that  in  the  laboratory environment (Yin et al., 2016). Therefore, the laboratory experimental results  obtained  in  this  study  are  potentially  different  from  the  field experimental results, and whether the findings obtained in the laboratory can be directly applied to field conditions is uncertain. Most noteworthy  is  that  the  soil  used  in  this  study  was  collected  from  an Hg-contaminated field to simulate the real Hg pollution in a natural environment. In future research, the laboratory and field experimental results  should be combined to further evaluate the effects of soil Hg pollution  on  the  bacterial  abundance,  composition,  and  ecological clusters under the application of NSBC in paddy fields. In addition, given that  the  application  rates  of  biochar  used  in  the  present  study  were considerably lower than the traditional rate, the responses of bacterial taxonomic attributes to mercury species in rhizosphere paddy soil need to be further investigated under a higher NSBC application rate.

## 5. Conclusions

In this study, the responses of the abundance and ecological clusters of the soil bacterial communities in rhizosphere paddy soil within the cooccurrence network of the bacterial community to T-Hg content and its species  changes  were  evaluated  under  NSBC  amendment.  Similar  to common biochar, NSBC had positive effects on the bacterial community in the rhizosphere soil through improving the bacterial abundance and optimising the community structure. Although variation in the Hg species could alter the abundance and structure of the bacterial communities, the reduction in T-Hg level due to elevated S content in NSBC was the  best  predictor  driving  the  dynamics  of  the  bacterial  community. Changes in some microenvironmental factors, such as pH and TS content,  could  also  significantly  explain  the  differences  in  the  bacterial taxonomic  attributes.  However,  variation  in  the  bacterial  taxonomic attributes  explained  by  the  pH  level  and  TS  was  smaller  than  that explained by T-Hg content. Moreover, a decrease in the T-Hg content induced by NSBC treatment obviously shifted the relative abundance of ecological clusters within the bacterial network. These results imply that NSBC amendment may be a cost-efficient and sustainable method to positively  regulate  microbiome-associated  soil  quality  in  Hg-polluted paddy soil.
